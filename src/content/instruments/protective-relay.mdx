A protective relay does three things: **measure** something, **compare** it against a
setting, and **decide** whether to trip.

The measurement and the comparison are straightforward. Everything interesting is in the
deciding — specifically, in how long the relay waits before it acts.

## Why waiting is the whole subject

A fault on a distribution feeder is seen by every relay upstream of it, all the way back to
the source. If they all trip, one fault on a spur takes out the entire substation.

So the relay **nearest** the fault must operate first, and every relay behind it must wait
long enough to give the nearest one a chance. That is **coordination**, and it is what
protection engineering is mostly about.

<Analogy>
  Think of it as a queue where politeness is enforced by delay.

  Every relay that can see the fault agrees something is wrong. Rather than all acting at
  once, each waits an amount that depends on how far it is from the problem — nearest waits
  least.

  If the nearest one clears it, everyone else sees the fault disappear and stands down. If
  it fails, the next one along has already been counting and takes over. Backup is built
  into the timing, not into a separate system.
</Analogy>

## Inverse-time curves

The waiting is not a fixed delay. It depends on **how large** the fault current is: a
severe fault is cleared quickly, a marginal overload is tolerated longer.

The IEC characteristic:

$$
t = \text{TMS} \times \frac{k}{\left(\dfrac{I}{I_s}\right)^{\alpha} - 1}
$$

where $I_s$ is the current setting and TMS the time multiplier.

<Callout type="engineering">
  The curve choice is not arbitrary — it is matched to what you are protecting.

  **Standard inverse** is the general-purpose feeder curve, and its shallow slope
  ($\alpha = 0.02$) means the operating time changes only gradually with current, which
  makes coordination between successive relays straightforward.

  **Extremely inverse** ($\alpha = 2$) has $t \propto 1/I^2$, which is the same shape as the
  thermal $I^2t$ withstand of a cable or a transformer. So the relay's curve tracks the
  damage curve of the equipment it protects — closely enough that it can be allowed nearer
  to it. It is also the right choice for coordinating with downstream **fuses**, which have
  a steeply inverse characteristic of their own.

  **Very inverse** sits between them, and is used where fault current varies substantially
  with fault position along a feeder.

  Choosing the curve is choosing what the relay is trying to imitate.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Notice the −1 in the denominator">
  At $I = I_s$ exactly, the denominator is zero and the operating time is **infinite**.

  This is correct, and it means the current setting is a threshold the relay approaches
  asymptotically rather than a trip point. A relay set at 100% of rated current does not
  operate at 100% — it takes a meaningful margin above the setting before the operating time
  becomes finite and useful.

  So a setting of 125% of full load current is normal, and setting a relay "at" the load
  current would leave it effectively inoperative for small overloads.
</Callout>

A **numerical** relay samples the CT and VT secondaries, computes phasors digitally — usually
by a discrete Fourier transform over one cycle — and implements many protection functions in
software on one platform. It self-monitors, records disturbances, timestamps events, and
communicates over IEC 61850.

<Callout type="engineering">
  The induction disc relay is worth understanding even though it is being replaced, because
  its physics is why the curves have the shape they do.

  Current through the coil produces a flux; eddy currents in an aluminium disc produce a
  torque proportional to roughly $I^2$; the disc rotates against a control spring and a
  permanent-magnet damper until a contact closes.

  Higher current means more torque, means faster rotation, means shorter operating time —
  the inverse characteristic arises from the mechanics, not from a designer's choice.

  The **time multiplier setting** simply changes how far the disc must travel before the
  contacts meet. That is why TMS is a clean multiplier on the whole curve: it scales the
  distance, not the speed.

  Numerical relays reproduce these curves in software for backward compatibility — the
  equations in IEC 60255 are, in effect, a mathematical description of a spinning aluminium
  disc.
</Callout>

## Coordination in practice

<Steps title="Setting a pair of relays">
  <Step n={1} title="Establish the maximum load current" expect="a number from the actual load, not the cable rating">
    The setting must be above maximum load — including any short-term overload you intend
    to ride through — so the relay does not operate on normal conditions.
  </Step>

  <Step n={2} title="Establish the minimum fault current" expect="the smallest fault the relay must still detect">
    Usually a high-resistance earth fault at the far end of the protected zone. The setting
    must be **below** this, or the relay will not see the fault at all.
  </Step>

  <Step n={3} title="Choose the current setting between the two" hazard="If minimum fault current is below maximum load current, overcurrent protection cannot work here — you need a different principle.">
    Typically 125–150% of maximum load. The gap between load and fault current is what makes
    overcurrent protection possible; on a weak system that gap can vanish.
  </Step>

  <Step n={4} title="Set the downstream relay's TMS to the minimum that is selective" expect="the fastest clearance the coordination allows">
    Nearest the fault should be fastest.
  </Step>

  <Step n={5} title="Add the grading margin for the upstream relay" expect="typically 0.3–0.4 s between successive relays">
    The margin covers the downstream **breaker operating time** (about 60–100 ms), the
    downstream relay's **overshoot** after the current stops, CT and relay **errors**, and a
    **safety factor**.

    Modern numerical relays allow 0.25–0.3 s; electromechanical ones need 0.4 s or more,
    largely because of overshoot.
  </Step>

  <Step n={6} title="Verify at both extremes" expect="selectivity at maximum AND minimum fault current">
    Coordination that works at maximum fault current can fail at minimum, because the two
    curves converge. Check both ends.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="warning" title="Grading accumulates, and that is the real constraint">
  With four relays in series at 0.4 s each, the relay nearest the source waits 1.2 s beyond
  the fastest — while carrying full fault current the whole time.

  That is a long time for a transformer or a busbar to sit in a fault, and it is why
  overcurrent grading alone becomes impractical on systems with many levels.

  The answer is protection that is **inherently selective** rather than time-graded:
  differential protection (ANSI 87) compares current in and out of a zone and needs no delay
  at all, and distance protection (ANSI 21) determines fault position directly from
  impedance. Both trip fast regardless of how many levels exist upstream.
</Callout>

## The relay test set

You cannot wait for a real fault. A **relay test set** injects controlled current and
voltage into the relay's inputs and measures what it does.

<Callout type="tip" title="Plot measured points against the theoretical curve">
  For Experiment 2, inject at 2×, 3×, 5× and 10× the setting and record the operating time
  at each. Plot those points on log-log axes with the IEC equation as a line through them.

  Log-log matters: an inverse curve is close to a straight line on those axes, so a
  systematic deviation is immediately visible in a way it is not on linear axes.

  Remember to state whether your recorded time includes the breaker operating time or only
  the relay's own decision time. They differ by 60–100 ms, and confusing them will make an
  otherwise correct relay look slow.
</Callout>

<Hazard title="Two hazards on a protection bench">
  **CT secondaries.** A relay is fed from CTs. Short the secondary before disconnecting the
  relay from it — the open-circuit CT hazard from the instrument transformer page applies in
  full, and a protection lab is where it actually happens.

  **Trip outputs.** The relay's output contact may be wired to a real breaker trip coil.
  Testing a relay that is still connected to live switchgear will trip it. Confirm what the
  trip contact is wired to before you inject anything.
</Hazard>

## In your experiments

**Experiment 2** — overcurrent protection of an induction motor. The complication is that a
motor's starting current is five to seven times its full-load current for several seconds,
and the relay must **not** trip on that while still protecting against a genuine overload.
That is the whole setting problem, and it is why the curve choice and the TMS matter rather
than just the pickup.

**Experiment 4** — under/over frequency (ANSI 81) and under/over voltage (27/59) relays.
Different measurands, same three-part structure: measure, compare, delay. Underfrequency
load shedding is the last automatic defence a power system has against total collapse, and
its settings are graded by frequency rather than by time.

## Viva preparation

<Viva band="definition">
  <Q q="What is an IDMT relay?">
    Inverse Definite Minimum Time. Its operating time falls as fault current rises — following
    t = TMS × k / ((I/Is)^α − 1) — approaching a definite minimum at very high currents.

    The inverse shape allows relays in series to be graded: the one nearest a fault sees the
    largest current, operates fastest, and clears before the ones behind it.
  </Q>

  <Q q="What do the current setting and time multiplier do?">
    The current setting Is is the threshold the relay compares against — it must be above
    maximum load and below minimum fault current. The time multiplier TMS scales the whole
    operating-time curve without changing its shape.

    On an induction disc relay TMS physically changes how far the disc must travel before the
    contacts meet, which is why it is a clean multiplier.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why does the IDMT equation have a −1 in the denominator?">
    So that at I = Is exactly, the operating time is infinite. The setting is an asymptotic
    threshold rather than a trip point, and a meaningful margin above it is needed before the
    operating time becomes usefully finite.

    This is why relays are set at around 125% of full load rather than at 100%.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="analysis">
  <Q q="Why is extremely inverse used to protect a transformer or to grade with fuses?">
    Its α = 2 gives t ∝ 1/I², the same shape as the thermal I²t withstand of cables and
    transformers — so the relay curve tracks the damage curve of the equipment and can be set
    closer to it.

    Fuses have a steeply inverse characteristic of their own, so an extremely inverse relay
    grades with them where a standard inverse curve would cross.
  </Q>

  <Q q="What determines the grading margin between two relays?">
    Four contributions: the downstream breaker operating time, roughly 60–100 ms; the
    downstream relay's overshoot after current ceases; CT and relay errors; and a safety
    factor.

    Numerical relays allow 0.25–0.3 s; electromechanical ones need 0.4 s or more, mostly
    because of overshoot in the mechanism.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why does overcurrent grading become impractical with many levels?">
    Because the margins accumulate. Four relays in series at 0.4 s each leave the one nearest
    the source waiting 1.2 s while carrying full fault current — too long for a transformer
    or busbar.

    The answer is inherently selective protection: differential (87), which compares current
    in and out of a zone and needs no time delay, or distance (21), which determines fault
    position from impedance. Both operate fast regardless of how many levels lie upstream.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="application">
  <Q q="Why is setting overcurrent protection for a motor harder than for a feeder?">
    Because starting current is five to seven times full-load current for several seconds,
    and it is a normal condition. The relay must ride through it and still protect against a
    genuine sustained overload only slightly above rated current.

    That gap is narrow, which is why curve selection and TMS matter more here than pickup
    alone — and why dedicated motor protection relays model the thermal state of the winding
    rather than relying on current magnitude alone.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<KeyIdea>
  A relay measures, compares and **waits** — and the waiting is the subject. Inverse-time
  curves exist so the relay nearest a fault operates first, with a grading margin covering
  breaker time, overshoot and errors. Those margins accumulate, which is why differential and
  distance protection exist: they are selective by principle rather than by delay.
</KeyIdea>
