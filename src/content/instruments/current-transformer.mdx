You cannot put an 800 A busbar through a meter. A current transformer scales it to a
standard 1 A or 5 A that instruments are built for, and provides isolation from the primary
voltage at the same time.

Everything else about a CT follows from one fact: **it is a current source**.

## Why that matters

An ordinary transformer is driven by a voltage, and the flux in its core is set by that
applied voltage. A CT is different — the primary current is fixed by the power system,
which does not care what the CT is doing.

Ampere-turns must balance:

$$
N_p I_p = N_s I_s + N_p I_{\text{mag}}
$$

With a burden connected, secondary current flows, its ampere-turns oppose the primary, and
the net magnetomotive force driving the core is small. **The core flux stays low**, which is
exactly the condition for accurate transformation.

Now open the secondary. $I_s = 0$, so nothing opposes the primary ampere-turns at all. The
entire $N_p I_p$ becomes magnetising MMF.

<Hazard title="An open CT secondary can develop kilovolts">
  The core is driven hard into saturation. Around each zero crossing the flux changes
  extremely rapidly, and

  $$e = N_s \frac{\mathrm{d}\phi}{\mathrm{d}t}$$

  produces a large voltage spike across the open secondary — potentially **several
  kilovolts** on a device that looks small and harmless.

  The consequences: insulation failure, a destroyed CT, and a genuine electrocution risk to
  whoever is holding the terminals.

  The core also heats severely, because it is now supporting full flux swing with no
  opposing MMF.

  **Short the secondary before disconnecting anything from it.** This is why CT terminal
  blocks have shorting links, and why the links are made before any meter or relay is
  removed. It is the most common serious accident in a protection laboratory.
</Hazard>

<Analogy>
  A CT is a current source, and a current source hates an open circuit exactly as much as a
  voltage source hates a short.

  Short a voltage source and it delivers enormous current. Open a current source and it
  develops enormous voltage. Both are the source trying to obey its nature against an
  impossible load.

  This is also why a **voltage** transformer has the opposite rule: never short its
  secondary. The two instrument transformers have mirror-image failure modes, and confusing
  them is easy.
</Analogy>

## Burden

The burden is the total impedance the CT drives — the relay, the meter, and the resistance
of the leads out to them.

<Callout type="engineering">
  **Lead resistance is often the largest part of the burden**, and it is the part people
  forget.

  A CT in a switchyard might be fifty metres from its relay panel. With 2.5 mm² cable at
  about 7 mΩ/m, and counting both go and return, that is:

  $$R_{\text{leads}} = 2 \times 50 \times 0.007 = 0.7\ \Omega$$

  On a **5 A** secondary the burden is $I^2 R = 25 \times 0.7 = 17.5$ VA — which may exceed
  the CT's entire rating on its own, before the relay is even connected.

  On a **1 A** secondary the same leads cost $1 \times 0.7 = 0.7$ VA. Twenty-five times
  less, because burden goes as the square of the current.

  This is precisely why 1 A secondaries are standard where cable runs are long, and 5 A
  where the relay is close to the CT. It is not arbitrary convention.
</Callout>

Exceed the rated burden and the CT can no longer drive the required secondary current
accurately. Its errors grow beyond class, and under fault conditions it saturates early —
which means the protection sees less current than is actually flowing, and may not operate.

## Metering and protection CTs are designed to be opposite

This is the most useful thing to understand about CTs, and it surprises people.

<Callout type="engineering">
  The two are designed to **fail in opposite directions**, and each is right for its job.

  A metering CT is optimised for accuracy at normal load, because that is what you bill on.
  When a fault multiplies the current twenty-fold, the metering CT saturates — and this is
  a **feature**, because it limits what reaches the energy meter and stops the fault
  destroying it.

  A protection CT must do the reverse. If it saturated during a fault, the relay would see a
  reduced current and might under-reach or fail to trip — precisely when it matters most. So
  it has a larger core and a much higher knee point, and it stays linear well past rated
  current at the cost of poorer accuracy at normal load.

  **You cannot substitute one for the other.** Using a metering CT for protection means the
  relay may not see the fault; using a protection CT for metering means your billing is
  inaccurate every day.
</Callout>

## The knee point

The knee point voltage is where the magnetising characteristic bends — formally, where a
**10% increase in applied voltage causes a 50% increase in magnetising current**.

Above the knee, extra applied voltage goes into magnetising the core rather than driving
the burden. That is saturation, and beyond it the secondary current no longer represents
the primary.

The knee point is what a CT is tested against during commissioning, and it is what
determines whether a given CT can support a given relay through a given fault.

## Polarity

CTs are marked **P1/P2** on the primary and **S1/S2** on the secondary. Current entering P1
produces current leaving S1.

<Callout type="warning" title="Reversed polarity gives negative power on that phase">
  On a power analyser, a CT connected backwards makes that phase's power read negative — and
  the total is then wrong by twice that phase's contribution.

  It is easy to spot if you check: a passive load cannot have negative power. It is entirely
  invisible if you do not.

  For differential protection the consequence is worse. Reversed polarity on one side makes
  a healthy circuit look like a permanent internal fault, and the relay trips on load. CT
  polarity is verified during commissioning with a battery and a centre-zero voltmeter, on
  every CT, for this reason.
</Callout>

## Viva preparation

<Viva band="definition">
  <Q q="Why must a CT secondary never be open-circuited?">
    A CT is a current source: the primary current is fixed by the system. With a burden
    connected, secondary ampere-turns oppose the primary and the net MMF driving the core is
    small, so flux stays low.

    Open the secondary and nothing opposes the primary MMF. The core saturates hard, and the
    very rapid flux change at each zero crossing induces a large voltage — potentially
    kilovolts — across the open terminals. The core also overheats.

    Short the secondary before disconnecting anything.
  </Q>

  <Q q="What is CT burden and why does it matter?">
    The total impedance the secondary drives — relay, meter and lead resistance — expressed
    in VA at rated secondary current.

    Exceeding it means the CT cannot drive the required current accurately, so errors grow
    beyond class and the CT saturates earlier under fault, which can prevent protection
    operating.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why are 1 A secondaries used for long cable runs and 5 A for short ones?">
    Burden goes as I²R, so the same lead resistance costs twenty-five times more VA at 5 A
    than at 1 A.

    Fifty metres of 2.5 mm² cable is about 0.7 Ω both ways: 17.5 VA at 5 A, which can exceed
    the CT rating on its own, versus 0.7 VA at 1 A.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="analysis">
  <Q q="Can a metering CT be used for protection?">
    No. A metering CT is designed to saturate under fault current, deliberately, to protect
    the connected meter. A relay fed from it would see a reduced current during a fault and
    might under-reach or fail to trip.

    A protection CT has a larger core and higher knee point so it stays linear to ten or
    twenty times rated current, at the cost of poorer accuracy at normal load. The two are
    designed to fail in opposite directions, and neither substitutes for the other.
  </Q>

  <Q q="What is the knee point voltage?">
    The point on the magnetising characteristic where a 10% increase in applied voltage
    produces a 50% increase in magnetising current.

    Above it, additional voltage goes into magnetising the core rather than driving the
    burden — the secondary current stops representing the primary. It is what determines
    whether a CT can support a given relay through a given fault.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<KeyIdea>
  A CT is a **current source**, so an open secondary is the mirror image of a shorted
  voltage source — the core saturates and kilovolts appear. Short it before disconnecting
  anything. And metering and protection CTs are designed to behave oppositely under fault:
  one saturates on purpose, the other must not.
</KeyIdea>
