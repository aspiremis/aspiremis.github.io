The load bank is what makes the transmission line experiment an experiment. Without it you
have a line with nothing at the end and nothing to measure.

## Three loads, three phase angles

/>

Combine them and the resultant current sits at some angle $\varphi$ between, which is
exactly the variable Experiment 1 sweeps.

<Callout type="engineering">
  The reason this matters is the drop formula from Experiment 1:

  $$\Delta V \approx I(R\cos\varphi \pm X\sin\varphi)$$

  With **resistance only**, $\sin\varphi = 0$ and only the small $IR$ term survives.

  With **inductance**, both terms add, and since $X > R$ the reactive one dominates — the
  largest drop of the three.

  With **capacitance**, the reactive term subtracts, and once it exceeds the resistive one
  the drop goes negative and the voltage rises.

  So the load bank is not three loads; it is a way of setting the **sign and size of the
  reactive term**. That is the physical variable the experiment is actually sweeping.
</Callout>

## Resistive elements

Usually wire-wound or ribbon resistors, sometimes literally heating elements. All the power
they draw becomes heat, which has three practical consequences.

<Hazard title="Resistive elements stay hot long after switch-off" level="caution">
  A 1 kW element at working temperature does not cool in the time it takes you to unplug a
  lead. Burns from load banks are far more common than shocks from them.

  Look before you reach behind the cabinet, and give it time.
</Hazard>

<Callout type="warning" title="Resistance rises with temperature">
  Metallic resistance increases with temperature — roughly 0.4% per °C for many alloys. A
  bank that has been running at full load for twenty minutes presents measurably more
  resistance than one just switched on.

  So the current at a given "step" drifts downward as the session proceeds. This shows up in
  Experiment 1 as an apparent nonlinearity that has nothing to do with the line.

  The defence: record the **measured current**, not the nominal step number, and use the
  measured value in every calculation. This is the same discipline as recording measured
  $V_S$ at every step.
</Callout>

## Inductive elements

Iron-cored reactors. Two things to know:

**They are not pure inductances.** The winding has resistance, so an "inductive" step
actually draws a lagging current at perhaps 0.9 power factor rather than at 90°. Check the
analyser rather than assuming.

**They can saturate.** Drive an iron-cored reactor beyond its rating and the core saturates,
the inductance falls, and the current rises faster than linearly — while becoming
noticeably distorted. If your inductive steps behave oddly at the top of the range, this is
usually why.

## Capacitive elements

<Hazard title="Capacitors hold charge after the supply is removed">
  Discharge resistors are fitted, and they take time — typically tens of seconds to a
  minute. They also **fail open without any external sign**.

  The procedure from the safety module applies exactly: isolate, wait, **measure** across the
  terminals with a meter you have proved, then touch.

  An extinguished indicator lamp proves nothing. A lamp needs current to glow, and it can be
  dark while the capacitor is still charged through a failed connection.
</Hazard>

<Callout type="engineering">
  **A capacitor's kvar depends on the square of the voltage**, which surprises people
  constantly:

  $$Q = \frac{V^2}{X_C} = V^2 \omega C$$

  So a bank rated 10 kvar at 415 V delivers only about 8.1 kvar at 375 V — a 10% voltage
  drop costs 19% of the reactive support.

  This is a genuinely unfortunate characteristic. Shunt capacitors are installed to hold
  voltage **up**, and their output falls exactly when the voltage sags and you need them
  most. It is a positive feedback in the wrong direction, and it is one reason SVCs and
  STATCOMs exist — a STATCOM's output falls only linearly with voltage, not as its square.

  Worth carrying into Experiment 3(b) on shunt compensation, where it explains why
  capacitors alone are not a complete answer to a weak system.
</Callout>

## Using it well

<Steps title="Every sweep">
  <Step n={1} title="Start at minimum and confirm it" expect="near-zero current before you energise">
    All steps off, including capacitor steps — a capacitor step left on gives a no-load
    voltage above the sending end and confuses the whole first reading.
  </Step>

  <Step n={2} title="Increase in equal steps, and record the MEASURED current" expect="the current, not the step number, as your independent variable">
    Step numbers are nominal. Measured current is data.
  </Step>

  <Step n={3} title="Match by current when comparing load types" expect="comparable points across the R, L and C sweeps">
    Comparing "step 3 resistive" with "step 3 inductive" compares nothing if they draw
    different currents. Match the current and the comparison becomes meaningful.
  </Step>

  <Step n={4} title="Check the power factor on the analyser" expect="close to 1 for resistive, well below for inductive">
    Confirms the load really is what the label says. A resistive step reading 0.85 power
    factor means something else is engaged.
  </Step>

  <Step n={5} title="Return to minimum before switching off" hazard="Then discharge and verify the capacitor steps.">
    Every time.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Viva preparation

<Viva band="definition">
  <Q q="Why does an inductive load cause a larger voltage drop than a resistive load at the same current?">
    The approximate drop is I(R cos φ + X sin φ). A resistive load has sin φ = 0, so only the
    IR term survives. An inductive load makes both terms contribute, and since X is
    substantially larger than R on a line, the reactive term dominates.

    That is why voltage support is fundamentally a reactive-power problem rather than a
    real-power one.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why does a capacitor bank's reactive output fall when the voltage sags?">
    Because Q = V²ωC — the output goes as the square of the voltage. A 10% voltage drop
    costs about 19% of the reactive support.

    This is unfortunate, because capacitors are installed to hold voltage up and their
    contribution collapses exactly when it is most needed. It is one reason SVCs and
    STATCOMs are used on weak systems, since a STATCOM's output falls only linearly with
    voltage.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="procedure">
  <Q q="Why record measured current rather than the load step number?">
    Because a step is nominal and the actual current drifts. Resistive elements heat as the
    session proceeds and their resistance rises about 0.4% per °C, so the same step draws
    progressively less current.

    Using step numbers introduces an apparent nonlinearity that belongs to the load bank, not
    the line under test.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Your 'resistive' step shows a power factor of 0.85. What do you conclude?">
    That the load is not purely resistive. Either an inductive step is also engaged, or the
    element has significant inductance, or the analyser's CT polarity or wiring mode is
    wrong.

    Check the analyser setup first, since a wiring error affects every reading, then check
    which steps are actually engaged.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<KeyIdea>
  The load bank sets the **sign and size of the reactive term** in
  ΔV ≈ I(R cos φ ± X sin φ) — that is the variable Experiment 1 is really sweeping. Record
  measured current rather than step numbers, because resistive elements heat and drift; and
  remember a capacitor's kvar falls with the **square** of voltage, so it weakens exactly
  when it is needed.
</KeyIdea>
