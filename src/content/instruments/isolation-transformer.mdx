An isolation transformer changes nothing about the voltage. It changes what the voltage is
*referenced to*, and that turns out to matter a great deal.

## Why a single touch is normally dangerous

Your mains supply has its neutral bonded to earth at the supply transformer. So the earth
under your feet is a conductor connected back to the source.

Touch a live conductor while standing on that earth and you have completed a circuit:
live → through you → to earth → back to the transformer neutral. Current flows, and the
numbers from the safety module apply.

<Analogy>
  Think of a bird on a transmission line.

  It sits on 400 kV and is entirely unharmed, because it touches only one conductor. There
  is no path back to the source, so no current flows regardless of the voltage.

  An isolation transformer makes **you** the bird. Its secondary is not earthed anywhere, so
  touching one output conductor completes no circuit — there is nowhere for the current to
  return.
</Analogy>

## What it actually does

Two separate windings, coupled magnetically, with no electrical connection between them.
Power crosses the core; conductors do not.

The secondary is therefore **floating** — neither of its terminals has a defined
relationship to earth. Touching either one on its own draws no current.

<Hazard title="Touching BOTH output conductors is exactly as dangerous as before">
  Isolation removes the earth path. It does not remove the 240 V between the two output
  terminals.

  A hand on each output conductor completes the secondary circuit and puts the full voltage
  across you — and the current path is hand to hand, straight through the chest, which is
  the worst case.

  So the one-hand rule still applies. Isolation protects against the accidental *single*
  contact, which is the common accident. It protects against nothing else.
</Hazard>

<Callout type="engineering">
  **The oscilloscope case is the one that destroys equipment in teaching labs.**

  A mains-powered oscilloscope has its probe ground clip bonded to the mains earth through
  the instrument's chassis and power lead. Clip it to any point that is not at earth
  potential and you have connected that point to earth — a short circuit through the probe
  lead, which is not designed to carry fault current.

  The result is a vaporised probe ground lead, a damaged scope input, tripped protection,
  and occasionally an arc in someone's hand.

  Three correct solutions, in order of preference:

  1. A **differential probe** — measures between two points with no earth reference at all.
     This is the right answer.
  2. An **isolation transformer on the circuit under test**, so its "neutral" can be
     clipped safely.
  3. A **battery-powered scope**.

  And the wrong one, which people do anyway: cutting the earth pin off the oscilloscope's
  mains lead. This floats the entire instrument chassis — every knob and connector you touch
  — at whatever potential the probe ground is clipped to. Never do this.
</Callout>

## Isolation is frequency dependent

The windings are physically adjacent, so there is capacitance between them. At mains
frequency its impedance is enormous and the isolation is effectively perfect. As frequency
rises, that impedance falls:

$$
X_C = \frac{1}{2\pi f C}
$$

For $C = 500$ pF: about **6.4 MΩ** at 50 Hz — genuinely isolating. At 100 kHz it is
**3.2 kΩ**, which isolates nothing.

<Callout type="warning" title="Switching noise crosses an isolation transformer">
  This is why isolating a variable speed drive does not stop its switching transients from
  reaching the rest of the bench, and why an isolation transformer alone is not an EMC fix.

  An **electrostatically screened** transformer — a Faraday shield of earthed foil between
  the windings — intercepts most of that capacitive current and returns it to earth before
  it reaches the secondary. If your bench transformer has a screen terminal, it must be
  earthed to do anything.
</Callout>

## Isolation transformer or RCD?

They protect against different things, and a bench often has both.

<Callout type="engineering">
  The row worth dwelling on is the second-fault one.

  Once an isolated secondary develops **one** fault to earth — a damaged lead touching a
  frame, say — that conductor is now earth-referenced, and the isolation is silently gone.
  Nothing indicates this, because a single fault on an isolated system causes no current
  and no symptom.

  The system now behaves exactly like an earthed one, and the *next* accidental contact is
  a normal electrocution.

  This is precisely why IT earthing systems — the same principle at installation scale, used
  in hospitals — are required to have **insulation monitoring devices** that alarm on the
  first fault. Isolation with no monitoring degrades to no isolation without telling anyone.
</Callout>

## Viva preparation

<Viva band="definition">
  <Q q="What does an isolation transformer protect against, and what does it not?">
    It removes the earth reference from the secondary, so a single contact with one output
    conductor completes no circuit and no current flows.

    It does not protect against touching both output conductors — the full secondary voltage
    is still there, and that path is hand to hand through the chest. It also does not
    protect once a first earth fault has appeared on the secondary, because the system is
    then referenced again.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why is an autotransformer not an isolation transformer?">
    An autotransformer has one winding with a tap, so primary and secondary are electrically
    continuous — the output shares the supply neutral. Isolation requires two windings
    coupled only magnetically.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="procedure">
  <Q q="Why is it dangerous to connect an oscilloscope probe ground to a live point?">
    The probe ground is bonded to mains earth through the instrument chassis and its power
    lead. Clipping it to a non-earth point shorts that point to earth through a lead never
    designed to carry fault current.

    The correct solutions are a differential probe, an isolation transformer on the circuit
    under test, or a battery-powered scope. Removing the scope's earth pin is not a
    solution — it floats the whole chassis, including everything you touch.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why does an isolation transformer not stop switching noise?">
    Because the windings are physically adjacent and there is capacitance between them. At
    50 Hz that capacitance presents megohms and the isolation is effectively perfect, but
    its impedance falls as 1/f — at 100 kHz a 500 pF coupling is only a few kilohms.

    An electrostatic screen between the windings, earthed, intercepts most of that
    capacitive current.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<KeyIdea>
  An isolation transformer makes you **the bird on the wire** — one conductor touched, no
  return path, no current. It does nothing about touching both conductors, nothing after a
  first earth fault has quietly referenced the secondary, and nothing at high frequency
  where inter-winding capacitance bridges it.
</KeyIdea>
