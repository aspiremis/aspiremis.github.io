Shunt capacitors are the cheapest way to supply reactive power, and for that reason they
are everywhere — on distribution feeders, in substations, alongside every large motor.

They are also the component most likely to cause a problem you did not anticipate.

## What they do

A capacitor draws leading current, which is the same as **supplying** reactive power:

$$
Q_C = \frac{V^2}{X_C} = V^2\omega C
$$

Install one near an inductive load and the capacitor supplies the reactive power locally
instead of the network importing it over the line. Line current falls, losses fall (as the
square of current), and the voltage drop along the line falls too.

<Callout type="engineering">
  The economics are unusually direct.

  A load drawing 100 kW at 0.7 power factor lagging draws 143 kVA, so:

  $$Q = \sqrt{143^2 - 100^2} \approx 102\ \text{kvar}$$

  Add 102 kvar of capacitance and the reactive component is cancelled: the line now carries
  100 kVA instead of 143. Current falls by 30%, and **losses fall by more than half**, since
  $I^2R$ scales as the square.

  That is the entire argument. Capacitors cost very little per kvar, they have no moving
  parts, and they pay back in reduced losses and avoided reinforcement — which is why
  utilities apply power factor penalties and why nearly every industrial installation has a
  bank.
</Callout>

## Three ways they cause trouble

### 1. Stored energy

<Hazard title="A disconnected capacitor bank can still kill you">
  $W = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2$, and a bank of any size holds a genuinely dangerous amount at close
  to peak voltage.

  Discharge resistors are required by standard — for LV banks, residual voltage below 50 V
  within 60 seconds. But they are a passive component in a hot cabinet and they **fail open
  without any external indication**.

  The procedure is the one from the safety module, and it has no shortcut:

  1. Isolate.
  2. Wait the full stated discharge time.
  3. **Measure** across the terminals with a meter you have proved on a known source.
  4. Only then touch.

  There is a further subtlety: a capacitor that has been discharged can partially **recover
  a charge** over the following minutes, as charge trapped in the dielectric redistributes.
  This is dielectric absorption, and it is why substation practice is to apply an earthing
  device and leave it applied, rather than discharge once and rely on it.
</Hazard>

### 2. Inrush current

An uncharged capacitor is a short circuit at the instant of energisation. Closing a switch
onto one draws a current limited only by the circuit inductance and resistance — typically
tens of times rated current, at a frequency of several hundred hertz to a few kilohertz.

<Callout type="warning" title="Back-to-back switching is far worse">
  Energising one bank while a second is already energised is the severe case. The already-charged
  bank discharges into the uncharged one through only the small inductance of the busbar
  between them — a very low impedance path.

  Inrush can reach a hundred times rated current at several kilohertz. This welds contactors,
  destroys fuses and stresses the capacitors themselves.

  The standard mitigations are **inrush limiting reactors** in series with each step, or
  **point-on-wave switching** that closes the contacts at the instant the voltage difference
  across them is zero. On a teaching bench, simply switching one step at a time with a pause
  between is the practical version.
</Callout>

### 3. Harmonic resonance

This is the one that catches engineers rather than students, and it is the most important
thing on this page.

A capacitor bank adds capacitance to a system that is predominantly inductive. Together
they form a **parallel resonant circuit**, with resonance at:

$$
h_r = \sqrt{\frac{S_{\text{sc}}}{Q_C}}
$$

where $h_r$ is the resonant harmonic order, $S_{\text{sc}}$ the short-circuit level at the
bus, and $Q_C$ the bank rating.

<Callout type="engineering">
  Work an example. A bus with a 50 MVA short-circuit level and a 2 Mvar bank:

  $$h_r = \sqrt{\frac{50}{2}} = 5$$

  Resonance at the **5th harmonic** — 250 Hz. And the 5th is one of the most abundant
  harmonics produced by six-pulse rectifiers, which is to say by most industrial drives.

  At parallel resonance the impedance seen by the harmonic current source is very high, so a
  modest injected harmonic current produces a **large** harmonic voltage. That voltage drives
  large circulating currents between the capacitor and the system inductance — overheating
  the capacitors, blowing their fuses, distorting the bus voltage for everyone connected to
  it, and occasionally destroying the bank outright.

  The perverse part: the installation was working acceptably before the capacitors were
  added *to improve* power quality.

  The fix is a **detuned reactor** in series with each capacitor step — typically 6% or 7%,
  chosen to place the series resonance just below the lowest significant harmonic, usually
  around 3.8 to 4.2. Below that order the branch is capacitive and supplies reactive power
  as intended; above it the branch is inductive, so it cannot form a parallel resonance with
  the system at any harmonic frequency present.

  One inductor, and an entire failure mode is removed.
</Callout>

## Voltage rise

Capacitors raise voltage — that is usually the point. But on a lightly loaded system the
rise can push voltage above the statutory limit.

$$
\Delta V \approx \frac{Q_C X}{V}
$$

This is why capacitor banks are **switched** rather than fixed: engaged as load rises, and
disconnected at night when the load falls and their support would push the voltage too high.

Automatic power factor correction controllers do exactly this, switching steps in and out to
hold a target power factor.

<Callout type="engineering">
  The STATCOM row is the important comparison, and it follows directly from the V² problem.

  A capacitor's reactive output collapses as the square of voltage — exactly when a sagging
  system needs it most. A STATCOM is a voltage-source converter that can inject rated
  current at reduced voltage, so its reactive output falls only **linearly**.

  At 0.5 pu voltage, a capacitor delivers 25% of its rating. A STATCOM delivers 50%. On a
  system approaching voltage collapse that difference decides whether the support arrives in
  time.

  It costs a great deal more, which is why capacitors remain the default and STATCOMs appear
  where the system is weak or the response must be fast.
</Callout>

## Viva preparation

<Viva band="definition">
  <Q q="Why does a capacitor bank's kvar output fall when voltage drops?">
    Because Q = V²ωC — the output depends on the square of the applied voltage. A 10%
    voltage drop reduces the output by about 19%.

    This is a genuine weakness: capacitors are installed to support voltage, and their
    contribution collapses precisely when the voltage sags and support is most needed.
  </Q>

  <Q q="What is harmonic resonance with a capacitor bank?">
    The bank's capacitance forms a parallel resonant circuit with the system's inductance,
    at harmonic order h = √(S_sc / Q_C).

    If that order coincides with a harmonic the load produces — the 5th is the usual
    culprit, from six-pulse rectifiers — the high impedance at resonance turns a modest
    harmonic current into a large harmonic voltage, driving circulating currents that
    overheat the capacitors and distort the bus voltage for everyone.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="analysis">
  <Q q="How do you prevent harmonic resonance?">
    A detuned reactor in series with each capacitor step, typically 6% or 7%, placing the
    series resonance just below the lowest significant harmonic — around order 3.8 to 4.2.

    Below that order the branch remains capacitive and supplies reactive power normally.
    Above it the branch is inductive, so it cannot form a parallel resonance with the system
    at any harmonic present. One component removes the entire failure mode.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why are capacitor banks switched rather than permanently connected?">
    Because their voltage rise, ΔV ≈ Q_C X / V, is helpful under load and harmful without
    it. At night when load falls, a fixed bank would push the voltage above statutory limits.

    Automatic power factor correction controllers switch steps in and out to hold a target
    power factor, which handles both the reactive requirement and the voltage limit.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why is back-to-back capacitor switching worse than energising a single bank?">
    The already-energised bank discharges into the uncharged one through only the small
    busbar inductance between them — a very low impedance path — so inrush can reach a
    hundred times rated current at several kilohertz.

    Mitigations are inrush limiting reactors in series with each step, or point-on-wave
    switching that closes contacts when the voltage across them is zero.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<KeyIdea>
  Capacitors are the cheapest reactive support and they fail in three characteristic ways:
  they hold **lethal charge** after isolation (and partially recover it), they draw enormous
  **inrush** on switching, and they can **resonate** with system inductance at
  h = √(S_sc/Q_C) — often the 5th harmonic, which is exactly what rectifier loads produce.
  A 6–7% detuned reactor removes the third.
</KeyIdea>
