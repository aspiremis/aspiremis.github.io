The clamp meter solves the one problem that makes an ammeter awkward: to measure current in
series, you have to break the circuit.

A clamp reads the **magnetic field** around a conductor instead. Nothing is inserted,
nothing is disconnected, and no burden voltage appears in the circuit you are measuring.

<Callout type="engineering">
  The CT-based clamp is the common one and the cheapest, and its limitation follows directly
  from Faraday's law: an induced voltage requires **changing** flux. A DC current produces
  constant flux, so it induces nothing and the meter reads zero.

  A Hall-effect device measures flux density itself, so it responds to DC. On a bench with
  battery banks, PV strings or a DC link this is not a convenience — it is the difference
  between a reading and no reading.

  Rogowski coils are the interesting third option. Having no iron core, they cannot
  saturate, so a single flexible loop measures from a few amperes to tens of kiloamperes
  with a linear response. That is why fault recorders and large switchgear use them. The
  price is that the output is proportional to $di/dt$ and must be integrated, which costs
  low-frequency accuracy and rules out DC entirely.
</Callout>

## The three ways to hold it wrong

<Hazard title="Clamp exactly one conductor" level="caution">
  This is the mistake everyone makes once.

  Clamp a phase **and** its neutral together and the two currents are equal and opposite, so
  their fields cancel and the meter reads approximately **zero**. On a two-core flex that
  looks exactly like a dead circuit.

  Not merely a wrong reading — a dangerously reassuring one. Separate the conductor you want
  before clamping, or use a clamp adapter designed for the job.
</Hazard>

<Callout type="warning" title="Close the jaw completely">
  A gap in the magnetic circuit, even a small one, drops the reading substantially. Dirt,
  a bent tip or a jaw not fully latched all do it.

  You can hear and feel a properly closed jaw click. If a reading looks low, reseat before
  suspecting the circuit.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Centre the conductor">
  Accuracy is specified with the conductor centred. Held against one side of the jaw, the
  reading can shift by a few per cent — enough to matter when the specification is only
  ±2% to begin with.

  Nearby conductors carrying large currents also couple in. On a crowded panel, pull the
  conductor clear if you can.
</Callout>

## Reading small currents

Below roughly 1 A a general-purpose clamp is not trustworthy — the resolution and the
zero offset dominate.

<Callout type="tip" title="Ten turns through the jaw">
  Pass the conductor through the jaw **N** times and the meter sees N times the current.
  Divide the reading by N.

  Ten turns turns a 0.3 A current into a 3 A reading, which is comfortably inside the
  instrument's good range, and improves the effective resolution by the same factor.

  This works because a clamp measures ampere-turns, not amperes. It is the same fact that
  makes a CT ratio work, used deliberately.
</Callout>

For genuine leakage measurement — milliamperes — you need a **leakage clamp**, which has a
much more sensitive core and heavy magnetic shielding. Clamping phase and neutral
*together* is then the correct technique rather than the mistake: any imbalance between
them is current returning by another path, which is exactly what an RCD detects.

<Callout type="engineering">
  The last row matters more than it looks.

  A clamp reading zero amperes tells you no current is flowing. It says **nothing** about
  voltage. An isolated but energised circuit with no load draws no current and reads zero on
  a clamp while sitting at full voltage.

  Proving dead is a voltage test, and it follows the three-step procedure in the safety
  module. A clamp has no part in it.
</Callout>

## In your experiments

In Experiment 1 the line current is the independent variable in every plot, and a clamp
lets you take it without breaking into the circuit at each load step — which matters,
because every disconnection is an opportunity to introduce a loose connection.

For the recorded observations, prefer the power analyser's current channel: it is more
accurate and it is phase-referenced, which the clamp is not. Use the clamp as an
independent cross-check. Two instruments disagreeing is how you find a CT ratio entered
wrongly.

## Viva preparation

<Viva band="definition">
  <Q q="Why can a standard clamp meter not measure DC?">
    A CT-based clamp works by transformer action, which requires **changing** flux to induce
    a secondary voltage. A steady DC current produces constant flux, so nothing is induced
    and the meter reads zero.

    A Hall-effect clamp measures flux density directly rather than its rate of change, so it
    responds to DC as well as AC.
  </Q>

  <Q q="What happens if you clamp both the phase and neutral conductors together?">
    Their currents are equal and opposite, so the fields cancel and the meter reads
    approximately zero — indistinguishable from a dead circuit.

    Used deliberately with a sensitive leakage clamp, though, this is the correct technique:
    any residual reading is current returning by a path other than the neutral, which is
    exactly what an RCD detects.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="procedure">
  <Q q="How would you measure 300 mA accurately with a clamp meter?">
    Pass the conductor through the jaw ten times and divide the reading by ten. The meter
    sees 3 A, which is inside its accurate range, and the effective resolution improves
    tenfold.

    A clamp responds to ampere-turns, not amperes — the same principle a CT ratio uses,
    applied deliberately.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Your clamp reads lower than expected. What do you check first?">
    Whether the jaw is fully closed and latched, then whether the conductor is centred, then
    whether more than one conductor is inside the jaw.

    All three reduce the reading, and none of them produce any indication that something is
    wrong.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<KeyIdea>
  A clamp measures the field around **one** conductor without inserting anything — so no
  burden voltage, and no need to break the circuit. Clamp two conductors and the fields
  cancel to a reassuring zero; and a CT-based clamp cannot see DC at all, because
  transformer action needs changing flux.
</KeyIdea>
