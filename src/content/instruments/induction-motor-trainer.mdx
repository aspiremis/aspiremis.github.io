Around two-thirds of all industrial electricity goes through induction motors. They are
cheap, rugged, have no brushes, and start by themselves.

They also draw six times their rated current for several seconds every time they start, and
that single fact makes protecting them harder than protecting almost anything else.

## How it turns

Three windings displaced 120° in space, fed by currents displaced 120° in time, produce a
magnetic field that **rotates** at synchronous speed:

$$
N_s = \frac{120 f}{P}
$$

The rotating field induces currents in the rotor bars — hence *induction* — and those
currents, in that field, produce torque.

<Callout type="engineering">
  The rotor **must** run slower than the field. If it ever caught up, there would be no
  relative motion, no changing flux through the rotor bars, no induced current and therefore
  no torque.

  So the machine settles at a speed where the slip generates exactly the rotor current
  needed for the load torque:

  $$s = \frac{N_s - N}{N_s}$$

  This self-regulation is the whole elegance of the machine. Load it harder and it slows
  slightly, slip rises, rotor current rises, torque rises to match. No controller, no
  feedback loop, no brushes — the physics closes the loop.
</Callout>

## Why starting current is so large

At the instant of switching on, the rotor is stationary. Slip is 1, so the rotor sees the
full supply frequency, and the machine looks electrically like a **short-circuited
transformer** — the rotor is a shorted secondary.

The only thing limiting the current is the leakage impedance, which is small. Hence five to
seven times full-load current.

<Callout type="warning" title="Starting current stays high nearly to full speed">
  A common misconception is that the current tapers off smoothly as the motor accelerates.

  It does not. Current remains close to its starting value until the machine is at roughly
  80–90% of synchronous speed, then falls sharply in the last part of the run-up.

  So the protection cannot rely on the current decaying — it must ride out a near-constant
  five-to-seven times overload for the full acceleration time, which on a high-inertia load
  can be ten seconds or more.
</Callout>

  ]}
/>

<Callout type="engineering">
  Look at rows two and three. **The current is identical.** Six times full load in both
  cases, and the relay must ride through one and trip on the other.

  The only thing distinguishing them is **duration** — a start ends, a stall does not.

  That is why motor protection is fundamentally about time rather than magnitude, and why a
  plain instantaneous overcurrent element cannot protect a motor. The relay must:

  - tolerate 6× FLC for longer than the starting time,
  - trip at 6× FLC before the locked-rotor withstand time expires.

  Those two constraints define a window, and if the starting time approaches the
  locked-rotor withstand time — which happens on high-inertia loads — the window closes and
  simple overcurrent protection cannot do the job. Then you need a speed switch, or a relay
  that measures the motor's thermal state directly.
</Callout>

## The thermal replica

Modern motor protection relays do not just measure current. They **model the heating**:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{d}\theta}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{I^2}{I_{\text{FLC}}^2} - \frac{\theta}{\tau}
$$

a first-order thermal model where $\theta$ is the modelled temperature rise and $\tau$ the
thermal time constant. The relay integrates heating and allows for cooling between events.

<Callout type="tip" title="This is why cold and hot curves differ">
  A motor starting from cold has its full thermal capacity available. One that has just run
  at full load is already partly heated, so less margin remains and the allowable
  locked-rotor time is shorter.

  Nameplate and relay data therefore quote **cold** and **hot** withstand times separately,
  and a thermal replica relay knows which state the motor is in because it has been
  integrating all along.

  It also explains the rule limiting consecutive starts — typically two from cold, one from
  hot. Each start dumps a large amount of heat into the rotor, and the rotor has very little
  thermal mass.
</Callout>

## Single-phasing

One fuse blows, or one phase is lost. The motor keeps running on two phases — and this is
where the real damage happens.

<Hazard title="Single-phasing can destroy a motor without tripping overcurrent protection">
  When a phase is lost, the machine keeps turning but draws substantially more current in
  the remaining phases. On a lightly loaded motor that increased current may still sit
  **within** the relay's setting, so nothing operates.

  Meanwhile the rotor is being heated far harder than the line current suggests.
</Hazard>

<Callout type="engineering">
  The mechanism is worth understanding properly, because it explains why a separate
  protection function exists.

  Losing a phase creates a large **negative-sequence** component. And an induction motor's
  negative-sequence impedance is approximately its **locked-rotor** impedance — very small,
  around 1/6 of the positive-sequence impedance.

  So a modest negative-sequence voltage drives a **large** negative-sequence current.

  Worse, the negative-sequence field rotates *backwards* relative to the rotor, so the rotor
  sees it at a slip of $(2 - s)$ — nearly **twice supply frequency**. At 100 Hz the skin
  effect in the rotor bars raises their effective resistance considerably, so the heating
  per ampere is far greater than for positive-sequence current.

  Combined, negative-sequence current heats the rotor roughly **five to six times** as
  effectively as the same positive-sequence current.

  This is why dedicated **negative-sequence protection (ANSI 46)** exists, and why relying on
  overcurrent alone to catch single-phasing is a well-documented way to lose motors.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Star-delta cuts current to a third — and torque to a third too">
  Both scale with the square of the applied phase voltage, and star gives $1/\sqrt{3}$ of
  the delta phase voltage, so both fall to one third.

  That is a genuine constraint: if the load needs more than a third of full starting torque
  to break away, the motor will not accelerate in star at all, and the transition to delta
  then occurs at low speed and draws almost the full DOL current anyway.

  You do not get reduced current for free. You trade torque for it.
</Callout>

    'Motor mechanically secure — it will try to move on starting',
    'Phase sequence checked if the direction of rotation matters',
    'Nameplate recorded: FLC, rated speed, connection, insulation class',
    'Synchronous speed and full-load slip computed from the nameplate',
    'Everyone stands clear of the shaft before starting',
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="tip" title="Listen to it">
  A healthy induction motor has a characteristic hum that settles within a second or two of
  reaching speed.

  A **growl** or a heavy 100 Hz beat usually means single-phasing or serious unbalance. A
  rising and falling whine can mean a broken rotor bar. A rhythmic scraping means bearings.

  This is genuinely diagnostic, and it is knowledge that only comes from standing next to
  machines. Pay attention to what a good one sounds like so you notice when one is not.
</Callout>

## Viva preparation

<Viva band="definition">
  <Q q="Why must an induction motor rotor run slower than the stator field?">
    Because torque requires relative motion. If the rotor turned at synchronous speed there
    would be no changing flux through the rotor bars, no induced EMF, no rotor current and no
    torque.

    The machine settles at the slip that produces exactly the rotor current needed for the
    load torque — a self-regulating loop closed by the physics, with no controller.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why is starting current five to seven times full load current?">
    At standstill the slip is 1, so the rotor sees full supply frequency and the machine
    looks like a short-circuited transformer — the rotor is a shorted secondary.

    The current is limited only by the leakage impedance, which is small.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="analysis">
  <Q q="A starting motor and a stalled motor draw the same current. How does the protection distinguish them?">
    By duration. The magnitudes are identical — around six times full load — so the only
    difference is that a start ends and a stall does not.

    The relay must tolerate 6× FLC for longer than the starting time and trip on it before
    the locked-rotor withstand time expires. Those two constraints define a window, and on a
    high-inertia load where starting time approaches withstand time, that window can close —
    which then requires a speed switch or a thermal replica relay rather than plain
    overcurrent.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why is single-phasing so damaging, and why might overcurrent protection miss it?">
    Losing a phase produces a large negative-sequence component. The motor's negative-sequence
    impedance is roughly its locked-rotor impedance — about a sixth of positive sequence — so
    a modest negative-sequence voltage drives a large current.

    That current's field rotates backwards relative to the rotor, so the rotor sees it at
    nearly twice supply frequency, where skin effect raises the bar resistance. The heating
    per ampere is five to six times that of positive-sequence current.

    On a lightly loaded motor the increased line current may still be within the overcurrent
    setting, so nothing trips while the rotor cooks. This is why negative-sequence protection
    (ANSI 46) exists as a separate function.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Star-delta starting reduces starting current to a third. What is the cost?">
    Starting torque also falls to a third, since both scale with the square of applied phase
    voltage and star gives 1/√3 of the delta phase voltage.

    If the load requires more than a third of full starting torque to break away, the motor
    will not accelerate in star at all, and the transition to delta then happens at low speed
    drawing nearly full DOL current anyway.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<KeyIdea>
  A starting motor and a stalled motor draw the **same current** — six times full load — so
  motor protection is about time, not magnitude. And single-phasing heats the rotor five to
  six times harder per ampere than normal current, while the line current may stay within
  the overcurrent setting, which is why negative-sequence protection is a separate function.
</KeyIdea>
