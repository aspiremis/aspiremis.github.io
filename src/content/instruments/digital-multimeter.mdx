You will reach for this before anything else, and it is the instrument whose readings you
are most likely to trust when you should not.

Not because it is inaccurate — a modern DMM is remarkably good — but because it will
happily report a number in situations where that number does not mean what you think.

## Why it exists

Before the digital multimeter there was the **analogue multimeter**: a moving-coil movement
with a switched network of shunts and multipliers. It worked, and two of its problems
mattered enough to replace it.

The first was **loading**. A classic analogue meter is specified in ohms per volt — 20 kΩ/V
was good — so on a 10 V range its input resistance was 200 kΩ. Connect that across a
high-impedance node and the meter changes the voltage it is trying to measure. A DMM
presents 10 MΩ, which is high enough to ignore in nearly all laboratory work.

The second was **reading it**. A needle against a mirrored scale requires you to judge a
position, and two students reading the same needle disagree. A digital display does not
have that problem.

<Callout type="note" title="Analogue meters have not disappeared, and there is one good reason">
  A needle shows you a *trend* — rising, falling, oscillating, unstable — far better than
  digits, which just flicker. When you are adjusting a variac and watching for the point
  where a voltage stops rising, the needle is genuinely easier to read.

  This is why bargraph displays exist on good DMMs: a digital reading with an analogue
  strip underneath it, because the two answer different questions.
</Callout>

## What is inside

Every measurement a DMM makes is converted into a DC voltage, which is then digitised.
Everything before the converter is there to perform that conversion.

<Steps title="The signal path">
  <Step n={1} title="Input protection" expect="nothing, until something goes wrong">
    Series resistors, spark gaps and MOVs, plus a fuse on the current inputs. This stage
    does nothing at all in normal use and is the only reason the meter survives being
    connected to the wrong thing.
  </Step>

  <Step n={2} title="Range scaling">
    A precision resistive divider for voltage, a low-value **shunt** for current. The range
    switch selects which tap the converter sees, which is why ranges come in decades.
  </Step>

  <Step n={3} title="AC-to-DC conversion" expect="the step that determines whether an AC reading is trustworthy">
    Either an averaging rectifier or a true-RMS converter. This is the whole subject of the
    next section, and the difference between the two is the most consequential thing about
    the instrument.
  </Step>

  <Step n={4} title="Analogue-to-digital conversion">
    Classically a **dual-slope integrating** converter: charge a capacitor from the unknown
    input for a fixed time, then discharge it from a known reference and count how long
    that takes. The ratio of the two times gives the input, and component tolerances
    largely cancel because the same capacitor is used both ways.
  </Step>

  <Step n={5} title="Display">
    The counter drives the display directly.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="engineering">
  The integration time in step 4 is not arbitrary. If the converter integrates over an
  exact multiple of the mains period, any 50 Hz noise picked up on the leads integrates to
  **zero** and vanishes from the reading.

  This is why bench meters specify integration in *power line cycles* (NPLC), and why a
  slower reading is a quieter one. It is a genuinely elegant piece of engineering: the
  interference is rejected by arithmetic rather than by shielding.

  It also explains something you will notice — a meter set to a fast update rate is
  noisier. You are trading rejection for speed.
</Callout>

## True RMS, and why it matters here more than anywhere

An **average-responding** meter rectifies the AC input, measures the average, and
multiplies by a constant.

For a pure sine wave, RMS is $V_m/\sqrt{2}$ and the rectified average is $2V_m/\pi$, so the
ratio between them — the form factor — is fixed:

$$
\frac{V_{\text{rms}}}{V_{\text{avg}}} = \frac{V_m/\sqrt{2}}{2V_m/\pi} = \frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{2}} \approx 1.111
$$

So the meter measures the average and scales by 1.111. On a clean sine it is exactly right.
On anything else it is wrong, because 1.111 was only ever true for a sinusoid.

A **true-RMS** meter computes $\sqrt{\overline{v^2}}$ directly, either with an analogue
computing block or by sampling and doing the arithmetic. It is right for any waveform
inside its bandwidth and crest factor limits.

<Callout type="warning" title="Check which one you are holding before you record an AC number">
  A true-RMS meter says so on its face — *TRMS* or *True RMS* printed near the display. If
  it does not say so, assume it is average-responding.

  In a power systems laboratory this matters constantly. The moment a rectifier, a variable
  speed drive or a phase-controlled load appears in the circuit, an average-responding
  meter reads low and you have no indication that anything is wrong. Two meters on the same
  node disagreeing is the usual way people discover this.
</Callout>

<Callout type="engineering">
  True RMS is not unlimited either. Two specifications bound it.

  **Bandwidth** — typically 45 Hz to 1 kHz on a handheld. Harmonics above that are simply
  not counted, so a heavily distorted current with significant energy at the 25th harmonic
  will still read low, on a *true-RMS* meter, without warning.

  **Crest factor** — the ratio of peak to RMS, typically limited to about 3:1. The current
  drawn by a rectifier-fed power supply can exceed that, and beyond the limit the converter
  clips.

  When you genuinely need the harmonic content, the DMM is the wrong instrument and a power
  quality analyser is the right one.
</Callout>

## Reading the accuracy specification

A DMM specification looks like this:

$$
\pm(0.5\%\ \text{of reading} + 3\ \text{counts})
$$

Two terms, and they are not the same kind of thing.

- **% of reading** is proportional error — gain error in the divider and the reference.
- **counts** is a fixed error in the last digits — offset and quantisation.

Work an example. A 6000-count meter on its 60 V range has a resolution of 0.01 V. Reading
**50.00 V**:

$$
0.5\% \times 50 = 0.25\ \text{V}, \qquad 3\ \text{counts} = 0.03\ \text{V}
$$

Total uncertainty **±0.28 V**, so the true value lies between 49.72 and 50.28 V. The
display shows four significant figures; you have about three.

Now read **5.00 V** on the same 60 V range:

$$
0.5\% \times 5 = 0.025\ \text{V}, \qquad 3\ \text{counts} = 0.03\ \text{V}
$$

Total **±0.055 V** — that is **1.1%**, twice the headline figure, because the fixed term now
dominates.

<Callout type="tip" title="Always use the lowest range that will hold the reading">
  On the 6 V range instead, the same 5 V reading has a resolution of 0.001 V, so 3 counts is
  0.003 V and the total drops to ±0.028 V — better than half the error.

  This is the single most useful habit with a DMM, and autoranging does it for you. Manual
  ranging is worth it only when you need a fixed range for consistency across a sweep.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Resolution is not accuracy">
  A display showing `50.00` has a resolution of 0.01 V and an accuracy of ±0.28 V. Those
  last two digits are real — the meter is genuinely responding to changes that small — but
  they are not *correct* to that level.

  Recording all four digits in an observation table is fine. Reporting a result to four
  significant figures because the meter showed four is not.
</Callout>

## Safety category — the marking that actually matters

Printed near the input jacks you will find something like **CAT III 600 V**. This is not a
maximum voltage rating in the way you might assume. It describes the **transient
overvoltage** the instrument can survive at that location in an installation.

  headings={['Category', 'Where', 'Why the energy differs']}
  rows={[
    { option: 'CAT I', when: 'Protected electronic equipment', cost: 'No mains connection' },
    { option: 'CAT II', when: 'Appliances, socket outlets', cost: 'Impedance of the wiring limits fault energy' },
    { option: 'CAT III', when: 'Distribution boards, busbars, fixed installation', cost: 'Closer to the source — far more available energy', recommended: true },
    { option: 'CAT IV', when: 'Service entrance, overhead lines, meters', cost: 'Directly exposed to lightning and switching surges' },
  ]}
/>

The rule is that **the category matters more than the voltage number**. A CAT II 1000 V
meter is *less* safe on a distribution board than a CAT III 600 V one, because the
distribution board can deliver a transient with enough energy to flash over inside the
meter, and the CAT III instrument is built with the clearances and the energy-absorbing
input network to survive it.

<Hazard title="Never measure voltage with the leads in the current jacks">
  The current input is a **near short circuit** — a shunt of a few milliohms. Placing that
  across a live supply is placing a short across it.

  On a good meter an HRC fuse clears it. On a cheap or badly repaired one, or on a supply
  stiff enough, you get an arc flash inside an instrument you are holding in your hand.

  Build the habit now: **when you finish measuring current, move the lead back to the volts
  jack immediately.** Not later, not before the next measurement — immediately. This is the
  single most common way multimeters, and occasionally students, get hurt.
</Hazard>

## Using it well

<Steps title="Voltage">
  <Step n={1} title="Check the meter before you check the circuit" expect="the meter reading the known source correctly">
    Test it on a known live source first — a socket, a bench supply set to a known value.
    A meter with a flat battery or a broken lead reads **zero**, which is
    indistinguishable from a dead circuit.
  </Step>
  <Step n={2} title="Leads in COM and V" hazard="Confirm the current jack is empty.">
    Black to COM, red to the V/Ω jack.
  </Step>
  <Step n={3} title="Select AC or DC, and range" expect="a stable reading, not a drifting one">
    Autoranging handles the range. Selecting DC on an AC circuit gives approximately zero —
    a reading that looks exactly like a de-energised circuit.
  </Step>
  <Step n={4} title="Connect in PARALLEL, across the component">
    A voltmeter has high impedance and goes across. Getting this backwards means putting a
    10 MΩ resistor in series with your load, and the circuit stops working.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Steps title="Current">
  <Step n={1} title="De-energise first" hazard="Breaking a live circuit to insert a meter draws an arc.">
    The circuit must be broken to insert an ammeter in series. Do that dead.
  </Step>
  <Step n={2} title="Move the red lead to the A or mA jack">
    Note the fuse rating printed beside it — that is your ceiling.
  </Step>
  <Step n={3} title="Connect in SERIES, in the path of the current" expect="a reading that falls when load is removed">
    Low impedance, in line. In parallel it is a short.
  </Step>
  <Step n={4} title="Re-energise, read, de-energise, move the lead back">
    Every time.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="warning" title="Burden voltage">
  An ammeter is not free. The shunt develops a voltage — the **burden voltage** — which
  subtracts from the circuit it is inserted into. On the mA ranges of a handheld meter this
  can be hundreds of millivolts.

  Measuring a few milliamps in a 3 V circuit through a meter that drops 0.3 V means you are
  measuring a circuit that no longer exists. In a power lab, where you are usually working
  at hundreds of volts, this rarely matters — but it is the reason a clamp meter is often
  the better instrument, since it inserts nothing at all.
</Callout>

## What it cannot tell you

This is the section that turns a DMM from a magic box into an instrument you understand.

<Deck title="Four things the display will not warn you about">
  <Flashcard front="What is the phase angle between this voltage and this current?" tag="Cannot measure">
    A DMM measures magnitude only. It has no phase reference and no second channel, so it
    cannot give you power factor, real power, or the angle across a line — all of which
    Experiment 1 needs. That is what a power analyser is for.
  </Flashcard>

  <Flashcard front="Is this waveform actually a sine?" tag="Cannot measure">
    A single number carries no shape information. A distorted 230 V and a clean 230 V read
    identically on a true-RMS meter — correctly, and indistinguishably. Only an
    oscilloscope or a power quality analyser shows you the waveform.
  </Flashcard>

  <Flashcard front="Did the voltage dip for 40 ms while I was reading it?" tag="Cannot measure">
    A DMM integrates over its conversion window and updates a few times a second. A
    transient, a sag, or a switching event is averaged away or missed entirely. Catching
    those needs a recorder with a trigger.
  </Flashcard>

  <Flashcard front="Is this circuit dead?" tag="Dangerous assumption">
    A zero reading means the meter measured zero. It does not distinguish a dead circuit
    from a flat battery, a blown fuse, a broken lead or the wrong function selected. This
    is why you prove the meter on a known live source both BEFORE and AFTER testing for
    dead.
  </Flashcard>
</Deck>

## Calibration and care

Calibration is a comparison against a traceable standard, and a laboratory meter is
typically recalibrated annually. Look for the sticker: it carries the date and the next due
date, and a meter outside its interval produces numbers you cannot defend in a report.

Between calibrations:

<Checklist
  id="dmm-care"
  title="Before each session"
  items={[
    'Check the leads for cracked insulation and exposed metal at the shrouds',
    'Check that the probe tips are not bent or loose in the handles',
    'Prove the meter on a known live source',
    'Check the battery indicator — a low battery causes drift, not just a blank display',
    'Confirm the fuse rating printed by the current jacks matches what you are about to do',
    'Check the CAT rating is right for where you are measuring',
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="tip" title="Leads fail more often than meters">
  A broken conductor inside an intact-looking lead gives an intermittent or zero reading and
  will waste an hour of your session. If a reading is behaving strangely, swap the leads
  before you suspect the circuit.
</Callout>

## Where this shows up in industry

A commissioning engineer verifying a new substation panel uses a CAT IV meter to confirm
that isolation is real before working — the *prove dead* procedure, where the meter is
tested on a known source, used to test the circuit, then tested on the known source again.
Both proving steps are mandatory, and the reason is exactly the fourth flashcard above.

A maintenance technician uses insulation resistance rather than a DMM for winding tests —
a DMM's ohms range applies a few volts and tells you the winding is continuous, which is
not the same as telling you it is insulated. A megger applies 500 V or more and asks a
genuinely different question.

And a protection engineer uses a DMM for continuity and secondary wiring checks, then puts
it away and uses a relay test set for anything involving timing or phase — because, again,
the DMM cannot see phase.

## Viva preparation

<Viva band="definition">
  <Q q="What is the difference between a true-RMS and an average-responding multimeter?">
    An average-responding meter rectifies the input, measures the average and multiplies by
    the form factor 1.111, which is only correct for a pure sine wave. A true-RMS meter
    computes the square root of the mean square directly, so it is correct for any waveform
    within its bandwidth and crest factor limits.

    On distorted waveforms — rectifier loads, phase-controlled loads — an average-responding
    meter reads low, typically by 10 to 40%, with no indication that anything is wrong.
  </Q>

  <Q q="What does ±(0.5% of reading + 3 counts) mean?">
    Two error terms. The first is proportional to the measured value and comes from gain
    error in the divider and reference. The second is a fixed error of three units in the
    last displayed digit, from offset and quantisation.

    The consequence is that percentage accuracy gets worse as the reading gets smaller
    relative to the range — which is why you always use the lowest range that holds the
    reading.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why is the input impedance of a DMM 10 MΩ?">
    So it does not load the circuit. The meter is in parallel with what it measures, so its
    impedance must be much higher than the source impedance at that node, or the act of
    measuring changes the value being measured. Older analogue meters at 20 kΩ/V loaded
    high-impedance circuits noticeably.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="procedure">
  <Q q="How do you connect a multimeter to measure current, and why is it different from voltage?">
    In series, with the circuit broken and de-energised while you insert it, and with the
    red lead moved to the current jack. An ammeter is a low impedance and must carry the
    current being measured; a voltmeter is a high impedance and goes across the component.

    Connecting an ammeter in parallel places a near short circuit across the source.
  </Q>

  <Q q="What is CAT III 600 V, and is it safer than CAT II 1000 V?">
    CAT III 600 V describes transient overvoltage withstand at distribution level — fixed
    installations, busbars, distribution boards — at a working voltage of 600 V.

    And yes, at a distribution board the CAT III 600 V meter is safer, because the category
    reflects available fault energy at that location. Nearer the source, the same voltage
    can deliver far more energy into a fault, and the CAT III instrument has the clearances
    and input protection to survive it. Category first, voltage second.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Your meter reads zero across a circuit you believe is live. What do you conclude?">
    Nothing yet. A zero reading is consistent with a dead circuit, a flat battery, a blown
    current fuse, a broken lead, or the wrong function selected.

    Prove the meter on a known live source. If it reads correctly there, the circuit is
    genuinely dead — and even then, proper practice is to prove the meter again afterwards,
    in case it failed between the two tests.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="application">
  <Q q="Why can a DMM not measure power factor?">
    Power factor is the cosine of the phase angle between voltage and current. A DMM
    measures one magnitude at a time and has no phase reference and no second synchronised
    channel, so the angle is simply not available to it.

    You can infer it indirectly — measure V, I and P with a wattmeter, then compute
    cos φ = P/(VI) — but the DMM alone cannot supply P either.
  </Q>

  <Q q="In Experiment 1, why is a DMM not sufficient on its own?">
    The experiment needs sending and receiving voltage, line current, and real and reactive
    power at both ends. A DMM gives the first two as magnitudes, but voltage regulation with
    inductive and capacitive loads is fundamentally about the *phase* relationship — the
    approximate drop is I(R cos φ ± X sin φ), and the sign depends entirely on whether the
    load current leads or lags.

    Without phase you can record that the voltage fell, but not explain why, and the PV
    curve needs real power directly. Hence the power analyser.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<KeyIdea>
  A DMM gives you an accurate **magnitude** and nothing else — no phase, no waveform, no
  transient. Check whether it is true-RMS before recording any AC reading, use the lowest
  range that holds the value, and treat a zero reading as unproven until the meter has been
  tested on a known live source.
</KeyIdea>
