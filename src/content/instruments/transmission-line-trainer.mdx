A real 400 kV line is 300 km long and carries a gigawatt. This panel is a metre wide and
carries a few amperes, and the equations describing them are the same.

That substitution is what makes Experiments 1 and 3 possible at all.

## What a line actually is

A transmission line has four parameters, all **distributed** along its length rather than
lumped anywhere:

    { option: 'Series inductance', when: 'L (H/km)', cost: 'Magnetic field around the conductors' },
    { option: 'Shunt capacitance', when: 'C (F/km)', cost: 'Electric field between conductors and to earth' },
    { option: 'Shunt conductance', when: 'G (S/km)', cost: 'Leakage and corona — usually negligible' },

/>

Strictly, the line obeys the telegrapher's equations and the exact solution involves
hyperbolic functions of the propagation constant $\gamma = \sqrt{(R + j\omega L)(G + j\omega C)}$.

For lines short enough — and for teaching — a **lumped** approximation is close enough, and
far easier to build.

## The π model

Concentrate the whole series impedance in the middle, and split the total shunt admittance
into two halves, one at each end:

$$
Z = (R + j\omega L)\,\ell, \qquad \frac{Y}{2} = \frac{j\omega C \,\ell}{2}
$$

<Callout type="engineering">
  **Whether to include the shunt capacitance is what separates your two experiments.**

  Experiment 1(a) says "simplified" line: series impedance only, and the model predicts that
  no-load receiving voltage equals sending voltage, because with no load there is no current
  and therefore no drop.

  Experiment 3 measures the **Ferranti effect** — the receiving voltage *rising* above the
  sending voltage on an unloaded line. That is impossible in the short-line model. It
  requires the shunt capacitance, because the charging current drawn by that capacitance
  flows through the series inductance and produces a voltage rise.

  So the two experiments use the same panel with different sections engaged, and the
  difference between their models is the entire physical content of the second one. If your
  trainer lets you switch the shunt capacitors in and out, doing Experiment 1(a) both ways
  is a genuinely instructive five minutes.
</Callout>

## ABCD parameters

Any two-port network relates its ends by:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} V_S \\ I_S \end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} V_R \\ I_R \end{bmatrix}
$$

For a nominal π section:

$$
A = D = 1 + \frac{ZY}{2}, \qquad B = Z, \qquad C = Y\left(1 + \frac{ZY}{4}\right)
$$

<Callout type="tip" title="Two checks that catch most algebra errors">
  **$AD - BC = 1$** for any passive reciprocal network. This must hold for your measured
  parameters too, and it is the fastest check on a set of ABCD measurements.

  **$A = D$** for a symmetrical network — which a π section is. If your measured A and D
  differ substantially, the two halves are not matched, or one measurement is wrong.

  Experiment 3(a) asks you to determine ABCD parameters from open-circuit and short-circuit
  tests. Verify both identities before you believe the result.
</Callout>

And note what $A$ means physically: with the receiving end open ($I_R = 0$), $V_S = A V_R$,
so $V_R = V_S / A$. Since $A = 1 + ZY/2$ and $ZY$ is **negative real** for a lossless line
($j\omega L \times j\omega C = -\omega^2 LC$), $|A| < 1$ — and the receiving voltage exceeds
the sending voltage. That is the Ferranti effect, falling straight out of the algebra.

## How many sections

A single π section is a poor model of a long line, because it lumps at one point something
that is genuinely spread out. Splitting the line into $n$ shorter π sections in cascade
improves the approximation, and the error falls roughly as $1/n^2$.

<Callout type="engineering">
  There is a diminishing-returns argument here worth knowing.

  For a 300 km line at 50 Hz, one π section might give a few per cent error in the
  receiving-end voltage; three sections brings it under one per cent; ten sections buys
  almost nothing more, because you have already reached the accuracy of your knowledge of
  R, L and C.

  This is the same trade as mesh refinement in any numerical method: refine until the
  discretisation error drops below the uncertainty in your inputs, then stop. Refining
  further produces a more precise answer to a question whose data does not support it.
</Callout>

Your trainer's sections also let you **vary the line length** as an experimental variable —
engage more sections for a longer line and watch the regulation worsen and the Ferranti
effect strengthen.

## Reading the panel

<Steps title="Before your first use">
  <Step n={1} title="Find the nameplate or the panel legend" expect="R, X and C per section, or per kilometre with a stated length">
    Every prediction in Experiments 1 and 3 needs these numbers. Without them you can
    observe trends but not check magnitudes.
  </Step>

  <Step n={2} title="Identify how sections are engaged" expect="switches or links, and a clear indication of which are in circuit">
    Note which sections you used in your record — a result from three sections is not
    comparable with one from five.
  </Step>

  <Step n={3} title="Identify the shunt capacitor switches" hazard="These are the components that hold charge after switch-off.">
    And whether they can be disabled, which lets you compare the short-line and π models
    directly.
  </Step>

  <Step n={4} title="Note the rated current" expect="the value that will end your PV curve sweep">
    Experiment 1(b) almost certainly stops on this rather than on reaching the nose.
  </Step>

  <Step n={5} title="Compute the X/R ratio" expect="a number well below a real line's 5–20">
    A trainer's inductors have real winding resistance, so X/R is lower than a transmission
    line's. This is the single biggest reason your measurements will depart from textbook
    predictions, and knowing the number in advance lets you say so quantitatively.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="warning" title="A trainer's X/R ratio is not a transmission line's">
  A 400 kV line has X/R between about 10 and 20 — reactance dominates so completely that
  many analyses neglect R entirely.

  A bench trainer's inductors are wound with real wire and have real resistance, so X/R
  might be 3 or 4. That is closer to a **distribution feeder** than a transmission line.

  Consequences you should expect and report:

  - The resistive term in $\Delta V \approx I(R\cos\varphi + X\sin\varphi)$ contributes more
    than the textbook implies.
  - The capacitive voltage rise is weaker, because $R\cos\varphi$ has more to overcome.
  - Your measured $P_{\max}$ in Experiment 1(b) falls below the lossless $E^2/2X$, and the
    turning voltage sits above $0.707E$.

  None of these is an error. Quantifying them, with the measured X/R as the explanation, is
  a better result than matching an idealised formula.
</Callout>

## What the model cannot show you

- **Travelling waves and reflections.** A lumped model has no propagation delay, so it
  cannot represent a surge travelling along the line — which is what surge arresters and
  travelling-wave fault locators are about.
- **Corona and weather effects.** No electric field stress at bench voltage.
- **Skin effect and temperature.** R is fixed on the panel; a real conductor's resistance
  rises with both frequency and temperature.
- **Mutual coupling** between circuits on the same tower, and the untransposed asymmetry
  that produces negative-sequence voltage on real lines.

<Callout type="tip" title="Say what the model omits in your discussion">
  A report that states the trainer's X/R and names the effects the lumped model cannot
  represent demonstrates you understand the model as a **model**.

  That is a different and better thing than understanding the experiment, and it is what a
  viva is usually probing for when it asks "how realistic is this?"
</Callout>

## Viva preparation

<Viva band="definition">
  <Q q="Why is a transmission line modelled as a π section rather than a simple series impedance?">
    Because the shunt capacitance is physically distributed along the line and cannot be
    neglected beyond about 80 km. The π model splits the total shunt admittance in half and
    places one half at each end, with the whole series impedance between.

    Without the shunt branch, the model cannot produce the Ferranti effect at all — no-load
    receiving voltage would simply equal sending voltage.
  </Q>

  <Q q="What are ABCD parameters and what identities must they satisfy?">
    They relate sending-end voltage and current to receiving-end voltage and current for any
    two-port network.

    For a passive reciprocal network AD − BC = 1, and for a symmetrical network A = D. Both
    hold for a π section, and checking them is the fastest verification of a set of measured
    parameters.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="analysis">
  <Q q="Why does your trainer's behaviour differ from a real transmission line?">
    Principally the X/R ratio. A real 400 kV line has X/R of 10 to 20; a bench trainer's
    inductors have significant winding resistance, giving perhaps 3 or 4 — closer to a
    distribution feeder.

    So the resistive term contributes more to the voltage drop, the capacitive rise is
    weaker, and the maximum transferable power falls below the lossless E²/2X while the
    critical voltage sits above 0.707E.

    The lumped model also cannot represent travelling waves, corona, skin effect or mutual
    coupling between circuits.
  </Q>

  <Q q="How many π sections do you need to model a long line?">
    Enough that the discretisation error drops below the uncertainty in your knowledge of R,
    L and C. For a 300 km line, one section might give a few per cent error and three brings
    it under one per cent; beyond that the improvement is smaller than the data supports.

    The alternative is the exact solution using hyperbolic functions of the propagation
    constant, which has no discretisation error at all.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<KeyIdea>
  The π model concentrates the series impedance and splits the shunt admittance at both ends
  — and **that shunt branch is the entire physical content of the Ferranti effect**, absent
  from the short-line model. Record your trainer's X/R: at 3 or 4 rather than a real line's
  10 to 20, it explains most of the gap between your measurements and the textbook.
</KeyIdea>
