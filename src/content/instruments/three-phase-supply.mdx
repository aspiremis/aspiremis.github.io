Nearly all electrical power is generated, transmitted and consumed as three phases. It is
worth understanding why, because the reasons explain most of what a power system looks
like.

## Why three

**Constant instantaneous power.** In a single-phase system the instantaneous power pulses
at twice the supply frequency, falling to zero twice per cycle. Sum three phases displaced
by 120° and the pulsations cancel exactly:

$$
p(t) = \sum_{k=1}^{3} V_m I_m \cos(\omega t - \theta_k)\cos(\omega t - \theta_k - \varphi) = \tfrac{3}{2}V_m I_m \cos\varphi
$$

A constant. So a three-phase motor produces smooth torque where a single-phase one
vibrates at 100 Hz.

**A rotating magnetic field for free.** Three windings displaced 120° in space, fed by
currents displaced 120° in time, produce a magnetic field that rotates at synchronous speed
with no commutator, brushes or starting mechanism. The induction motor — the workhorse of
industry — exists because of this.

**Less copper.** A three-phase system delivers a given power with substantially less
conductor material than three separate single-phase circuits, because the return currents
share a path and, when balanced, cancel entirely.

<Callout type="engineering">
  That last point is the economic argument, and it is why the whole grid is built this way.

  In a **balanced** three-phase four-wire system the three phase currents sum to zero:

  $$I_R + I_Y + I_B = 0$$

  so the neutral carries **nothing**. You are delivering three circuits' worth of power over
  four conductors instead of six, and the fourth is barely loaded.

  This is also why transmission lines have no neutral at all — at transmission voltages the
  load is balanced by design, so the conductor would carry nothing and cost a great deal.
</Callout>

Both give the same total power:

$$
P = \sqrt{3}\,V_L I_L \cos\varphi
$$

<Callout type="engineering">
  **The delta's harmonic trapping is why so many distribution transformers are Dyn11.**

  Third-harmonic currents in a three-phase system are **in phase** with each other — they
  are a zero-sequence component. In a star connection they add rather than cancel, so they
  all flow in the neutral, which is why neutral conductors on circuits with many electronic
  loads can carry more current than the phases.

  In a delta they have nowhere to go but round and round inside the winding, so they never
  reach the line. The delta is a harmonic trap, and it is free.

  Hence the standard Dyn11 distribution transformer: the delta HV winding keeps triplen
  harmonics off the transmission system, while the star LV winding supplies a neutral for
  single-phase loads.
</Callout>

## Phase sequence

The order in which the phases reach their peak — RYB or RBY — determines which way a
three-phase motor turns.

<Hazard title="Swapping two phases reverses a motor" level="caution">
  This is a one-connection error with mechanical consequences. A pump running backwards may
  not move fluid; a machine tool running backwards can destroy a workpiece or itself.

  Before connecting any three-phase machine, check the sequence with a **phase sequence
  indicator** — a small device with two lamps or a rotating disc that shows the order
  directly.

  And note: to reverse a motor deliberately you swap **any two** phases. Swapping all three
  changes nothing.
</Hazard>

In symmetrical component terms, the sequence *is* the positive-sequence direction. Reverse
it and you have applied negative-sequence voltage — which in an induction machine produces
a field rotating backwards, large currents and severe heating. Negative-sequence protection
exists for exactly this.

## Unbalance

When phase loads differ, three things happen:

- **The neutral carries current.** In a four-wire system the residual returns through the
  neutral, which is why it must be sized properly and never fused or switched.
- **Phase voltages shift.** With any impedance in the neutral, the star point moves and the
  three phase voltages become unequal — lightly loaded phases rise, heavily loaded phases
  fall.
- **Negative sequence appears.** Which heats motors disproportionately.

<Hazard title="A broken neutral is one of the most dangerous faults in LV distribution">
  With the neutral intact, each single-phase load sits at 240 V regardless of the others.

  Break the neutral and the star point is no longer tied. The three loads are now in series
  across 415 V, and the voltage divides in proportion to their impedances. A lightly loaded
  phase — a few LED lamps — can rise to nearly **400 V**, while a heavily loaded phase
  collapses.

  Everything on the lightly loaded phase is destroyed at once, and this presents as
  "several appliances failed simultaneously" rather than as an obvious electrical fault.

  This is why the neutral is never fused, never switched independently, and why
  neutral-integrity monitoring exists on distribution networks.
</Hazard>

<Callout type="warning" title="Your supply is not stiff">
  Every experiment on this site assumes a fixed sending-end voltage. Your laboratory
  supply is fed through the building's distribution, and it sags when the bench next to
  yours switches on a load bank.

  Record $V_S$ at **every** step and use the measured value in every calculation. A supply
  sag that you attribute to your line model is the most common systematic error in
  Experiment 1.
</Callout>

## Viva preparation

<Viva band="definition">
  <Q q="Why is three-phase power preferred to single-phase?">
    Three reasons. The instantaneous power of a balanced three-phase system is constant
    rather than pulsating at twice supply frequency, so machines produce smooth torque.
    Three windings fed by three time-displaced currents produce a rotating magnetic field
    without any commutator, which is what makes the induction motor possible. And a given
    power is delivered with substantially less conductor, because balanced return currents
    cancel.
  </Q>

  <Q q="What is the relationship between line and phase quantities?">
    In star, line voltage is √3 times phase voltage and line current equals phase current.
    In delta, line voltage equals phase voltage and line current is √3 times phase current.

    Total power is √3 V_L I_L cos φ in both cases.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="analysis">
  <Q q="Why does a delta winding trap third-harmonic currents?">
    Third harmonics in a three-phase system are in phase with each other — a zero-sequence
    component. In a delta they simply circulate around the closed loop and never appear in
    the line currents.

    In a star connection with a neutral they add instead of cancelling and all flow in the
    neutral, which is why neutrals on circuits with many electronic loads can carry more
    current than the phases.
  </Q>

  <Q q="What happens if the neutral of a four-wire supply breaks?">
    The star point floats. The three single-phase loads are then effectively in series
    across the line voltage, and it divides in proportion to their impedances.

    A lightly loaded phase can rise to nearly 400 V and everything on it is destroyed;
    a heavily loaded phase collapses. This is why the neutral is never fused or switched.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<KeyIdea>
  Three phases give constant instantaneous power, a rotating field for free, and a neutral
  that carries nothing when balanced. **Phase sequence decides motor direction** — swap any
  two to reverse it — and a **broken neutral** lets the star point float, driving lightly
  loaded phases toward line voltage.
</KeyIdea>
