A voltage transformer does for voltage what a CT does for current: scales it to a standard
low value — 110 V — that instruments and relays are built for, with isolation from the
primary.

The useful way to learn it is as the CT's mirror image. Nearly every rule inverts.

<Hazard title="Never short-circuit a VT secondary">
  A VT is a voltage source. Short its secondary and the primary voltage — fixed by the
  system, which does not care — drives an enormous current through the winding.

  The VT burns out, usually violently, and it is connected to the full system voltage while
  doing so.

  This is why VT secondaries are fused, and why the fuse rating matters: too large and it
  will not clear before the winding fails.
</Hazard>

<Callout type="warning" title="Fuses on both sides, for different reasons">
  **Secondary fuses** protect the VT from a fault in the wiring out to the relays and
  meters — a short in the panel should not destroy the transformer.

  **Primary fuses** protect the *system* from the VT. A VT failing internally becomes a
  fault on the busbar it is connected to, and without a primary fuse that fault is cleared
  by the busbar protection — tripping everything on the bus, for one small failed
  transformer.

  The two fuses face in opposite directions, protecting different things.
</Callout>

## Why the burden is high

A VT is connected in parallel and should draw as little current as possible, so its burden
is a **high** impedance — the input of a voltmeter or relay.

Loading a VT more heavily than its rating causes an internal voltage drop across the
winding impedance, so the secondary reads low and the phase shifts. Both errors move it
outside its accuracy class.

Note that this is the same *symptom* as an overloaded CT — under-reading — arriving by a
completely different mechanism.

## Ferroresonance

This is the VT failure mode with no CT equivalent, and it is genuinely subtle.

A VT has a **nonlinear inductance** — the magnetising inductance of its iron core, which
falls sharply once the core saturates. Put that in a circuit with capacitance — the
capacitance of a busbar, a cable, or the grading capacitors of a circuit breaker — and you
have a resonant circuit whose resonant frequency depends on the amplitude.

<Callout type="engineering">
  In an ordinary LC circuit, resonance happens at one frequency and you can design around
  it.

  With a **saturable** inductance the effective L depends on how hard the core is driven, so
  the resonant frequency moves with amplitude. The system can jump into a sustained
  oscillation — often at a subharmonic, a third or a fifth of supply frequency — and stay
  there, drawing large currents and developing overvoltages, until something fails or the
  circuit is broken.

  It is typically triggered by a **switching event**: energising a busbar, opening an
  isolator, or the transient after a fault is cleared. Everything is normal, one switching
  operation happens, and a VT that has run for twenty years begins to overheat and destroys
  itself within minutes.

  The classic mitigation is a damping resistor across the open-delta tertiary winding, which
  absorbs energy at the subharmonic frequency and suppresses the oscillation. It is why so
  many three-phase VT sets carry a third winding that seems to do nothing in normal
  operation.
</Callout>

## Wound VTs and CVTs

Above roughly 66 kV, a wound VT becomes very large and expensive — the insulation
requirement dominates.

A **capacitor voltage transformer** instead uses a capacitive divider to drop the system
voltage to an intermediate level, then a conventional wound transformer from there. The
capacitor stack is cheaper than insulating a full winding at EHV, and it can double as a
coupling capacitor for power line carrier communication.

<Callout type="engineering">
  The transient response row has a real protection consequence.

  When a close-in fault collapses the voltage almost to zero, a CVT's capacitor stack cannot
  follow instantly — stored energy discharges through the circuit and produces a **transient
  output** that does not represent the actual system voltage for a cycle or more.

  A distance relay measuring impedance as V/I during that window sees a voltage that is
  wrong, and may over-reach — tripping for a fault outside its zone.

  Modern numerical distance relays include specific CVT transient detection logic to handle
  exactly this. It is a good example of protection design compensating for a known
  measurement artefact rather than pretending the instrument is ideal.
</Callout>

## In your experiments

At 415 V your bench does not need a PT — the analyser's voltage inputs connect directly.
You will meet them in the protection lab and in any substation.

What transfers now is the **mirror-image discipline**: CT secondaries are shorted before
disconnection, VT secondaries are never shorted. Getting these the wrong way round destroys
equipment in both directions.

## Viva preparation

<Viva band="definition">
  <Q q="Why must a VT secondary never be shorted, when a CT secondary must be?">
    Because they are opposite kinds of source. A CT is a current source — its primary
    current is fixed by the system, so an open secondary leaves nothing to oppose the
    primary MMF and the core saturates, producing kilovolts.

    A VT is a voltage source — its primary voltage is fixed by the system, so a shorted
    secondary draws enormous current and burns the winding out.

    Each is safe in the state that would destroy the other.
  </Q>

  <Q q="What is the rated voltage factor of a VT?">
    How much overvoltage the VT can withstand and for how long — typically 1.2 times rated
    continuously, and 1.5 or 1.9 times for 30 seconds.

    It matters because during an earth fault on an unearthed or impedance-earthed system,
    the healthy phases rise toward line voltage — a factor of √3 — and the VTs on those
    phases must survive it long enough for the protection to clear the fault.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="analysis">
  <Q q="What is ferroresonance, and why does it affect VTs?">
    A sustained oscillation between the nonlinear magnetising inductance of a VT core and
    circuit capacitance — busbar, cable, or circuit breaker grading capacitors.

    Because the inductance is saturable, the resonant frequency depends on amplitude, so the
    circuit can lock into a sustained oscillation, often at a subharmonic, drawing large
    currents and producing overvoltages until something fails.

    It is usually triggered by a switching event. The standard mitigation is a damping
    resistor across an open-delta tertiary winding.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why does a CVT cause problems for distance protection?">
    When a close-in fault collapses the voltage almost to zero, the capacitor stack cannot
    follow instantly, and its stored energy produces a transient output that does not
    represent the true system voltage for a cycle or more.

    A distance relay computing impedance as V/I during that window sees an erroneous
    voltage and may over-reach, tripping for a fault outside its zone. Numerical relays
    include CVT transient detection to compensate.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<KeyIdea>
  A VT is the CT's **mirror image** — a voltage source, connected in parallel, with a high
  burden impedance, and never to be shorted. Its unique failure mode is ferroresonance,
  where a saturable inductance and circuit capacitance lock into a sustained subharmonic
  oscillation after an ordinary switching event.
</KeyIdea>
