Every experiment in your PSAO hardware lab starts with "raise the variac slowly". This is
what you are turning.

## One winding, not two

An ordinary transformer has two electrically separate windings coupled by a magnetic
circuit. An **autotransformer** has one winding with a tap, and the variac makes that tap
movable — a carbon brush sliding along a bared track on the toroidal winding.

The output is a fraction of the winding, so:

$$
\frac{V_{\text{out}}}{V_{\text{in}}} = \frac{N_{\text{tapped}}}{N_{\text{total}}}
$$

Sliding the brush changes $N_{\text{tapped}}$ continuously, giving smooth control from zero
to full.

<Hazard title="A variac does not isolate">
  This is the whole safety story, and it catches people constantly.

  With one winding, the output is **electrically continuous** with the input. The output
  neutral *is* the supply neutral. Turning the dial to zero shorts the output to the input
  neutral — it does not disconnect anything.

  Consequences:

  - Touching the output "live" terminal at any dial setting can put you across the supply.
  - The output cannot be treated as floating. Referencing an oscilloscope to it will connect
    the scope's earthed probe ground to a live conductor.
  - Isolation requires a **two-winding isolation transformer**, and no dial position on a
    variac substitutes for one.

  If your bench has both, they do different jobs and are in series for a reason.
</Hazard>

## Why it is efficient

An autotransformer is smaller, cheaper and more efficient than a two-winding transformer of
the same rating, and the reason is worth understanding.

Only the **difference** between input and output power is transferred magnetically. The
rest passes conductively through the shared winding. So for a ratio close to 1:1, most of
the power never goes through the core at all, and the core can be far smaller than the
throughput suggests.

<Callout type="engineering">
  This is also the argument for **autotransformers in transmission**: interconnecting 400 kV
  and 220 kV systems, where the ratio is under 2:1, an autotransformer is dramatically
  cheaper and lighter than a two-winding unit of the same MVA.

  The trade is exactly the one on your bench, scaled up. There is no galvanic separation
  between the two systems, so a fault or a disturbance on one side is not blocked from the
  other, and the earthing arrangements of the two systems become coupled.

  Efficiency and isolation are the two things you are choosing between, and you cannot have
  both from one winding.
</Callout>

## The rating is in amperes

A variac's limit is the current its brush and winding can carry, and that limit is the same
at every dial position. So:

$$
S_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{out}} \times I_{\text{rated}}
$$

which means the available **kVA falls in proportion to the output voltage**. A 10 A variac
gives 2.4 kVA at 240 V and only 1.2 kVA at 120 V.

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not size a variac from kVA alone">
  A "2 kVA variac" is a 2 kVA variac *at full output*. At half output it will supply 1 kVA,
  and loading it beyond its current rating at any dial position overheats the brush contact
  — which is the part that fails.

  Size from the **current** your load will draw.
</Callout>

## The output can exceed the input

Many variacs wind a little extra beyond the input tap, so the dial runs past 100% — often
to 110% or 115%, giving up to about 270 V from a 240 V supply.

<Callout type="warning" title="Check where the input tap sits before you turn the dial">
  This exists so you can compensate for a low supply. It also means an inattentive turn past
  the marked nominal position applies an overvoltage to whatever you are feeding.

  Look at the scale. If it goes past 240, know that it does before you start turning.
</Callout>

## Using it well

<Steps title="Every time">
  <Step n={1} title="Confirm it is at ZERO before energising" hazard="Energising with the dial up applies full voltage instantly to a circuit you may not have finished wiring.">
    Look at the dial, do not assume. Then confirm which direction increases — it is not
    always clockwise.
  </Step>

  <Step n={2} title="Raise slowly, watching the meters" expect="voltage rising smoothly, current rising as expected">
    Slowly enough to react. A wiring error usually announces itself in the first few volts,
    and stopping at 20 V costs nothing where stopping at 240 V may cost equipment.
  </Step>

  <Step n={3} title="Set your working voltage and leave it alone" expect="V_S constant for the whole sweep">
    Experiment 1 depends on this. Adjusting the variac to correct a sagging voltage means
    measuring your own reflexes rather than the line.
  </Step>

  <Step n={4} title="Return to zero before switching off" expect="the dial at zero, every time">
    So the next person energises into nothing. This is the habit worth being pedantic about.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="tip" title="Listen to it">
  A healthy variac is nearly silent. A loud hum usually means it is overloaded or the core
  is saturating; a crackle or intermittent output means the brush contact is dirty or worn,
  or the track is pitted.

  Carbon dust accumulating inside is normal and eventually causes tracking. If the output is
  unstable as you turn the dial, report it rather than working around it.
</Callout>

<Callout type="engineering">
  The dimmer row matters for measurement work.

  A phase-controlled dimmer varies voltage by chopping part of each half cycle away. The RMS
  falls, but the waveform is now rich in harmonics — which means an average-responding meter
  reads it wrongly, and any experiment assuming a sinusoid is invalidated.

  A variac shifts the amplitude and leaves the shape alone. For laboratory work where the
  theory assumes sinusoidal quantities, that distinction is the whole reason it is a variac
  on the bench and not a cheaper electronic alternative.
</Callout>

## Viva preparation

<Viva band="definition">
  <Q q="What is the difference between an autotransformer and a two-winding transformer?">
    An autotransformer has a single winding with a tap, so the primary and secondary share
    turns and are electrically continuous. A two-winding transformer has two separate
    windings coupled only magnetically, giving galvanic isolation.

    The autotransformer is smaller, cheaper and more efficient because only the difference
    between input and output power passes through the core — but it provides no isolation
    whatsoever.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why is a variac rated in amperes rather than kVA?">
    Its limit is the current the brush contact and winding can carry, and that is the same
    at every dial position. Since S = V × I with I fixed, the available kVA falls in
    proportion to the output voltage.

    A 10 A variac gives 2.4 kVA at 240 V and 1.2 kVA at 120 V.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="procedure">
  <Q q="Is it safe to touch the variac output when the dial is at zero?">
    No. There is one winding, so the output is electrically continuous with the supply
    regardless of dial position. At zero the output is shorted to the supply neutral, not
    disconnected from it.

    Isolation requires a two-winding isolation transformer. No dial position substitutes.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why raise the variac slowly rather than switching straight to the working voltage?">
    So that a wiring error announces itself at a voltage low enough to be harmless. Most
    faults show up in the first few volts — a meter reading backwards, a current where there
    should be none, a hum from something that should be silent.

    Stopping at 20 V costs nothing. Discovering the same error at 240 V may cost equipment
    or worse.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<KeyIdea>
  One winding is what makes a variac efficient and what makes it dangerous: the output is
  **electrically continuous with the supply**, so zero on the dial is not disconnection.
  Rated in amperes, so available kVA falls with the setting — and it preserves the waveform,
  which a phase-controlled dimmer does not.
</KeyIdea>
