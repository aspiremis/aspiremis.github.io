Nobody is killed by volts. People are killed by **current**, and by a smaller current than
almost anyone expects.

The figure that ought to stay with you is this: the current that will kill you is roughly
**one twentieth** of what it takes to light a 10 W bulb.

## The numbers

These are the thresholds from IEC 60479, for 50 Hz alternating current passing hand to
hand through an adult. They vary between people, and the boundaries are soft — but the
orders of magnitude do not move.

<Callout type="warning" title="The let-go threshold is the one to memorise">
  Below about 10 mA you can pull away. Above it your flexor muscles — the ones that close
  your hand — contract harder than the extensors that open it, so **your grip tightens on
  the conductor**.

  You are then held in the circuit, and what would have been a startle becomes a fatal
  exposure a few seconds later. This is why the single most useful habit in a laboratory
  is never to *grip* anything you have not proved dead. Approach with the back of a hand,
  not the palm.
</Callout>

## Why 230 V is not survivable by luck

Ohm's law, with the body as the resistance.

$$
I = \frac{V}{R_{\text{body}}}
$$

Body resistance is not a constant — it depends almost entirely on skin condition:

| Condition | Hand-to-hand resistance |
|---|---|
| Dry, intact skin | 10 000 – 100 000 Ω |
| Damp skin | ~1 000 Ω |
| Wet, or broken skin | 500 – 1 000 Ω |
| Internal tissue alone | 300 – 500 Ω |

Now compute. At **230 V**, with damp hands at 1 000 Ω:

$$
I = \frac{230}{1000} = 230\ \text{mA}
$$

That is more than four times the fibrillation threshold.

Even with dry skin at 10 000 Ω you get 23 mA — past let-go, into respiratory arrest
territory. And **the skin's resistance collapses within milliseconds of the current
starting**, because the outer layer breaks down. So the dry-skin figure describes only the
first instant; what follows is the wet-skin case.

<Callout type="engineering">
  This is why the lab benches have **residual current devices** set at 30 mA.

  An RCD compares the current going out with the current coming back. If they differ by
  more than 30 mA, some current is returning by another path — through you, through a
  fault to earth — and it trips in under 40 ms.

  Note what it is doing: 30 mA is *above* the let-go threshold and *below* the fibrillation
  threshold, and 40 ms is short enough that the heart is unlikely to be caught in its
  vulnerable phase. The device is not preventing a shock. It is arranging for the shock to
  end before it becomes lethal.

  A fuse or MCB will not do this. A 16 A MCB sees 230 mA through your body as an entirely
  normal load and never operates.
</Callout>

## Path matters more than magnitude

The same current can be harmless or fatal depending on where it flows.

<Analogy>
  Think of the body as a resistive network with one critically sensitive component in the
  middle of it.

  Current that flows **finger to finger on one hand** takes a short path through tissue
  that does not much care. Current that flows **left hand to right hand**, or **hand to
  opposite foot**, passes directly through the chest — and therefore through the heart.

  The magnitude is identical. The consequence is not.
</Analogy>

This produces the oldest rule in electrical work, and it is worth adopting as a physical
habit rather than a piece of knowledge:

<Hazard title="Keep one hand behind your back, or in a pocket">
  When you must work near anything that might be live, use **one hand only**.

  With one hand, a shock path runs hand-to-foot — bad, but it may miss the heart. With two
  hands on the equipment, the path runs arm to arm, straight across the chest.

  This costs you nothing and it is the difference between an injury and a fatality. Adopt
  it now, while you are learning, so that it is automatic by the time you are working on
  something that matters.
</Hazard>

## Why 50 Hz is close to the worst case

Mains frequency is, unluckily, near the most dangerous frequency for the human heart.

The heart has a **vulnerable period** in each beat — a window during repolarisation when an
external stimulus can trigger fibrillation. At 50 Hz the current reverses every 10 ms, so
within a single heartbeat there are dozens of opportunities to catch that window.

<Callout type="warning" title="DC is not safe, it is differently dangerous">
  DC produces one strong contraction at make and another at break rather than a sustained
  tetanic grip, so the let-go threshold is higher.

  But DC cannot be interrupted by a zero crossing, so a DC arc — once struck — sustains
  itself far more readily than an AC arc. This is exactly why DC switchgear is physically
  larger than AC switchgear at the same rating, and why battery banks and PV strings are
  taken very seriously.
</Callout>

## Three hazards, not one

Shock is the one everyone thinks of. It is not the only one, and on a distribution-level
bench it may not be the most dangerous.

### Arc flash

A short circuit through air produces an arc. The arc column reaches roughly **20 000 K** —
about three times the surface of the sun — in a few milliseconds.

It does not need to touch you. The radiated heat alone causes severe burns at a distance,
and clothing that melts rather than chars makes the injury far worse. This is why synthetic
fabrics are banned in laboratories and why arc-rated clothing exists.

### Arc blast

The same arc vaporises copper. Copper expands roughly **67 000 times** on vaporising, and
that expansion is a pressure wave.

The blast throws people across rooms, ruptures eardrums, and turns switchgear panels and
molten metal into projectiles. Injuries from arc blast are frequently mechanical rather
than electrical.

### Secondary injury

The most common laboratory injury from electricity is not the shock. It is what the
**startle** causes — jerking a hand back into a rotating shaft, falling off a stool,
dropping a tool into an energised panel.

<Callout type="engineering">
  Rank these by what actually happens on a teaching bench.

  At 230 V single phase, shock is the primary risk. At **415 V three phase with real fault
  current behind it** — which is what feeds a PSAO bench — arc flash becomes at least as
  serious, because the available fault energy is now enough to sustain a substantial arc.

  This is the physical meaning of the CAT rating on your multimeter. It is not about the
  voltage. It is about how much energy the supply can pour into a fault at that point in
  the installation.
</Callout>

## Stored energy: the hazard after switch-off

Switching off does not de-energise a capacitor.

$$
W = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2
$$

A 100 µF capacitor charged to 400 V holds:

$$
W = \tfrac{1}{2} \times 100\times10^{-6} \times 400^2 = 8\ \text{J}
$$

Eight joules sounds trivial. Delivered through the chest in a few milliseconds it is
comparable to what a defibrillator uses deliberately — which tells you it is entirely
capable of stopping a heart rather than restarting one.

<Hazard title="Capacitors in Experiments 1, 3 and 5">
  Your capacitive load bank and any line-model shunt capacitance hold charge after
  isolation. Discharge resistors take time and **can fail open without any visible sign**.

  The procedure is not negotiable:

  1. Isolate, and lock off if you can.
  2. Wait for the stated discharge time.
  3. **Measure** across the terminals with a meter you have proved on a known source.
  4. Only then touch anything.

  An indicator lamp going out is not proof. A lamp draws current; it can be dark while the
  capacitor still holds hundreds of volts through a failed connection.
</Hazard>

## If someone is being shocked

<Steps title="In order, and the first step is the one people get wrong">
  <Step n={1} title="DO NOT TOUCH THEM" hazard="If they are still in contact with a live conductor, touching them puts you in the circuit too. Two casualties help nobody.">
    Your instinct will be to pull them away. Override it.
  </Step>

  <Step n={2} title="Cut the supply" expect="the emergency stop is the fastest route — you should already know where it is">
    Hit the emergency stop, or open the isolator. This is why you locate the E-stop
    *before* you start work, every session.
  </Step>

  <Step n={3} title="If you cannot cut the supply, use an insulator">
    Push them clear with something dry and non-conducting — a wooden broom handle, a
    plastic chair. Never anything metal or damp, and never your hands.
  </Step>

  <Step n={4} title="Call for help immediately">
    Shout for the demonstrator. Send someone specific for medical help — point at a person
    and tell them to go, because a general appeal to a room produces nobody moving.
  </Step>

  <Step n={5} title="Check breathing; begin CPR if trained">
    Electrical shock kills by stopping the heart. CPR keeps blood moving until a
    defibrillator arrives, and it is the single intervention that changes the outcome.
  </Step>

  <Step n={6} title="Insist on medical attention even if they seem fine">
    Cardiac arrhythmias can develop hours after an electrical shock, and internal burns
    along the current path are invisible from outside. "I feel fine" is not a diagnosis.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="tip" title="Learn where these are on your first day">
  Not when you need them.

  - The **emergency stop** for your bench, and the master for the room
  - The **main isolator** for the laboratory
  - The **first aid kit** and who the trained first aider is
  - The **fire extinguisher** rated for electrical fires — CO₂ or dry powder, **never
    water**
  - The **exit route**, and the alternative one

  Walk to each of them once. Physically. Knowing where something is on a plan is different
  from knowing where it is in a room you are panicking in.
</Callout>

<KeyIdea>
  **10 mA and you cannot let go. 50 mA and your heart may fibrillate.** At 230 V through
  damp skin that is 230 mA — so the margin is not a factor of two, it is a factor of five in
  the wrong direction.

  Work one-handed, prove things dead before touching them, and know where the emergency
  stop is before you need it.
</KeyIdea>
