Your measurements are worth exactly as much as your record of them. A perfect experiment
with an unreadable record book is a wasted afternoon.

## The record book

The convention is not arbitrary — every rule exists because of a specific way records fail.

    { option: 'Never erase or use correction fluid', when: 'An obliterated entry looks like concealment. Cross out with one line so the original stays legible' },

    { option: 'Never write on loose paper', when: 'It gets lost. Every time' },
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="warning" title="Record raw readings, not what you calculated in your head">
  If you write down 4.2% regulation instead of the two voltages, and you later realise the
  formula should have used the measured $V_S$ rather than the nominal one, you cannot fix
  it. The information is gone.

  Write down what the meter showed. Every derived quantity can be recomputed from raw
  readings; raw readings cannot be recovered from a derived quantity.
</Callout>

<Callout type="engineering">
  This convention comes from research and industrial practice, where it carries real weight.

  A bound, paginated, ink-written, dated and countersigned notebook is legal evidence — in
  patent disputes, in accident investigations, in regulatory audits. Pharmaceutical and
  aerospace laboratories run on exactly these rules, and pages are numbered specifically so
  a removed page is detectable.

  You are learning the discipline at low stakes so that it is automatic when the stakes are
  higher. It will also matter for your thesis: a supervisor asking "when did you take this
  measurement?" wants an answer with a date on it.
</Callout>

### What to record beyond the numbers

- The **conditions**: ambient temperature, supply voltage, anything that might explain an
  anomaly later.
- **Anything unexpected**, even if you cannot explain it. A note reading *"reading unstable
  above step 4, possibly a loose lead"* is worth a great deal in a week's time.
- **What you changed** between readings, in the order you changed it.
- **Which meter** you used for which quantity, if there is more than one.

<Callout type="tip" title="Get it signed before you leave the room">
  Most laboratories require the demonstrator to sign your raw observations at the bench.

  Unsigned readings can be rejected, and there is no way to recreate them once the bench is
  dismantled and rewired for another group. Make it the last item on your shutdown
  checklist.
</Callout>

## The report

Every section answers one question. Knowing which question stops you writing the wrong
thing in it.

<Steps title="Report structure">
  <Step n={1} title="Aim" expect="one or two sentences, specific enough to be checkable">
    *What were you trying to find out?*

    "To study voltage regulation" is too vague. "To determine the voltage regulation of a
    simplified transmission line model under resistive, inductive and capacitive loading,
    and to compare the result with the approximate drop formula" is an aim you can be held
    to.
  </Step>

  <Step n={2} title="Apparatus" expect="enough for someone to assemble the same bench">
    *What did you use?*

    List with ranges, ratings and identifiers. "Voltmeter" is useless; "Digital multimeter,
    true RMS, 0–600 V AC, ±(1.0% + 5 counts), ID DMM-04" lets a reader judge your
    uncertainties.
  </Step>

  <Step n={3} title="Theory" expect="the derivation, not a copied textbook chapter">
    *What should happen, and why?*

    Derive the relationships you will use. Keep it to what the experiment needs — a
    reproduced chapter shows you can copy, a focused derivation shows you understood.
  </Step>

  <Step n={4} title="Procedure" expect="past tense, what you actually did">
    *What did you do?*

    Including any departure from the sheet. If you stopped a sweep early because you reached
    rated current, that belongs here — it is a fact about the experiment, not a confession.
  </Step>

  <Step n={5} title="Observations" expect="raw data, tabulated, with units in the headers">
    *What did you measure?*

    Raw readings only. No calculated columns here — those go in the next section, so a
    reader can separate measurement from inference.
  </Step>

  <Step n={6} title="Calculations" expect="one worked example in full, then a table">
    *How did you get from readings to results?*

    Work through **one** row completely, showing the substitution, then tabulate the rest.
    This lets a marker verify your method without wading through forty repetitions, and it
    is where you show the uncertainty propagation.
  </Step>

  <Step n={7} title="Results and graphs" expect="axes labelled with quantity AND unit, and a caption that states the finding">
    *What did you find?*

    Plot measured points as markers, theory as a line. Never join measured points with a
    smooth curve — the line implies you measured the space between them.
  </Step>

  <Step n={8} title="Discussion" expect="the section that actually carries the marks">
    *What does it mean, and why did it not match perfectly?*

    See below.
  </Step>

  <Step n={9} title="Conclusion" expect="the aim, answered">
    *So what?*

    Two or three sentences answering the aim directly. No new information.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="engineering">
  **The discussion is where the marks are, and it is the section students write last and
  shortest.**

  A discussion that says "the results agree with theory, small errors were due to
  instrument error and human error" is worth almost nothing. It is the sentence everybody
  writes, and markers read it as "I did not think about this".

  A good discussion does four specific things:

  1. **Quantifies** the disagreement — "measured regulation was 12% higher than predicted"
     rather than "there was some error".
  2. **Attributes** it to a named mechanism, with an argument — "the approximate formula
     neglects the quadrature term, which grows with load angle, and the discrepancy indeed
     grows with current".
  3. **Tests** the attribution where possible — "recomputing with the exact phasor
     expression reduces the gap to 1.8%, supporting this explanation".
  4. **States** what remains unexplained. Honesty about a residual you cannot account for
     is worth more than a confident wrong story.

  Point 3 is the one that separates a good report from an average one. Anyone can name a
  possible cause; showing that the cause quantitatively accounts for what you saw is
  engineering.
</Callout>

## The viva

A viva examines whether you understood what you did, and it has a recognisable structure.
Examiners work outward.

<Compare
  headings={['Stage', 'Typical question', 'What is being tested']}
  rows={[
    { option: 'Definitions', when: '"What is voltage regulation?"', cost: 'Did you learn the vocabulary?', recommended: false },
    { option: 'Procedure', when: '"Why did you keep V_S constant?"', cost: 'Did you understand what you were doing, or follow steps?' },
    { option: 'Analysis', when: '"Why is your result 12% off?"', cost: 'Can you reason about your own data?' },
    { option: 'Application', when: '"Where would a utility use this?"', cost: 'Do you see past the bench?' },
    { option: 'Extension', when: '"What if the load were a constant-power load?"', cost: 'Can you apply it to something you have not been taught?' },
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="tip" title="The question behind the question">
  Examiners rarely want the fact they asked for. They want to see whether you can get to it.

  *"Why did you use a true-RMS meter?"* is not really about your meter. It is asking whether
  you know that an average-responding meter assumes a sinusoid, and whether you understand
  when that assumption breaks.

  So answer the question, then add the reasoning in one sentence. That single extra sentence
  is usually what distinguishes a confident answer from a memorised one.
</Callout>

### Answering well

- **Answer the question asked.** Not the adjacent one you revised.
- **Say "I don't know" when you don't.** Then reason aloud toward it. An examiner would much
  rather watch you think than listen to a confident fabrication — and they will always
  detect the fabrication.
- **Use your own data.** "In my measurements the capacitive case gave negative regulation,
  which is what the sign of the reactive term predicts" is far stronger than a general
  statement.
- **Own the anomalies.** If a result was odd, say so and offer your best explanation. Trying
  to hide it invites exactly the questions you least want.
- **Draw.** Ask for paper. A phasor diagram or a sketch of a curve answers more clearly than
  a paragraph, and it shows you are thinking in the right representation.

<Hazard title="Never present data you did not measure" level="caution">
  Fabricated or adjusted results are academic misconduct, and the consequences run from a
  zero to expulsion.

  They are also easier to detect than students imagine. Real measurements have
  characteristic scatter; invented ones are too smooth, too linear, or agree with theory
  more closely than the instruments could support. Markers who have seen hundreds of these
  recognise the pattern quickly.

  A result that disagrees with theory, reported honestly and explained thoughtfully, scores
  better than a fabricated one that agrees — and it is the only version you can defend when
  someone asks how you got it.
</Hazard>

## How marks are usually distributed

Weightings vary, but the shape is consistent:

    { option: 'Viva', when: '15–20%', cost: 'Being unable to explain your own numbers' },
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="tip" title="Two of these are nearly free">
  **Pre-lab preparation** and the **record book** together are around a third of the mark,
  and neither requires cleverness — only turning up prepared and writing carefully.

  Students routinely concentrate on the calculations, which are the part most likely to be
  right anyway, and neglect the two components that are entirely within their control.
</Callout>

<KeyIdea>
  Record **raw** readings in ink, at the bench, signed before you leave — everything derived
  can be recomputed, nothing raw can be recovered. Then put your effort into the
  **discussion**, because "errors were due to human error" is the sentence every marker has
  read a thousand times, and quantifying a disagreement and testing your explanation of it
  is what the marks are actually for.
</KeyIdea>
