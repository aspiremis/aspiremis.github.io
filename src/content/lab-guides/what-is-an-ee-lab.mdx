Every lab manual starts at the experiment. None of them explain what a laboratory session
*is* — so if you have never been in one, you arrive already behind.

This page is the part nobody writes down.

## What a laboratory is for

It is not for confirming what the lecture said. That is a common misreading, and it leads
students to treat a disagreeing measurement as a mistake to be hidden rather than a result
to be explained.

A laboratory exists to teach three things that cannot be taught on paper:

**That the model is an approximation.** Your transmission line has resistance the textbook
example ignored, your meter has a burden voltage, your supply sags under load. Learning
*where* theory and hardware part company is most of what an engineer knows that a
physicist does not.

**Manual competence.** Wiring a three-phase circuit correctly, recognising a CT connected
backwards, knowing what a bearing that is about to fail sounds like. None of this transfers
from reading.

**Judgement about measurement.** How much to trust a number, when a reading is telling you
something and when it is telling you your instrument is wrong.

<Callout type="engineering">
  A disagreement between measurement and theory is a **result**, not a failure.

  The instinct — nearly universal, and worth killing early — is to nudge the numbers toward
  what the formula predicted. Do not. The gap is where the learning is, and a report that
  explains a 12% discrepancy honestly is worth far more than one that reports 0.5% because
  the numbers were helped.

  Examiners have seen a great deal of adjusted data. They are much better at recognising it
  than students expect, and it is the one thing that turns a viva hostile.
</Callout>

You will meet several over your M.Tech. They differ in what they are trying to teach and in

<Hazard title="The open-circuited CT is the one that surprises people">
  A current transformer whose secondary is left open-circuited while primary current flows
  develops an extremely high voltage across the open terminals — potentially several
  kilovolts from a device sitting on a bench in a protection lab.

  The reason is that a CT is a current source. With the secondary shorted through a meter
  or relay burden, the secondary ampere-turns oppose the primary and the core flux stays
  low. Open the secondary and nothing opposes the primary magnetomotive force, the core
  saturates hard, and the enormous rate of change of flux at each zero crossing induces a
  large voltage spike.

  **Never open a CT secondary while the primary is energised.** Short it first. This is why
  CT terminal blocks have shorting links, and it is the most common way people are hurt in
  a protection laboratory.
</Hazard>

## How a session runs

The shape is broadly the same everywhere.

<Steps title="A typical three-hour session">
  <Step n={1} title="Before you arrive — the pre-lab" expect="you already know what the meters should read, and roughly why">
    Read the experiment. Work through the theory. Predict the result.

    This is the part that determines whether the session is useful. Arriving without having
    read the experiment means spending the session copying the procedure and understanding
    nothing — and it is the single most common way students waste a laboratory.

    That is exactly what these Academy pages are for.
  </Step>

  <Step n={2} title="Arrival and briefing">
    The demonstrator explains the experiment, points out the hazards, and often asks
    questions to check you have prepared. This is an informal viva, and it counts.
  </Step>

  <Step n={3} title="Wiring" hazard="Do not energise until your demonstrator has checked the circuit.">
    Build the circuit from the diagram. Expect this to take longer than you think the first
    few times — half the session is normal at the start.

    Work systematically: one loop at a time, tracing the diagram with a finger.
  </Step>

  <Step n={4} title="Checking" expect="a demonstrator's signature or verbal approval before any supply is applied">
    Someone experienced looks at your circuit before it is energised. Every time, including
    when you are confident.
  </Step>

  <Step n={5} title="Measurement" expect="raw readings written down as you go, in ink">
    Take readings in a planned order, record them immediately, and record the raw values
    rather than something you calculated in your head.

    Do a rough sanity check as you go. Noticing that a value is impossible *while the bench
    is still wired* saves the entire session.
  </Step>

  <Step n={6} title="Shut down and sign off">
    Load to minimum, variac to zero, isolate, discharge, verify. Then get your raw
    observations **signed by the demonstrator before you leave the room**.
  </Step>

  <Step n={7} title="After — calculations, plots, report">
    Written up in your own time, submitted at the next session. The viva follows.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="tip" title="Sanity-check at the bench, not at home">
  The most valuable five minutes of any session is the five spent looking at your numbers
  before you dismantle the circuit.

  A voltage that rose when it should have fallen, a power factor above 1, a current that did
  not change when you changed the load — every one of these is trivially fixable while the
  bench is still wired, and unfixable a week later when you are writing up.

  Ask of each number: *is this even possible?*
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="A variac does not isolate">
  This catches people constantly.

  An autotransformer has **one** winding with a sliding tap, so the output is
  electrically continuous with the input. Turning it to zero does not disconnect you from
  the mains, and the output neutral is the supply neutral.

  A two-winding **isolation transformer** is what provides galvanic separation. If your
  bench has both, they are doing different jobs and neither substitutes for the other.
</Callout>

## What is expected of you

- **Prepare before you arrive.** Everything else follows from this.
- **Ask when you are unsure.** Nobody thinks less of a student who asks which terminal is
  which. They think a great deal less of one who guesses and lets the smoke out.
- **Record honestly.** Including the readings that look wrong. Especially those.
- **Do not touch what is not yours.** Another group may be energised and measuring.
- **Leave the bench as you found it.** The next group inherits your mess or your care.

<Callout type="engineering">
  A note on demonstrators, because students routinely under-use them.

  They are usually PhD students who have run this experiment dozens of times and know
  exactly which terminal on the panel is unreliable and which meter drifts. That knowledge
  is not written anywhere and is genuinely valuable.

  Ask them *why*, not just *how*. "Why is the CT connected that way round?" gets you a real
  explanation and, incidentally, marks you as someone who prepared.
</Callout>
