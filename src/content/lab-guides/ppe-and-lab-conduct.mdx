Personal protective equipment is the **last** line of defence, not the first. If PPE is
what stands between you and injury, several earlier controls have already failed.

The order of controls, strongest first: eliminate the hazard, substitute something safer,
engineer a barrier, use procedure, and only then PPE. A laboratory that relies on gloves
where it should have used an isolator has designed its safety backwards.

That said — the earlier controls do fail, and this is what you wear when they do.

<Hazard title="Remove your rings before you enter">
  A metal ring across two terminals is a near-perfect short circuit — very low resistance,
  and welded to your finger.

  The current is enormous, the ring reaches red heat in under a second, and it cannot be
  removed because your finger swells immediately. Ring injuries in electrical work are
  characteristically severe and frequently end in amputation.

  This applies to watches, metal-strapped fitness bands, bracelets and necklaces. Take them
  off before you enter, not when you remember.
</Hazard>

<Callout type="engineering">
  The synthetic fabric rule is not fussiness about dress code.

  Cotton exposed to an arc **chars** — it burns, but it stays on you as a layer and can be
  cut away afterwards. Polyester **melts** at around 250 °C, well below arc temperatures,
  and the molten polymer adheres to skin and continues transferring heat after the arc has
  gone.

  Burn units treat these as two different classes of injury. It is the single cheapest
  safety decision you will make: wear cotton.
</Callout>

### On rubber gloves

You will see insulating gloves in photographs of electrical work. In a teaching laboratory
they are usually **not** the right answer, and it is worth understanding why.

Insulating gloves are rated by class, must be inspected and air-tested before every use,
require leather overgloves to protect them from puncture, and must be within their test
interval — typically six months. A glove that has been in a drawer for two years, that
nobody has tested, gives you confidence without protection, which is worse than no glove at
all.

The correct control for a teaching bench is **isolation** — prove it dead and work on
something that cannot hurt you. Gloves are for live working, and you should not be doing
live working.

## The behaviours that cause incidents

Very few laboratory injuries come from someone not knowing a rule. Almost all come from
knowing it and skipping it, usually under time pressure.

<Callout type="warning" title="Time pressure is the common factor">
  Look down that list: most of it is what a careful person does when the session is ending
  and the work is not finished.

  The correct response is to **stop and leave it unfinished**. An incomplete experiment
  costs you a repeat session. A rushed one can cost considerably more, and no demonstrator
  has ever been angry with a student for running out of time safely.
</Callout>

## Laboratory conduct

The rules that are not about electricity:

- **No food or drink.** A spilled drink on an energised bench is a conductive path across
  everything. It is also how equipment dies.
- **Bags and coats away from the bench.** Trip hazards, and they get caught on leads.
- **One group per bench.** Crowding means someone is working on something they cannot see.
- **Do not touch another group's setup.** They may have it energised and be measuring.
- **Report damage immediately** — a cracked lead, a loose terminal, a missing cover. Do not
  quietly swap it for a good one and leave the broken one in the rack for the next group.
- **Leave the bench as you found it.** Leads coiled, load banks at minimum, variac at zero,
  supply off.

<Callout type="tip" title="The variac-at-zero habit">
  Always return the variac to zero before switching off, and always confirm it is at zero
  before switching on.

  The failure it prevents: the next person energises the bench expecting nothing to happen
  and gets full voltage instantly, into a circuit they have not finished wiring. It costs
  you two seconds. Do it every time, including the times you are certain you already did.
</Callout>

## Emergencies

### Electric shock

Covered fully in [the hazards page](/lab/foundations/electrical-hazards), and worth
repeating because the first action is counter-intuitive:

**Do not touch them. Cut the supply first.** Then call for help, then check breathing, then
CPR if you are trained. Medical attention afterwards regardless of how fine they seem —
arrhythmias can develop hours later.

### Electrical fire

<Steps title="In order">
  <Step n={1} title="Cut the supply if it is safe to reach the isolator" expect="the fire may stop being an electrical fire once de-energised">
    An energised fire is a different problem from an ordinary one. Removing the supply
    removes both the ignition source and the shock hazard to anyone fighting it.
  </Step>

  <Step n={2} title="Raise the alarm" hazard="Do not spend time fighting a fire before anyone else knows about it.">
    Alarm first, then decide whether to fight it.
  </Step>

  <Step n={3} title="Use CO₂ or dry powder — NEVER water" hazard="Water conducts. A water extinguisher on an energised fire puts you in the circuit.">
    CO₂ is preferred on electrical equipment: it displaces oxygen, leaves no residue, and
    does not damage what it is not burning.
  </Step>

  <Step n={4} title="If it is not immediately controllable, leave">
    Burning insulation produces genuinely toxic smoke — PVC releases hydrogen chloride.
    Do not stay in the room to keep trying.
  </Step>
</Steps>

### Burns

Cool with running water for at least twenty minutes. Do not apply ice, creams or anything
else. Do not remove clothing stuck to the burn.

**Electrical burns are deceptive**: the visible entry and exit wounds can be small while
the tissue damage along the current path between them is extensive. Any electrical burn
needs medical assessment regardless of how it looks.

### Injury from machinery

Experiment 2 uses an induction motor with a rotating shaft. Stop the machine first — the
emergency stop, not the supply switch — before attempting anything else.

<Callout type="tip" title="Point at a specific person">
  "Someone call for help" produces a room full of people each assuming someone else is
  going.

  Point at one individual and give them a specific instruction: *"You — get the
  demonstrator, now."* This is standard first-aid practice for exactly this reason, and it
  works.
</Callout>

## Before every session

<Checklist
  id="session-start"
  title="Session start"
  tone="safety"
  items={[

    'Cotton clothing, sleeves down, closed shoes',
    'Long hair tied back — Experiment 2 has a rotating shaft',
    'Bag and coat stowed away from the bench',
    'Emergency stop located and reachable from where I will stand',
    'Fire extinguisher located, and it is CO₂ or dry powder',
    'I am not working alone',
    'Bench inspected: no cracked leads, no loose terminals, no missing covers',
    'Variac at zero, load bank at minimum, all capacitor steps off',
  ]}
/>

<Checklist
  id="session-end"
  title="Session end"
  tone="safety"
  items={[
    'Load reduced to minimum, variac returned to zero',
    'Supply isolated',
    'Capacitors discharged, and verified with a meter rather than assumed',
    'Leads disconnected and coiled',
    'Any damaged equipment reported, not quietly returned to the rack',
    'Observations recorded and signed by the demonstrator before I leave',
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="warning" title="Get your observations signed before you leave">
  Not a safety point, but it belongs on the same checklist because it has the same failure
  mode — remembering when it is too late.

  Most laboratories require the demonstrator to sign your raw observations at the bench.
  Unsigned readings can be rejected, and you cannot recreate them afterwards.
</Callout>

<KeyIdea>
  PPE is the **last** control, not the first — if it is what saves you, isolation already
  failed. Remove metal jewellery, wear cotton, and recognise that nearly every laboratory
  incident happens to someone who knew the rule and skipped it because the session was
  ending. Stopping with the work unfinished is always the right call.
</KeyIdea>
