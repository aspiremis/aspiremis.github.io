Three conductors in your laboratory end up connected to the ground, and they do completely
different jobs. Confusing them is how people get hurt by circuits they believed were safe.

## Earth, neutral and bonding

<Hazard title="The neutral is a live conductor">
  This is the single most dangerous misconception in electrical work.

  The neutral is bonded to earth **at the supply transformer**, not at your bench. By the
  time it reaches you it has carried load current through the impedance of the whole return
  path, so it sits at some voltage above local earth. On an unbalanced three-phase system
  with a broken neutral, it can sit at close to **phase voltage**.

  Treat the neutral exactly as you treat a phase conductor: prove it dead before touching
  it. "It's only the neutral" has killed people.
</Hazard>

### Why earthing exists at all

The purpose of a protective earth is not, as most people assume, to "drain electricity
away". It is to **guarantee that a fault produces enough current to operate the
protection**.

Consider a motor whose winding insulation fails and energises its metal frame.

- **Without an earth connection**, the frame sits at phase voltage. Nothing operates,
  because no current flows. The machine appears entirely normal and functions perfectly —
  until someone touches it, at which point *they* become the fault path.
- **With a low-impedance earth connection**, the fault current is large — hundreds of
  amperes — and the fuse or MCB clears it in milliseconds. The frame is dead before anyone
  arrives.

<Callout type="engineering">
  The consequence is that **a high-resistance earth is worse than useless**, and this is
  genuinely counter-intuitive.

  If the earth path has, say, 100 Ω of resistance, then a phase-to-frame fault drives about
  2.3 A. That is not enough to operate a 16 A MCB — so the fault persists indefinitely —
  and meanwhile the frame sits at a substantial voltage above true earth, with a live path
  now permanently connected to it.

  You have created exactly the hazard the earth was supposed to remove, and disguised it.
  This is why earth continuity is tested rather than assumed, and why loop impedance
  matters as much as the presence of a wire.
</Callout>

<Analogy>
  Earthing is a **fire alarm**, not a fire extinguisher.

  Its job is not to make the fault harmless. Its job is to make the fault **loud enough
  that the protection notices** and disconnects. A quiet fault is the dangerous one.
</Analogy>

## Safe isolation

Isolation is not "I turned it off". It is a procedure with a defined end state: the circuit
is disconnected, it cannot be reconnected without your knowledge, and you have **proved**
it is dead.

<Steps title="The isolation procedure">
  <Step n={1} title="Identify" expect="certainty about which isolator feeds your bench, not a reasonable guess">
    Establish exactly which circuit you are isolating and which device isolates it. Trace
    it if you are not certain.

    Mislabelled distribution boards are common. The label is a hypothesis; the test proves
    it.
  </Step>

  <Step n={2} title="Isolate" hazard="Switching off at the equipment is not isolation. Use the isolator, not the on/off switch.">
    Open the isolator or withdraw the fuse. An equipment switch may break only one pole and
    leaves the circuit connected.
  </Step>

  <Step n={3} title="Secure" expect="a physical barrier to someone else re-energising it">
    Lock off if a lock is available. Tag it with your name, the date, and why. If neither
    is possible, do not leave the isolator unattended.
  </Step>

  <Step n={4} title="Prove dead — three steps, not one" hazard="A meter that reads zero because it is broken is indistinguishable from a dead circuit.">
    This is the step people shorten, and it is the one that matters:

    1. Test the meter on a **known live source** — a proving unit or a socket you know is live.
    2. Test the circuit — **all** conductors, including the neutral, and every combination:
       phase to phase, phase to neutral, phase to earth, neutral to earth.
    3. Test the meter on the **known live source again**, to confirm it did not fail between
       the first two steps.

    Skipping step 3 is the classic error. A meter with a failing lead can pass step 1, fail
    silently, and read zero on a live circuit in step 2.
  </Step>

  <Step n={5} title="Discharge stored energy" hazard="Capacitors hold charge after isolation. Measure, do not assume.">
    Wait the stated time, then measure across every capacitor bank. An extinguished
    indicator lamp is not proof of discharge.
  </Step>

  <Step n={6} title="Only now, work">
    And if you leave the area and return, prove it dead again. You do not know what happened
    while you were gone.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="warning" title="The three-step prove is not bureaucracy">
  It exists because meters fail in a way that looks exactly like a safe result.

  A flat battery, a broken lead, a blown internal fuse, the wrong function selected — every
  one of these produces a reading of zero on a live circuit, and every one of them looks
  identical to success.

  A zero reading is only evidence when you have independently established that the meter
  can read non-zero, immediately before and immediately after.
</Callout>

## Lock-out / tag-out

LOTO exists because of one specific accident that keeps happening: **someone else turns it
back on.**

A colleague finds an isolator open, sees no reason for it, assumes it was left that way by
mistake, and closes it. Meanwhile you have your hands inside the equipment.

<Compare
  headings={['Control', 'What it does', 'Strength']}
  rows={[
    { option: 'Lock', when: 'Physically prevents operation', cost: 'Strong — the isolator cannot be closed', recommended: true },
    { option: 'Tag', when: 'Informs whoever finds it', cost: 'Weak — relies on the person reading and obeying it' },
    { option: 'Both', when: 'Prevents AND explains', cost: 'The standard practice' },
    { option: 'Neither', when: 'Nothing', cost: 'Only acceptable if the isolator stays in your sight' },
  ]}
/>

The principles, in the order they matter:

- **One lock per person.** Everyone working on the equipment applies their own lock. It
  stays locked until the last person removes theirs.
- **Only you remove your lock.** Not your supervisor, not the person who needs the machine.
  If a lock must be cut, that is a formal process with authorisation, not a decision made
  in the moment.
- **The tag says who and why.** A tag reading "DO NOT OPERATE" with no name is nearly
  useless — nobody knows who to ask.

<Callout type="engineering">
  In a teaching laboratory you may not have padlocks, and the honest substitute is
  **procedural**: tell your demonstrator what you have isolated, tag it with your name, and
  do not leave the isolator out of your sight while you are working.

  In industry this is not optional. Every serious utility and plant operates a permit-to-work
  system, where isolation is documented, the isolator is locked, and a written permit
  transfers responsibility for the equipment to the person working on it. You will meet this
  the first day of any internship, and the reasoning is exactly what is on this page.
</Callout>

## Earthing systems you will meet

The letters describe how the supply and the installation are earthed. You will see them on
drawings and in the wiring regulations.

<Compare
  headings={['System', 'Meaning', 'Where']}
  rows={[
    { option: 'TN-S', when: 'Separate neutral and earth conductors throughout', cost: 'Common in older UK-style installations; cleanest for measurement' },
    { option: 'TN-C-S (PME)', when: 'Combined then separated at the origin', cost: 'Very common; the earth is derived from the supply neutral' },
    { option: 'TT', when: 'Installation has its own local earth electrode', cost: 'Rural supplies; relies on an RCD because loop impedance is high' },
    { option: 'IT', when: 'Supply is unearthed or earthed via high impedance', cost: 'Hospitals, some industrial processes — a first fault does not disconnect supply' },
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="tip" title="IT systems are the interesting one">
  In an IT system the supply is deliberately not earthed, so a **single** phase-to-earth
  fault causes almost no current and the supply keeps running. An insulation monitoring
  device raises an alarm instead.

  The reason is continuity of supply where an unexpected disconnection is itself dangerous
  — an operating theatre, a continuous process plant. The trade is that you must find and
  fix the first fault before a second one arrives, because two faults on different phases
  becomes a short circuit with no protection having yet operated.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout type="warning" title="Treating the neutral as safe">
  It is a live conductor. Prove it dead like any other.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Switching off at the equipment instead of isolating">
  An equipment switch may break one pole and is not designed for isolation duty.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Trusting the label on the distribution board">
  Labels are frequently wrong after years of modifications. Prove it.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Proving dead with an untested meter">
  Test on a known source before and after. A broken meter reads zero.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Assuming discharge resistors worked">
  They fail open silently. Measure across the terminals.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Leaving an isolator open and unattended without a tag">
  Someone will close it, reasonably, because nothing told them not to.
</Callout>

<KeyIdea>
  Earthing does not make a fault safe — it makes it **large enough for the protection to
  operate**, which is why a high-resistance earth is worse than none. The neutral is a live
  conductor. And a circuit is dead only when you have proved your meter works, tested every
  conductor, and proved your meter works again.
</KeyIdea>
