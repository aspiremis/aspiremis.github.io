The nameplate is the manufacturer's contract with you. It says: operate it this way and it
will last twenty years; operate it otherwise and you are on your own.

Almost every experiment in your PSAO lab needs a number from one, and every one of them
begins with "record the nameplate values". Here is what those numbers mean.

## A three-phase induction motor plate

Experiment 2 uses one. A typical plate:

    { label: 'Insulation class', value: 'F' },
    { label: 'Temperature rise', value: 'B' },

    { label: 'Ambient', value: '40 °C' },

/>

Several of these are commonly misread.

<Callout type="warning" title="Rated output is mechanical, not electrical">
  3.7 kW is what appears **at the shaft**. The electrical input is larger, by the
  efficiency:

  Input = 3.7 / 0.88 ≈ **4.2 kW**

  And the apparent power is larger again, by the power factor:

  S = 4.2 / 0.85 ≈ **4.9 kVA**

  Check that against the plate: √3 × 415 × 7.5 = 5.4 kVA. Close, and the difference tells
  you the quoted power factor and efficiency are the full-load figures rounded.

  Students routinely size a supply from the kW figure and find it inadequate. The number to
  size from is the **current**.
</Callout>

<Callout type="engineering">
  **Speed tells you slip, and slip tells you everything.**

  Synchronous speed for a 50 Hz four-pole machine is:

  N_s = 120f / P = 120 × 50 / 4 = 1500 rpm

  The plate says 1440 rpm at full load, so:

  s = (1500 − 1440) / 1500 = 0.04, i.e. **4% slip**

  From slip you get rotor frequency (2 Hz), rotor copper loss (4% of air-gap power), and
  the operating point on the torque-slip curve. A single number on a plate carries all of
  that — which is why "record the nameplate" is not busywork.
</Callout>

## Insulation class and temperature rise

Two separate letters that get confused constantly.

**Insulation class** is what the insulation can withstand. **Temperature rise** is what the
machine is designed to actually reach.

<Compare
  headings={['Class', 'Maximum hot-spot temperature', 'Material']}
  rows={[
    { option: 'A', when: '105 °C', cost: 'Cotton, paper, natural fibres — obsolete' },
    { option: 'E', when: '120 °C', cost: 'Enamelled wire' },
    { option: 'B', when: '130 °C', cost: 'Mica, glass fibre with organic binder' },
    { option: 'F', when: '155 °C', cost: 'Mica and glass with synthetic binder — the modern standard', recommended: true },
    { option: 'H', when: '180 °C', cost: 'Silicone, imide films' },
  ]}
/>

A plate reading **class F insulation, class B rise** means the insulation tolerates 155 °C
but the machine is designed to reach only 130 °C at rated load.

<Callout type="engineering">
  That 25 °C gap is deliberate, and it is worth understanding what it buys.

  Insulation ageing follows roughly the **Montsinger rule**: every 10 °C of extra
  temperature halves the insulation life. So a class F machine designed for class B rise is
  running about 25 °C cooler than its insulation can take — worth roughly a factor of five
  in expected life.

  It also gives you headroom. That margin is what absorbs a hot day, a slightly high
  ambient, or an overload. A machine specified with no margin has none of that.

  The same rule is why transformer loading guides let you overload deliberately for short
  periods: thermal mass means the winding takes time to heat, so a two-hour overload does
  not consume the life a permanent one would.
</Callout>

## Duty type

The **S** number states how the machine is expected to be used, and it changes the rating
substantially.

<Compare
  headings={['Duty', 'Meaning', 'Consequence']}
  rows={[
    { option: 'S1', when: 'Continuous — runs at rated load indefinitely', cost: 'Reaches thermal equilibrium; the usual case', recommended: true },
    { option: 'S2', when: 'Short-time — runs for a stated period from cold', cost: 'Higher rating allowed, because it never reaches equilibrium' },
    { option: 'S3', when: 'Intermittent periodic, with a stated duty cycle', cost: 'Rating depends on the cyclic duration factor' },
    { option: 'S4', when: 'Intermittent with significant starting', cost: 'Starting losses dominate; frequent starts limit the rating' },
    { option: 'S6', when: 'Continuous operation with intermittent load', cost: 'Never switched off, but loaded cyclically' },
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="warning" title="A duty rating is not interchangeable">
  A motor rated **S2 30 min** at 5 kW cannot deliver 5 kW continuously. It is allowed the
  higher figure precisely *because* it will be switched off before it reaches thermal
  equilibrium.

  Run it continuously at its S2 rating and the winding keeps heating past the point the
  design allowed for. It will not trip. It will simply age fast and fail early, and the
  failure will look like a manufacturing defect rather than a misapplication.
</Callout>

## IP rating

Two digits, each answering a different question.

**First digit — solids:**

| | |
|---|---|
| 0 | No protection |
| 2 | Fingers (>12 mm) |
| 4 | Wires, small tools (>1 mm) |
| 5 | Dust protected — some ingress, but not enough to interfere |
| 6 | Dust tight |

**Second digit — liquids:**

| | |
|---|---|
| 0 | No protection |
| 3 | Spraying water up to 60° from vertical |
| 4 | Splashing from any direction |
| 5 | Water jets |
| 6 | Powerful water jets |
| 7 | Temporary immersion |

So **IP55** is dust protected and resistant to water jets — the standard for an industrial
motor. **IP20** is finger-safe and offers nothing against water, which is why an IP20 panel
belongs indoors in a dry room.

## Transformer nameplates

Experiments 1 and 3 use line models, and the trainer will carry a plate. The fields differ
from a machine's:

<Spec
  title="Typical transformer nameplate fields"
  rows={[
    { label: 'Rated power', value: 'kVA or MVA', note: 'APPARENT power — a transformer is limited by current and voltage, not by power factor' },
    { label: 'Voltage ratio', value: '11000 / 415 V', note: 'No-load ratio; on-load output is lower by the regulation' },
    { label: 'Vector group', value: 'Dyn11', note: 'Determines paralleling compatibility' },
    { label: 'Impedance voltage', value: '5%', note: 'The single most consequential number — see below' },
    { label: 'Tap range', value: '±5% in 2.5% steps' },
    { label: 'Cooling', value: 'ONAN', note: 'Oil Natural, Air Natural' },
    { label: 'Type of duty', value: 'Continuous' },
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="engineering">
  **Percentage impedance is the field to understand.**

  It is the fraction of rated voltage that, applied to one winding with the other
  short-circuited, drives rated current. A 5% impedance means 5% of rated voltage produces
  100% of rated current.

  Two consequences follow immediately.

  **Fault current.** With the source assumed stiff, the short-circuit current is roughly
  rated current divided by the per-unit impedance — so 5% impedance gives about **20×**
  rated current into a terminal fault. That number sizes the switchgear.

  **Voltage regulation.** A low-impedance transformer has better regulation but higher fault
  current; a high-impedance one has worse regulation but limits faults. The choice is a
  genuine engineering trade-off, and it is why you cannot simply specify "as low as
  possible".
</Callout>

<Callout type="tip" title="Rated power in kVA, not kW — and this is deliberate">
  A transformer's limits are **thermal**, set by winding current (copper loss) and core flux
  (iron loss). Neither depends on the load's power factor.

  A 100 kVA transformer delivers 100 kVA whether the load is at unity or at 0.5 lagging. At
  unity that is 100 kW; at 0.5 it is 50 kW. The transformer does not care — the same current
  flows and the same heat is produced.

  Motors are rated in kW because their limit is what the shaft can deliver. Transformers are
  rated in kVA because theirs is what the winding can carry.
</Callout>

## A rating is thermal, not a cut-off

The most useful thing to understand about any rating: it is a statement about **heat over
time**, not a switch.

A motor at 110% load does not stop. It runs, produces slightly more torque, and heats
faster than its cooling can remove — so the winding temperature climbs past the design
value and insulation life shortens. Nothing announces this. The machine simply fails
earlier than it should have.

This is why thermal overload relays have **inverse time** characteristics: a small overload
is tolerated for a long time, a large one for a short time, mirroring the actual heating.
It is exactly the logic behind Experiment 2's overcurrent protection.

<Analogy>
  A rating is a speed limit, not a wall.

  Exceeding it does not stop you. It raises the probability and the consequence of failure,
  progressively and invisibly, and the bill arrives much later.
</Analogy>

<Callout type="tip" title="Compute the derived values at the bench">
  Synchronous speed, slip, rated kVA, base impedance for per unit — work these out while you
  are still standing at the machine.

  If one of them is impossible, you have misread the plate, and you can look again. A week
  later you cannot.
</Callout>

<KeyIdea>
  A nameplate rating is a **thermal statement**, not a cut-off — exceeding it shortens life
  invisibly rather than stopping anything. Motor kW is shaft power, so size supplies from
  the current; transformer kVA is apparent power because heat does not care about power
  factor; and percentage impedance sets both the regulation and the fault current.
</KeyIdea>
