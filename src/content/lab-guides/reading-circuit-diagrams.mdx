The first page of every experiment is a diagram, and it is usually a different *kind* of
diagram from the one you need to wire the circuit.

Knowing which is which saves a lot of confusion.

## Three diagrams, three questions

<Analogy>
  Think of describing a journey.

  The **single line diagram** is the underground map: topologically correct, geographically
  a lie, and exactly what you want for understanding where you can get to.

  The **schematic** is a road map: relationships accurate, scale approximate.

  The **wiring diagram** is turn-by-turn directions: useless for understanding the city,
  perfect for actually driving.

  Your experiment sheet gives you the first two. To wire the bench you have to construct the
  third yourself, from the panel in front of you — which is why the Figure placeholders on
  the experiment pages ask you to draw the SLD with *your* panel's terminal numbers on it.
</Analogy>

## The single line diagram

Power systems are three-phase and mostly balanced, so drawing three identical lines
everywhere adds nothing. The SLD collapses them to one.

The core symbols:

<Callout type="engineering">
  **A circuit breaker and an isolator are not interchangeable, and confusing them is
  dangerous.**

  A *circuit breaker* is designed to make and break **load and fault current**. It has arc
  chutes, a fast mechanism, and an interrupting rating.

  An *isolator* (disconnector) provides a visible, verifiable air gap for safety. It is
  **not** designed to break current at all. Opening one under load draws an arc it cannot
  extinguish.

  The operating sequence is therefore fixed: **open the breaker first, then the isolator.**
  Closing runs the other way: isolator first, then breaker. Substations interlock this
  mechanically because getting it wrong destroys equipment and injures people.
</Callout>

## IEC and ANSI disagree

You will meet both. Indian practice follows **IEC 60617**; much American literature and a
lot of software uses **ANSI/IEEE**.

<Callout type="tip" title="The resistor is the giveaway">
  Rectangle means you are reading an IEC drawing. Zigzag means ANSI.

  Establish which convention a drawing uses before you read anything else on it — because
  several symbols are similar enough between the two that a misread is possible, and
  drawings very rarely say which standard they follow.
</Callout>

## ANSI device numbers

Protection drawings label devices with numbers rather than names, from ANSI/IEEE C37.2. The
convention is universal and you will be expected to recognise the common ones — including
in Experiment 4.

<Deck title="The ones you need for PSAO">
  <Flashcard front="27" tag="ANSI device">
    **Undervoltage relay.** Operates when voltage falls below a set threshold. Experiment
    4(b).
  </Flashcard>

  <Flashcard front="59" tag="ANSI device">
    **Overvoltage relay.** Operates above a set threshold. The other half of Experiment
    4(b), and increasingly important on feeders with distributed solar.
  </Flashcard>

  <Flashcard front="81" tag="ANSI device">
    **Frequency relay** — 81U under, 81O over. Experiment 4(a). Underfrequency load shedding
    is the last automatic defence against a system-wide collapse.
  </Flashcard>

  <Flashcard front="50 and 51" tag="ANSI device">
    **50** is instantaneous overcurrent, **51** is time-delayed (inverse-time) overcurrent.
    Together they are the backbone of Experiment 2. The suffix N — 50N, 51N — means earth
    fault.
  </Flashcard>

  <Flashcard front="87" tag="ANSI device">
    **Differential protection.** Compares current in and current out; any difference means
    a fault inside the protected zone. Used on transformers, generators and busbars.
  </Flashcard>

  <Flashcard front="21" tag="ANSI device">
    **Distance relay.** Measures impedance to infer how far away a fault is. The subject of
    a MATLAB Hub lesson, and the standard protection for transmission lines.
  </Flashcard>
</Deck>

## Transformer vector groups

Beside every transformer on an SLD sits something like **Dyn11**. It is not decoration.

- **D** — primary delta connected (uppercase = HV winding)
- **y** — secondary star/wye connected (lowercase = LV winding)
- **n** — secondary neutral is brought out
- **11** — the LV phasor lags the HV by 11 × 30° = 330°, i.e. leads by 30°

<Callout type="engineering">
  The clock number is what determines whether two transformers can be **paralleled**.

  Connect a Dyn11 and a Dyn1 in parallel and there is a 60° phase difference between their
  secondaries. That difference appears across the interconnection and drives an enormous
  circulating current — a short circuit, in effect, through two healthy transformers.

  Vector group is checked before paralleling, every time. It is also why the number appears
  on the nameplate and on the drawing rather than being left to be worked out.
</Callout>

## From diagram to wired bench

The gap between "I understand the circuit" and "I have wired it" is where sessions get
lost. A method that works:

<Steps title="Wiring systematically">
  <Step n={1} title="Identify the main power path first" expect="a single traceable route from supply to load">
    Supply → protection → variac → line model → load. Get that loop complete before
    anything else.
  </Step>

  <Step n={2} title="Add the return path">
    Neutral or the other phases. On three-phase, complete all three before moving on.
  </Step>

  <Step n={3} title="Insert the series instruments" hazard="An ammeter goes in series. Connected in parallel it is a short circuit.">
    Ammeters and current coils break into the path. This means physically opening a
    connection and inserting the meter.
  </Step>

  <Step n={4} title="Add the parallel instruments" expect="voltmeters across, not in line">
    Voltmeters and voltage coils go across the points whose difference you want.
  </Step>

  <Step n={5} title="Trace the whole circuit against the diagram with a finger" expect="every line on the diagram accounted for by a physical lead">
    Out loud if you can. Say what each lead does as you touch it.

    This catches more errors than any other single habit, because it forces you to look at
    what is actually there rather than what you intended to build.
  </Step>

  <Step n={6} title="Have it checked, then energise">
    Every time.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Callout type="tip" title="Colour your leads by function">
  Red or brown for phase, black or blue for neutral, green-yellow for earth, and a distinct
  colour for measurement connections.

  On a bench with twenty identical leads this is the difference between a circuit you can
  debug in thirty seconds and one you have to dismantle. It also matches installation
  practice, so the habit transfers.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Draw the wiring diagram yourself">
  The experiment sheet gives you a schematic. Your panel has terminal numbers.

  Spend five minutes redrawing the schematic with **your panel's actual terminal numbers**
  written on it, before you touch a lead. You will wire it faster, you will debug it faster,
  and you have just produced the figure your report needs.
</Callout>

<KeyIdea>
  A single line diagram answers *how does power flow*; a wiring diagram answers *which
  terminal goes where*. Your experiment sheet gives you the first, so **draw the second
  yourself with your panel's terminal numbers** before touching a lead — then trace every
  connection against the diagram out loud before energising.
</KeyIdea>
