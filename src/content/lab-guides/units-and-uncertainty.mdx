Two ideas that every experiment in your lab depends on, and that most students carry
around half-formed: the per-unit system, and how many of the digits on a display are real.

## Per unit

Power systems have transformers in them. Which means the same power flows at 11 kV on one
side and 415 V on the other, with currents differing by the same ratio, and impedances by
its square.

Track all of that in actual units and every calculation carries a transformer ratio.
Per unit removes them.

Choose a base for two quantities — conventionally apparent power and voltage — and every
other base follows:

$$
S_{\text{base}}, \quad V_{\text{base}} \quad \Rightarrow \quad
I_{\text{base}} = \frac{S_{\text{base}}}{\sqrt{3}\,V_{\text{base}}}, \quad
Z_{\text{base}} = \frac{V_{\text{base}}^2}{S_{\text{base}}}
$$

using line-to-line voltage and three-phase power, which is the standard convention.

Then any quantity in per unit is itself divided by its base:

$$
V_{\text{pu}} = \frac{V_{\text{actual}}}{V_{\text{base}}}, \qquad
Z_{\text{pu}} = \frac{Z_{\text{actual}}}{Z_{\text{base}}}
$$

<Callout type="engineering">
  **The payoff: choose the voltage bases in the ratio of the transformer turns, and the
  transformer disappears from the diagram.**

  A transformer's per-unit impedance is the same seen from either side. So a network with
  five voltage levels becomes a single circuit with no ratios in it — which is why every
  load flow, every fault study and every stability program works internally in per unit.

  Three further conveniences fall out:

  - Voltages are near **1.0 pu** everywhere, so an error is obvious at a glance. Seeing
    0.94 pu immediately reads as low; seeing 390 V requires you to remember the base.
  - Transformer impedance is already quoted in per cent on the nameplate — which is per
    unit × 100.
  - Machine impedances of similar design have similar per-unit values regardless of size,
    so a figure can be sanity-checked against experience.
</Callout>

### Changing base

Manufacturer data comes on the equipment's own base. To combine it with the rest of your
network you must convert:

$$
Z_{\text{pu,new}} = Z_{\text{pu,old}} \times
\frac{S_{\text{base,new}}}{S_{\text{base,old}}} \times
\left(\frac{V_{\text{base,old}}}{V_{\text{base,new}}}\right)^{2}
$$

<Callout type="warning" title="Base conversion is where per-unit errors live">
  Note the asymmetry: power scales **linearly**, voltage scales as the **square**. Getting
  either exponent wrong gives an answer that is plausible and wrong.

  The check that catches it: convert back to actual ohms both ways. Actual impedance does
  not depend on your choice of base, so if the two routes disagree, the conversion is wrong.
</Callout>

<Exercise n={1} kind="practice" title="Work one through">
  A transformer is rated 100 MVA, 11/132 kV, with 10% impedance on its own base. Express its
  impedance on a 500 MVA, 132 kV system base.

  <Solution>
    The voltage bases already match on the 132 kV side, so only the power base changes:

    $$
    Z_{\text{pu,new}} = 0.10 \times \frac{500}{100} \times \left(\frac{132}{132}\right)^2 = 0.50\ \text{pu}
    $$

    Check it in ohms, both ways.

    On the transformer's own base:

    $$
    Z_{\text{base,old}} = \frac{132000^2}{100\times10^6} = 174.2\ \Omega
    \quad\Rightarrow\quad Z = 0.10 \times 174.2 = 17.4\ \Omega
    $$

    On the system base:

    $$
    Z_{\text{base,new}} = \frac{132000^2}{500\times10^6} = 34.85\ \Omega
    \quad\Rightarrow\quad Z = 0.50 \times 34.85 = 17.4\ \Omega
    $$

    Same ohms. That agreement is the whole check — the physical impedance cannot depend on
    which base you chose to describe it in, so two routes to the same number confirms the
    conversion.

    Note also what the answer means: 0.50 pu is a **large** impedance on the system base.
    The transformer is small relative to the system, so it dominates the impedance between
    those two points. Per unit made that immediately visible in a way that 17.4 Ω did not.
  </Solution>
</Exercise>

## How many digits are real

Your meter shows `230.47`. How many of those five digits should appear in your report?

<Callout type="warning" title="Resolution is not accuracy">
  **Resolution** is the smallest change the display can show. **Accuracy** is how close the
  reading is to the truth. They are routinely different by an order of magnitude.

  A meter specified at ±(0.5% + 3 counts), reading 230.47 V on a 600 V range:

  - 0.5% of 230.47 = 1.15 V
  - 3 counts at 0.01 V resolution = 0.03 V
  - Total uncertainty ≈ **±1.2 V**

  So the true value is somewhere between 229.3 and 231.7. The last two digits displayed are
  meaningless as a statement about the voltage, even though the meter is genuinely
  responding to them.

  Report **230 ± 1 V**. Recording all five digits in your raw observations is fine and
  correct; carrying all five into a result is a claim you cannot support.
</Callout>

### Significant figures

The rules, briefly:

- **Multiplication and division**: the result carries as many significant figures as the
  *least* precise input.
- **Addition and subtraction**: the result carries as many *decimal places* as the least
  precise input.
- Keep extra digits through intermediate steps; round only at the end.

$$
P = VI = 230 \times 4.7 = 1081 \rightarrow \mathbf{1.1 \times 10^3\ \text{W}}
$$

Two significant figures in, two out. Writing 1081 W claims a precision the 4.7 A never had.

<Callout type="engineering">
  **Subtraction of similar numbers is where precision goes to die**, and your Experiment 1
  is built on exactly this operation.

  Voltage regulation needs $\Delta V = V_S - V_R$. Suppose both are measured to ±1 V:

  - $V_S = 230 \pm 1$
  - $V_R = 225 \pm 1$
  - $\Delta V = 5 \pm 2$ V

  The inputs were good to 0.4%. The difference is good to **40%** — a hundredfold loss of
  precision, from one subtraction.

  This is *catastrophic cancellation*, and it is the same phenomenon the Learning Hub's
  floating-point lesson covers in numerical terms. Here it is physical, and the practical
  responses are: use the most accurate range available, use the same meter for both
  readings so systematic errors partly cancel, and take the difference at the largest load
  you safely can, where ΔV is largest relative to its uncertainty.
</Callout>

## Propagating uncertainty

When a result is computed from measurements, the uncertainties combine. Two rules cover
nearly everything you will do.

**For sums and differences**, absolute uncertainties add in quadrature:

$$
q = a \pm b \quad\Rightarrow\quad \delta q = \sqrt{(\delta a)^2 + (\delta b)^2}
$$

**For products and quotients**, *relative* uncertainties add in quadrature:

$$
q = ab \ \text{or}\ \frac{a}{b} \quad\Rightarrow\quad
\frac{\delta q}{|q|} = \sqrt{\left(\frac{\delta a}{a}\right)^2 + \left(\frac{\delta b}{b}\right)^2}
$$

**For a power**, the relative uncertainty is multiplied by the exponent:

$$
q = a^n \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{\delta q}{|q|} = |n|\,\frac{\delta a}{|a|}
$$

<Exercise n={2} kind="practice" title="Uncertainty in a power measurement">
  You measure V = 230 ± 1 V and I = 4.70 ± 0.05 A. Report the apparent power with its
  uncertainty.

  <Solution>
    $$
    S = VI = 230 \times 4.70 = 1081\ \text{VA}
    $$

    Relative uncertainties:

    $$
    \frac{\delta V}{V} = \frac{1}{230} = 0.435\%, \qquad
    \frac{\delta I}{I} = \frac{0.05}{4.70} = 1.064\%
    $$

    Combined in quadrature:

    $$
    \frac{\delta S}{S} = \sqrt{0.00435^2 + 0.01064^2} = 0.0115 = 1.15\%
    $$

    $$
    \delta S = 0.0115 \times 1081 = 12.4\ \text{VA}
    $$

    Report **S = 1081 ± 12 VA**, or more honestly **1.08 ± 0.01 kVA**.

    Two things worth extracting.

    **The current dominates.** Its 1.06% swamps the voltage's 0.44%, and in quadrature the
    smaller contribution nearly vanishes — 0.44% adds only 0.09 percentage points to the
    total. If you want a better result, improve the *current* measurement; improving the
    voltage buys almost nothing.

    That is the practical value of doing this calculation: it tells you **where to spend
    effort**. Chasing precision in the term that does not dominate is wasted work.
  </Solution>
</Exercise>

<Callout type="tip" title="Why quadrature rather than simple addition">
  Adding uncertainties directly assumes every error conspires in the same direction at the
  same moment — the worst case, and an unlikely one.

  Independent random errors partly cancel, and the statistically correct combination is the
  root of the sum of squares. Simple addition gives an over-cautious bound; quadrature gives
  the realistic one.

  This only holds for **independent** errors. If both measurements come from the same
  mis-calibrated meter, the errors are correlated, they do not cancel, and quadrature
  understates the true uncertainty. Systematic errors have to be found and removed, not
  averaged away.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Averaging cannot rescue a systematic error">
  A meter reading 2% high reads 2% high on every one of a hundred readings. Averaging them
  gives a beautifully precise, confidently wrong answer — and a small standard deviation
  that makes it *look* reliable.

  This is why calibration certificates exist, and why a result quoted with tight error bars
  from an uncalibrated instrument is worth less than an honest one with wide bars.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Never write kW when you mean kVA">
  They differ by the power factor, and in a report they are different claims.

  A transformer rated 100 kVA supplying a 0.8 power factor load delivers 80 kW. Writing
  "100 kW transformer" is wrong, and an examiner will notice.
</Callout>

<KeyIdea>
  Per unit exists to make transformers disappear — pick voltage bases in the turns ratio and
  the whole network becomes one circuit. And a meter's **resolution is not its accuracy**:
  work out how many digits the specification actually supports, remember that subtracting
  similar numbers destroys most of them, and check whether the uncertainty you are chasing
  is even the one that dominates.
</KeyIdea>
