An unbalanced three-phase system has three coupled phasors. Analysing it directly
means solving a 3×3 coupled system at every step.

Fortescue's insight, in 1918: any set of three unbalanced phasors can be decomposed
into three *balanced* sets — and in a balanced network those three sets don't interact.
Three independent single-phase problems instead of one coupled three-phase one.

## The transformation

With $a = 1\angle 120° = e^{j2\pi/3}$:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} V_a \\ V_b \\ V_c \end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & a^2 & a \\ 1 & a & a^2 \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} V_0 \\ V_1 \\ V_2 \end{bmatrix}
$$

and the inverse, with its factor of one third:

$$
\begin{bmatrix} V_0 \\ V_1 \\ V_2 \end{bmatrix} = \frac{1}{3}
\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 \\ 1 & a & a^2 \\ 1 & a^2 & a \end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix} V_a \\ V_b \\ V_c \end{bmatrix}
$$

The three sequences:

- **Positive (1)** — balanced, a-b-c rotation. What a healthy system carries.
- **Negative (2)** — balanced, a-c-b rotation. Appears under unbalance; heats rotors.
- **Zero (0)** — all three in phase. Requires a neutral or earth return path.

<Analogy>
  Think of it as **resolving a force into components**.

  A diagonal force is awkward to work with directly. Split it into horizontal and
  vertical components and each can be handled independently, then recombined.

  Symmetrical components does that for three-phase phasors — and the payoff is that in
  a balanced network the three "directions" don't influence each other.
</Analogy>

## Implementation

<CodeFile name="sequence_transform.m">
```matlab
function A = fortescue_matrix()
%FORTESCUE_MATRIX Symmetrical component transformation matrix.
%   V_abc = A * V_012;   V_012 = A \ V_abc

    a = exp(1i*2*pi/3);
    A = [1  1     1;
         1  a^2   a;
         1  a     a^2];
end

function V012 = to_sequence(Vabc)
%TO_SEQUENCE Phase quantities to symmetrical components.
    V012 = fortescue_matrix() \ Vabc(:);       % backslash, not inv
end

function Vabc = to_phase(V012)
%TO_PHASE Symmetrical components back to phase quantities.
    Vabc = fortescue_matrix() * V012(:);
end
```
</CodeFile>

Using `A \ Vabc` rather than `inv(A)*Vabc` is the linear-systems lesson applied — and
here `A` is perfectly conditioned, so it makes little numerical difference, but the
habit is right.

Verify the inverse contains the factor of 1/3:

```matlab
A = fortescue_matrix();
disp(round(inv(A) * 3, 10));      % should be [1 1 1; 1 a a^2; 1 a^2 a]
```

Deriving that symbolically once — as the Symbolic Math lesson suggested — makes the
1/3 stick far better than memorising the matrix.

## Why it decouples

For a **balanced** three-phase impedance with self $Z_s$ and mutual $Z_m$:

$$
Z_{abc} = \begin{bmatrix} Z_s & Z_m & Z_m \\ Z_m & Z_s & Z_m \\ Z_m & Z_m & Z_s \end{bmatrix}
$$

Transform it:

$$
Z_{012} = A^{-1} Z_{abc} A =
\begin{bmatrix} Z_s + 2Z_m & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & Z_s - Z_m & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & Z_s - Z_m \end{bmatrix}
$$

**Diagonal.** The sequences don't interact. That is the entire reason the method
exists.

```matlab
syms Zs Zm
A = fortescue_matrix();                        % or build symbolically
Zabc = [Zs Zm Zm; Zm Zs Zm; Zm Zm Zs];
Z012 = simplify(inv(A) * Zabc * A)             % confirm it is diagonal
```

<Callout type="warning" title="Decoupling requires a balanced network">
  The transformation always *works* — you can always compute sequence components of
  any three phasors. But the sequences only **decouple** if the network impedance
  matrix has that symmetric structure.

  An untransposed transmission line, or a single-phase load on one phase of a
  distribution feeder, produces off-diagonal terms in $Z_{012}$ and the sequences
  couple. Symmetrical components is then still valid but no longer simplifying, which
  is why distribution analysis often works directly in phase coordinates — and why
  OpenDSS does.
</Callout>

## Sequence networks in practice

Each sequence has its own network with its own impedances:

- **Positive** — the normal load flow network. Generators appear as sources.
- **Negative** — similar topology, no sources; machine negative-sequence impedance is
  roughly the subtransient value.
- **Zero** — depends heavily on transformer winding connections and grounding.

<Callout type="engineering">
  The zero-sequence network is where students lose marks, because it is the one that
  isn't just "the same network again":

  - **Delta winding** — blocks zero sequence entirely. Open circuit.
  - **Star with the neutral solidly earthed** — provides a zero-sequence path.
  - **Star with an ungrounded neutral** — blocks it.
  - **Delta-star earthed** — zero-sequence current circulates in the delta and appears
    as a source on the star side.

  A grounding impedance $Z_n$ in the neutral appears as **$3Z_n$** in the zero-sequence
  network, because all three phase currents flow through it and $I_a + I_b + I_c = 3I_0$.

  That factor of 3 is worth deriving once rather than remembering.
</Callout>

## Unbalance factors

What a power quality analyser reports.

<CodeFile name="unbalance.m">
```matlab
function u = unbalance(Vabc)
%UNBALANCE Voltage unbalance factors from three phasors.
%
%   Returns negative- and zero-sequence unbalance as percentages of the
%   positive sequence — the IEC definition.

    arguments
        Vabc (3,1) double
    end

    V012 = fortescue_matrix() \ Vabc;

    u.V0 = V012(1);
    u.V1 = V012(2);
    u.V2 = V012(3);

    assert(abs(u.V1) > 1e-12, 'Positive sequence is zero — cannot form a ratio');

    u.negative_pct = 100 * abs(u.V2) / abs(u.V1);      % VUF
    u.zero_pct     = 100 * abs(u.V0) / abs(u.V1);

    % Reconstruction check — the transform must be lossless
    err = norm(fortescue_matrix()*V012 - Vabc) / norm(Vabc);
    assert(err < 1e-10, 'Round-trip error %.2e', err);
end
```
</CodeFile>

<Callout type="engineering">
  The commonly applied limit is **2% voltage unbalance factor** (negative sequence over
  positive) at the point of common coupling. Some standards use 1% for sustained
  operation.

  It matters because negative-sequence voltage drives a rotating field opposite to the
  rotor in an induction machine. The resulting currents are large — negative-sequence
  impedance is close to the locked-rotor value — and they produce heating with no
  useful torque. A 2% voltage unbalance can cause 10–15% current unbalance, which is
  why the limit is so tight.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout type="warning" title="Forgetting the 1/3 in the inverse">
  `inv(A)` has it; hand-writing the inverse matrix without it scales every sequence
  component by three.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Wrong a-operator convention">
  Some texts order the transformation matrix rows as 0,1,2 and others as 1,2,0. Both
  are valid; mixing them silently swaps your positive and negative sequences. Pick one
  and state it in a comment.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Assuming zero sequence exists">
  With no neutral or earth path, $I_0 = 0$ regardless of how unbalanced the phases are.
  A three-wire delta system simply has no zero-sequence current.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Expecting decoupling on an unbalanced network">
  The transform always works; the decoupling requires network symmetry.
</Callout>

## Mini tasks

<Exercise n={1} kind="predict" title="What sequences are present?">
  For each set of phase voltages, predict which sequence components are non-zero.

  1. `[1∠0°, 1∠−120°, 1∠120°]`
  2. `[1∠0°, 1∠0°, 1∠0°]`
  3. `[1∠0°, 0, 0]`
  4. `[1∠0°, 1∠120°, 1∠−120°]`

  <Solution>
    1. **Positive only.** Perfectly balanced a-b-c. $V_1 = 1$, others zero.
    2. **Zero only.** All in phase is the definition of zero sequence. $V_0 = 1$.
    3. **All three, each 1/3.** A single energised phase decomposes equally:
       $V_0 = V_1 = V_2 = 1/3$. This is why a single line-to-ground fault involves all
       three sequence networks.
    4. **Negative only.** The a-c-b rotation. $V_2 = 1$.

    Case 3 is the one to remember — it explains the structure of the LG fault
    connection in the next lesson.
  </Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} kind="practice" title="Analyse a real unbalance">
  A three-phase supply measures 233, 228 and 241 V with angles 0°, −119° and 122°.
  Compute the unbalance factors and judge compliance against a 2% limit.

  <Solution>
    ```matlab
    Vabc = [233 * exp(1i*deg2rad(0));
            228 * exp(1i*deg2rad(-119));
            241 * exp(1i*deg2rad(122))];

    u = unbalance(Vabc);

    fprintf('V0 = %.3f V at %.1f deg\n', abs(u.V0), rad2deg(angle(u.V0)));
    fprintf('V1 = %.3f V at %.1f deg\n', abs(u.V1), rad2deg(angle(u.V1)));
    fprintf('V2 = %.3f V at %.1f deg\n', abs(u.V2), rad2deg(angle(u.V2)));
    fprintf('\nVUF (negative) = %.2f%%\n', u.negative_pct);
    fprintf('Zero sequence  = %.2f%%\n', u.zero_pct);

    if u.negative_pct > 2
        fprintf('EXCEEDS the 2%% limit\n');
    else
        fprintf('Within the 2%% limit\n');
    end
    ```

    Both magnitude and angle deviations contribute. A common mistake is judging
    unbalance from magnitudes alone — three equal magnitudes at 0°, −115° and 125° are
    substantially unbalanced with no magnitude difference at all.

    That's a genuine argument for computing the VUF properly rather than using the
    NEMA approximation based on maximum deviation from the mean, which sees only
    magnitude.
  </Solution>
</Exercise>

<KeyIdea>
  Symmetrical components turns **one coupled three-phase problem into three independent
  single-phase ones** — but only when the network is balanced. The transformation always
  works; the *simplification* is what depends on symmetry.
</KeyIdea>
