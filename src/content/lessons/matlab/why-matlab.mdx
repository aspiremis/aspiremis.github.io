Before learning any of the syntax, it's worth knowing what this language is *for*.
That sounds like a detour. It isn't — almost every confusing thing about MATLAB
becomes obvious once you know what problem it was built to solve.

## The problem it was invented for

In the 1970s, if you wanted to solve a system of linear equations on a computer,
you wrote Fortran. Not a line or two of Fortran — you declared arrays, wrote loops,
and called routines from a library called LINPACK with argument lists like:

```text
CALL SGEFA(A, LDA, N, IPVT, INFO)
CALL SGESL(A, LDA, N, IPVT, B, 0)
```

Then you compiled it, linked it, ran it, and if you'd made a mistake you started
again. Solving $Ax = b$ — one line of mathematics — took a working morning.

Cleve Moler was teaching numerical analysis at the University of New Mexico and
wanted his students to use those libraries **without learning Fortran first**. So
he wrote an interactive calculator that let them type matrix operations directly
and see results immediately. He called it MATLAB, short for **Matrix Laboratory**.

That original program had no programming language at all. No loops, no functions,
no scripts. It was a place to type matrix expressions and get answers.

<Analogy>
  It's the difference between **writing a letter to a library** asking them to look
  something up and post the answer back, and **standing in the library** pulling
  books off the shelf yourself.

  Fortran was the letter. MATLAB was walking in.
</Analogy>

## What that history left behind

Nearly everything that surprises newcomers is a fingerprint of that origin.

**Everything is a matrix.** In most languages a number is a number, and you build
arrays out of numbers. In MATLAB it's the reverse: a matrix is the fundamental
thing, and a single number is just a 1×1 matrix. This is why `size(5)` answers
`1 1` rather than raising an error.

**Multiplication means matrix multiplication.** `A * B` is the matrix product, not
element-by-element. That is the *mathematically* natural default in a matrix
laboratory, even though it surprises anyone arriving from Python or C. Element-wise
multiplication needs a separate operator, `A .* B`, and forgetting the dot is the
single most common beginner error in the language.

**Indexing starts at 1.** Not because of a quirk of implementation, but because
mathematicians write $a_1$ for the first element of a vector. MATLAB was designed
to match textbooks, not memory addresses.

<Callout type="engineering">
  This matters immediately in power systems. A Y-bus matrix, a Jacobian, a
  sequence-component transformation — all of them are matrix expressions in your
  textbooks. In MATLAB they stay matrix expressions. In a language that treats
  arrays as lists of numbers, you spend your effort translating.
</Callout>

## Why engineers still use it in 2026

MATLAB is not the fastest language, it isn't free, and it isn't the most popular.
Three things keep it dominant in engineering education and industry:

**The toolboxes are validated.** When you call a Control System Toolbox function,
you are calling code that has been tested against known results for decades and is
accepted in regulatory and safety contexts. That trust is genuinely hard to
replicate.

**Simulink has no real equivalent.** Drawing a power electronic converter as a block
diagram and simulating it is something the open-source world still does not match
for breadth of component models.

**It is the lingua franca of engineering papers.** When a paper says "the algorithm
was implemented in MATLAB," you can usually reproduce it. That network effect is
self-sustaining.

The specific weaknesses worth knowing:

- **Loops are slow.** MATLAB is interpreted, and a tight `for` loop over a million
  elements is dramatically slower than the equivalent vectorised expression. This
  shapes how you write MATLAB — you'll meet it properly in the Programming module.
- **The licence follows the institution.** Your IIT Bhubaneswar licence is
  excellent while you're here. It will not follow you to a job that hasn't bought
  one, which is a real argument for learning Python too.
- **It's not a general-purpose language.** Building a web service or a data
  pipeline in MATLAB is possible and almost always a mistake.

<Callout type="tip" title="Learn both, in order">
  This Hub teaches MATLAB first because your coursework assumes it *now*, then
  Python because your research career will want it. The second language is far
  easier than the first — most of what you're about to learn is ideas, not syntax,
  and ideas transfer.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout type="warning" title="Treating MATLAB as 'Python with different punctuation'">
  Learners who arrive with some Python often write MATLAB as loops over indices,
  because that's what feels natural. The code works, runs slowly, and misses the
  entire point of the language. MATLAB rewards thinking in whole arrays, and that
  shift in thinking is the actual skill.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Believing you must memorise functions">
  MATLAB ships thousands of functions. Nobody knows them all. What experienced
  users have is a *sense of what probably exists*, plus fluency with `help`,
  `doc` and `lookfor`. Aim for that, not recall.
</Callout>

## Mini tasks

<Exercise n={1} kind="read" title="Predict without running">
  Given that MATLAB treats everything as a matrix, what do you expect each of these
  to produce? Reason it out before you have MATLAB open.

  ```matlab
  size(7)
  size([1 2 3])
  size([1 2 3]')
  ```

  <Solution>
    - `size(7)` → `1 1`. The number 7 is a 1×1 matrix.
    - `size([1 2 3])` → `1 3`. One row, three columns — a **row** vector.
    - `size([1 2 3]')` → `3 1`. The `'` transposes it into a **column** vector.

    The third one matters more than it looks. In power system work, voltages and
    currents are almost always column vectors, because that's how they appear in
    $I = Y V$.
  </Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} kind="practice" title="Make the case">
  You need to run 300 OpenDSS simulations, collect the results, and produce a
  summary figure. Which language would you choose, and what is the single strongest
  argument against your choice?

  <Solution>
    **Python** is the better fit — OpenDSS has a well-supported Python interface,
    and driving hundreds of scenarios is scripting and data handling rather than
    matrix mathematics.

    The strongest argument against: if the rest of your analysis pipeline is already
    in MATLAB, splitting a project across two languages adds friction that can
    outweigh the better fit. Consistency has real value.

    Notice that the honest answer is "it depends on context," not "Python is
    better." That's the habit worth building.
  </Solution>
</Exercise>

<KeyIdea>
  MATLAB is a **matrix laboratory**. Everything is a matrix, `*` means matrix
  multiplication, and indexing starts at 1 — not as quirks, but as direct
  consequences of what it was built for. Hold that idea and most of the language
  stops being surprising.
</KeyIdea>
