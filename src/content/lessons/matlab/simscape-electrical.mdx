Plain Simulink blocks pass **signals**: a number flows from output to input, one
direction. That is exactly right for a control loop and exactly wrong for a circuit.

In a circuit, connecting two components makes current flow *both ways*, and voltage is
shared. There's no "output" of a resistor. Simscape Electrical models that properly.

## Physical connections versus signal flow

<Analogy>
  A Simulink signal line is a **telephone call** — information travels one way, and
  the listener doesn't affect the speaker.

  A Simscape connection is a **wire**. Connect a load and the source *notices*. Current
  flows, voltage sags, and both ends are coupled.

  You cannot model a circuit with telephone calls. That's what the two block libraries
  are for.
</Analogy>

Practically: Simscape ports are **circles** and their connections are undirected.
Simulink ports are arrows.

To read a physical quantity into the signal domain you need an explicit **sensor**
block, and to impose one you need a **controlled source**. Those conversions are where
the two worlds meet — typically a voltage sensor feeding a controller, whose output
drives a controlled source or a switch gate.

## The powergui is mandatory

Every Simscape Electrical model needs exactly one **powergui** block. It isn't
decoration — it configures the solver that resolves the network equations.

```matlab
% Set the mode from a script
powergui = find_system(model, 'BlockType', 'PowerGUI');
set_param(powergui{1}, 'SimulationMode', 'Discrete');
set_param(powergui{1}, 'SampleTime', '5e-6');       % 5 microseconds
```

<Callout type="engineering">
  **Phasor mode is the one worth understanding.** It replaces the differential
  equations with algebraic phasor equations at a single frequency, so a multi-second
  transient stability study runs in seconds instead of hours.

  The trade is total: harmonics, switching and any off-fundamental behaviour vanish
  entirely. It is exactly right for rotor-angle stability and completely wrong for
  power quality.

  Choosing the mode is choosing which physics you are modelling — not a performance
  tweak.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Discrete sample time and switching">
  In Discrete mode, `SampleTime` caps your time resolution. With a 10 kHz carrier
  (100 µs period), a sample time of 50 µs resolves the switching barely and inaccurately.

  Rule of thumb: **Ts ≈ switching period / 20**, so 5 µs for 10 kHz. Smaller is more
  accurate and slower.
</Callout>

## What's in the library

- **Sources** — AC/DC voltage and current, three-phase programmable source
- **Passives** — series and parallel RLC branches and loads, mutual inductance
- **Machines** — synchronous, asynchronous, permanent magnet, DC, with standard
  parameterisations
- **Power electronics** — diode, thyristor, IGBT, MOSFET, and complete bridge blocks
- **Transformers** — single and three-phase, saturable, three-winding
- **Transmission** — PI section line, distributed parameter line
- **Control & measurement** — sensors, PLL, PWM generators, RMS and power measurement,
  sequence analysers

The three-phase blocks are where the real value is. Getting a saturable three-phase
transformer or a synchronous machine right from first principles is weeks of work; here
it's a block with a parameter dialog.

This matters more than any block-level detail.

<Callout type="warning" title="Simscape Electrical is not a distribution analysis tool">
  It *can* model an unbalanced feeder, and it will be painfully slow and awkward for
  a hosting capacity study over hundreds of scenarios. OpenDSS exists for exactly that
  problem and does it in milliseconds per case.

  Choosing the wrong tool costs weeks. The rule: **switching and dynamics → Simscape;
  unbalanced steady-state distribution → OpenDSS; large algebraic networks → code.**
</Callout>

## A worked configuration

<CodeFile name="configure_model.m" caption="Setting up a converter model entirely from a script">
```matlab
function configure_converter(model, f_sw)
%CONFIGURE_CONVERTER Set solver and powergui for a switching study.
%   F_SW is the switching frequency in Hz.

    arguments
        model (1,:) char
        f_sw  (1,1) double {mustBePositive}
    end

    load_system(model);

    Ts = 1/(20*f_sw);                     % 20 samples per switching period

    pg = find_system(model, 'BlockType', 'PowerGUI');
    assert(~isempty(pg), 'Model has no powergui block — Simscape models require one');
    set_param(pg{1}, 'SimulationMode', 'Discrete');
    set_param(pg{1}, 'SampleTime', num2str(Ts));

    set_param(model, 'SolverType', 'Fixed-step');
    set_param(model, 'FixedStep',  num2str(Ts));
    set_param(model, 'StopTime',   '0.2');

    fprintf('Configured %s: discrete, Ts = %.2e s (%d samples/period)\n', ...
            model, Ts, round(1/(f_sw*Ts)));
end
```
</CodeFile>

Discrete powergui plus a fixed-step solver at the same `Ts` is the standard,
predictable configuration for switching studies — and it's what you need if the model
will later be used for code generation.

## Verifying a model

A Simscape model produces plausible-looking waveforms very easily. Check it.

```matlab
% 1. Power balance — losses must be positive and sensible
P_in  = mean(v_in  .* i_in);
P_out = mean(v_out .* i_out);
eta   = P_out / P_in;
assert(eta > 0 && eta < 1, 'Efficiency %.3f is physically impossible', eta);

% 2. Switching ripple must appear at the carrier frequency
[amp, f] = one_sided_fft(v_out, fs);
[~, pk]  = max(amp(f > 0.5*f_sw & f < 1.5*f_sw));
% ... confirm the peak sits at f_sw

% 3. RMS against theory for a known operating point
V_rms = rms(v_out);
fprintf('measured %.2f V vs expected %.2f V\n', V_rms, V_expected);
```

<Callout type="tip" title="Efficiency above 100% means the model is wrong">
  It's the fastest sanity check there is. A converter that outputs more power than it
  takes in usually means a sign convention error on a current measurement, or a source
  connected where a load should be. Assert on it.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout type="warning" title="Missing or duplicated powergui">
  Exactly one per model. Zero gives a cryptic error; two gives conflicting solver
  configuration.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Connecting a Simulink signal to a Simscape port">
  They're different domains. You need a sensor block to go physical → signal, and a
  controlled source to go the other way.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Sample time too large for the switching frequency">
  Aliased, wrong waveforms that look clean. Ts ≈ period/20.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Using phasor mode for a harmonic study">
  Phasor mode has no harmonics by construction. The answer will be beautifully clean
  and meaningless.
</Callout>

## Mini tasks

<Exercise n={1} kind="read" title="Which simulation mode?">
  1. THD of a six-pulse rectifier
  2. Rotor angle swing of four machines over 10 s after a fault
  3. Inrush current when energising a transformer
  4. Voltage sag propagation through a distribution network over 500 ms

  <Solution>
    1. **Discrete.** Harmonics are the entire measurement; phasor mode would report
       zero distortion.
    2. **Phasor.** Ten seconds of four-machine detail in discrete mode is very slow,
       and rotor-angle dynamics live at the fundamental. This is what phasor mode is
       for.
    3. **Continuous or fine Discrete.** Inrush is driven by core saturation and the
       point-on-wave at closing — genuinely nonlinear, needs fine time resolution.
    4. **Discrete.** Sub-cycle behaviour matters, but not at switching resolution.

    The question is always: **what is the fastest phenomenon I need to see?** Set the
    mode and sample time from that.
  </Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} kind="debug" title="Efficiency comes out at 104%">
  A DC-DC converter model reports output power exceeding input. What is wrong?

  <Solution>
    Physically impossible, so it's a measurement or convention error. In order of
    likelihood:

    **Current sensor polarity.** Simscape current measurement has a direction. Reversed
    on the input side, `P_in` comes out negative or undersized.

    **Averaging over a partial switching period.** `mean(v.*i)` over a non-integer
    number of cycles biases the result — the same synchronisation issue as the FFT
    lesson. Average over whole periods.

    **Measuring at the wrong node.** Voltage taken after the input filter rather than at
    the source omits some of the input power.

    Diagnose by checking each measurement in isolation at a steady operating point, and
    confirm the input current is positive when power flows in:

    ```matlab
    fprintf('P_in %.2f W, P_out %.2f W, losses %.2f W\n', P_in, P_out, P_in - P_out);
    assert(P_in > 0, 'Input power negative — check current sensor polarity');
    ```
  </Solution>
</Exercise>
