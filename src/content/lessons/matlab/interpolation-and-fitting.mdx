You have measurements at some points and want values in between. There are two
completely different situations hiding in that sentence, and treating them the same
way is one of the most common mistakes in engineering data work.

## The distinction

<Analogy>
  **Interpolation** assumes your data points are **exactly right**. You're filling in
  the gaps between known truths — like reading a value between two gridlines on a
  calibrated chart.

  **Regression** assumes your data points are **noisy measurements of a simpler
  underlying reality**. You're looking for the trend, and deliberately *not* passing
  through every point, because some of what you measured was noise.

  Interpolate a manufacturer's lookup table. Regress your own measurements.
</Analogy>

Get this backwards and you either smooth away real structure, or fit a curve to your
own measurement noise and then trust it.

## Interpolation with `interp1`

```matlab
x = [0 2 4 6 8 10];
y = [0 3 7 8 9 9.5];

xq = 0:0.1:10;

y_lin    = interp1(x, y, xq, 'linear');    % default
y_spline = interp1(x, y, xq, 'spline');    % smooth cubic
y_pchip  = interp1(x, y, xq, 'pchip');     % shape-preserving cubic
y_near   = interp1(x, y, xq, 'nearest');   % piecewise constant
```

All four pass through every original point. They differ entirely in what they do
between.

<Callout type="tip" title="pchip when the data is monotonic">
  `spline` produces the smoothest curve but can **overshoot** — it may dip below a
  minimum or rise above a maximum that isn't in your data. `pchip` is
  shape-preserving: monotonic data stays monotonic.

  For anything physically bounded — an efficiency curve that cannot exceed 1, a
  demand series that cannot go negative — `pchip` is the safer choice. A spline that
  dips to −2 MW between two positive readings has invented a value that cannot exist.
</Callout>

```matlab
% Extrapolation is off by default — you get NaN outside the range
interp1(x, y, 12)                          % NaN
interp1(x, y, 12, 'linear', 'extrap')      % extrapolates, at your own risk
```

That NaN default is deliberate and good. It refuses to invent data outside the range
you measured unless you explicitly ask.

## Regression with `polyfit`

```matlab
p = polyfit(x, y, n);        % least-squares fit of degree n
y_fit = polyval(p, xq);
```

<CodeFile name="line_resistance.m" caption="Fitting a physical relationship to measurements">
```matlab
% Measured voltage drop against current on a feeder.
% Theory says dV = I*R, a straight line through the origin.
I  = [10 25 40 55 70 85 100];                    % amperes
dV = [2.1 5.4 8.3 11.6 14.5 17.9 20.8];          % volts, with measurement noise

% Degree 1: dV = p(1)*I + p(2)
p = polyfit(I, dV, 1);
R_est  = p(1);
offset = p(2);

y_fit = polyval(p, I);
residuals = dV - y_fit;

SS_res = sum(residuals.^2);
SS_tot = sum((dV - mean(dV)).^2);
R2 = 1 - SS_res/SS_tot;

fprintf('Estimated resistance: %.4f ohms\n', R_est);
fprintf('Intercept: %.4f V (should be ~0 for a pure resistance)\n', offset);
fprintf('R-squared: %.5f\n', R2);
fprintf('RMS residual: %.4f V\n', sqrt(mean(residuals.^2)));
```
</CodeFile>

The intercept is the interesting output. Theory says a pure resistance passes through
the origin, so a large intercept means either a systematic measurement offset or
something in the circuit the model doesn't capture. **Checking whether the fitted
parameters are physically sensible is the actual analysis** — R² alone won't tell you.

## Overfitting

<CodeFile name="overfit_demo.m">
```matlab
rng(42);
x = linspace(0, 10, 12);
y_true = 2 + 0.5*x;
y = y_true + 0.4*randn(size(x));         % 12 noisy points on a straight line

xq = linspace(0, 10, 300);

figure; hold on;
scatter(x, y, 40, 'k', 'filled');
plot(xq, polyval(polyfit(x, y, 1),  xq), 'LineWidth', 1.6);
plot(xq, polyval(polyfit(x, y, 11), xq), '--', 'LineWidth', 1.3);
hold off;
legend({'Data', 'Degree 1', 'Degree 11'}, 'Location', 'northwest');
ylim([0 12]);
```
</CodeFile>

The degree-11 polynomial passes through all twelve points **exactly**. Its residual is
zero. It is also useless: it oscillates wildly between the points and is nonsense
anywhere off them.

<Callout type="warning" title="A perfect fit is a warning sign">
  Any degree-*n* polynomial can pass through *n+1* points exactly. Zero residual on
  noisy data means you have fitted the noise, not the signal.

  MATLAB warns — `Polynomial is badly conditioned` — for exactly this reason. The
  fitting problem itself becomes ill-conditioned at high degree, which is the
  linear-systems lesson showing up again.
</Callout>

The honest test is **out-of-sample**: fit on part of the data, evaluate on the rest.
A model that fits its training points perfectly and predicts held-out points badly has
learned noise.

## Choosing a model form

Prefer a model with physical meaning over a high-degree polynomial.

```matlab
% Bad: degree-4 polynomial through a decaying signal
p = polyfit(t, y, 4);

% Better: fit the form the physics implies
model = @(c, t) c(1) * exp(-t/c(2)) + c(3);
c0 = [1, 5, 0];
c  = lsqcurvefit(model, c0, t, y);        % Optimization Toolbox
```

The exponential has three parameters with meanings — amplitude, time constant, steady
state. The polynomial has five coefficients that mean nothing, and it extrapolates
absurdly.

<Callout type="engineering">
  Typical uses in power systems, and which is right:

  - **Transformer efficiency at part load** — interpolation. It's a lookup table.
  - **Estimating line parameters from measurements** — regression, degree 1, with the
    intercept checked against theory.
  - **Resampling 15-minute SCADA to hourly** — interpolation. `pchip`, so demand
    can't go negative between readings.
  - **Load growth over ten years** — regression, and be very careful about
    extrapolating. A polynomial trend fitted to ten years and extended to twenty is
    a prediction the data cannot support.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes

<Callout type="warning" title="Interpolating noisy data">
  Forces the curve through every measurement error. Use regression.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Raising the polynomial degree until it fits">
  It always eventually fits. That is not evidence of anything.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Extrapolating">
  Both methods are unreliable outside the measured range, and polynomials are
  spectacularly so. `interp1` returns NaN by default for good reason.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Reporting R² alone">
  R² always increases with more parameters. Report residual RMS, check whether the
  fitted parameters are physically plausible, and test out of sample.
</Callout>

## Mini tasks

<Exercise n={1} kind="read" title="Interpolate or regress?">
  1. A transformer datasheet gives efficiency at 25/50/75/100% load; you need 60%.
  2. Twenty measurements of voltage drop against current, to estimate resistance.
  3. Converting 15-minute meter data to hourly.
  4. Ten years of annual peak demand, to estimate next year's.

  <Solution>
    1. **Interpolate.** Datasheet values are exact. `pchip` — efficiency is bounded
       and spline could overshoot above 1.0.
    2. **Regress.** Measurements are noisy; the underlying relationship is a straight
       line. Degree 1, and check the intercept.
    3. **Interpolate.** You want to preserve the recorded values, not smooth them.
    4. **Regress — and be honest about the uncertainty.** Ten points, extrapolating
       one year out. A linear trend with a stated prediction interval is defensible;
       a cubic is not.
  </Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} kind="practice" title="Fit and validate">
  Fit a line to noisy voltage-drop data and report whether the fit is trustworthy.

  <Solution>
    ```matlab
    function report_fit(I, dV)
    %REPORT_FIT Fit dV = R*I + c and assess it.

        p = polyfit(I, dV, 1);
        fitted = polyval(p, I);
        res = dV - fitted;

        R_est   = p(1);
        offset  = p(2);
        rms_res = sqrt(mean(res.^2));
        R2      = 1 - sum(res.^2) / sum((dV - mean(dV)).^2);

        fprintf('R        = %.4f ohms\n', R_est);
        fprintf('offset   = %.4f V\n', offset);
        fprintf('R^2      = %.5f\n', R2);
        fprintf('RMS res  = %.4f V (%.2f%% of mean dV)\n', ...
                rms_res, 100*rms_res/mean(dV));

        % Residuals should look like noise. Structure means the model is wrong.
        if abs(corr(I(:), res(:))) > 0.3
            warning('Residuals correlate with current — the linear model is missing something');
        end

        if abs(offset) > 3*rms_res
            warning('Intercept is large relative to the noise — systematic offset?');
        end
    end
    ```

    The residual check is the one that matters. If residuals still correlate with the
    input, there is structure the model failed to capture, and no value of R² makes
    that acceptable. **Plot the residuals** — patterns are obvious visually and
    invisible in a summary statistic.
  </Solution>
</Exercise>

<KeyIdea>
  **Are your data points exact, or noisy?** Exact → interpolate, and use `pchip` for
  anything physically bounded. Noisy → regress with the **simplest model the physics
  justifies**, then check that the residuals look like noise rather than structure.
</KeyIdea>
