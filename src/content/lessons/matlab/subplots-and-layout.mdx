Power system results are rarely one quantity. You want voltage *and* current, real
*and* reactive power, all against the same time axis, and you want them aligned so
the eye can compare them.

## `tiledlayout` — the modern way

MATLAB has two systems. `subplot` is the old one and still works everywhere;
`tiledlayout` is newer, better, and what you should use.

<CodeFile name="pq_panels.m">
```matlab
hours = 0:23;
P = [38 35 34 33 34 37 45 52 58 61 60 57 55 54 56 59 63 68 66 60 54 48 43 40];
Q = P * 0.35 + 2*sin(hours/3);

figure;
t = tiledlayout(2, 1, 'TileSpacing', 'compact', 'Padding', 'compact');

nexttile;
plot(hours, P, 'LineWidth', 1.4);
ylabel('P (MW)');
grid on;

nexttile;
plot(hours, Q, 'LineWidth', 1.4, 'Color', [0.85 0.33 0.10]);
ylabel('Q (MVAr)');
grid on;

xlabel(t, 'Hour of day');                       % one shared x label
title(t, 'Feeder 3 — real and reactive demand');
```
</CodeFile>

Three things `tiledlayout` gives you that `subplot` doesn't:

**Shared labels.** `xlabel(t, ...)` on the layout, not the axes — one label for the
whole figure instead of repeating it under every panel.

**Tight spacing.** `'TileSpacing','compact'` removes the wasteful gaps `subplot`
leaves. `'tight'` removes more.

**Growable.** `tiledlayout('flow')` adds tiles as you call `nexttile`, without
deciding the grid in advance.

## Sharing the x-axis

When panels share a time axis, hide the duplicate tick labels and link the zoom.

```matlab
t = tiledlayout(3, 1, 'TileSpacing', 'tight');

ax1 = nexttile; plot(hours, V_pu);  ylabel('V (pu)');
ax2 = nexttile; plot(hours, P);     ylabel('P (MW)');
ax3 = nexttile; plot(hours, Q);     ylabel('Q (MVAr)');

xticklabels([ax1 ax2], {});          % only the bottom panel shows hours
xlabel(ax3, 'Hour of day');

linkaxes([ax1 ax2 ax3], 'x');        % zoom one, all three follow
```

`linkaxes` is the one to remember. Zoom into the evening peak on any panel and all
three move together — which is exactly what you want when you're trying to work out
whether a voltage dip lines up with a load spike.

<Callout type="engineering">
  This three-panel, linked-axis layout — voltage, real power, reactive power against
  time — is the standard way to present a QSTS simulation or a day of SCADA data.
  Learn it once and you'll use it constantly.
</Callout>

## Spanning tiles

```matlab
t = tiledlayout(3, 2);

nexttile([1 2]);        % one row tall, two columns wide — full-width top panel
plot(hours, total_demand);
title('System demand');

nexttile; plot(hours, feeder1); title('Feeder 1');
nexttile; plot(hours, feeder2); title('Feeder 2');
nexttile; plot(hours, feeder3); title('Feeder 3');
nexttile; plot(hours, feeder4); title('Feeder 4');
```

A wide summary panel above a grid of details is a good default for "here's the whole
system, and here's the breakdown."

## Two y-axes — use sparingly

```matlab
yyaxis left;
plot(hours, V_pu);  ylabel('Voltage (pu)');

yyaxis right;
plot(hours, P);     ylabel('Demand (MW)');
```

<Callout type="warning" title="Two y-axes can imply a relationship that isn't there">
  With independent scales, you can make any two series appear correlated or
  uncorrelated by choosing the limits. Readers see the visual coincidence, not the
  scaling choice you made.

  Use it when the two quantities are genuinely tied — voltage and current on the same
  feeder — and prefer stacked panels with a shared x-axis when they aren't. The
  panels make no claim about relative magnitude.
</Callout>

## Legends across a layout

```matlab
lg = legend(ax1, {'Bus 1', 'Bus 7', 'Bus 12'});
lg.Layout.Tile = 'east';        % one legend beside the whole layout
```

Placing the legend on the layout rather than inside an axes stops it covering data
and avoids repeating the same key in every panel.

## Common mistakes

<Callout type="warning" title="Repeating the x label under every panel">
  With a shared axis, one label on the layout is correct. Repeating it wastes space
  and implies the axes might differ.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Panels with different x limits">
  If the top panel shows 0–23 and the bottom 6–18, the eye compares them anyway and
  reads a relationship that isn't there. `linkaxes` prevents this.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="Too many tiles">
  Beyond about six panels nobody reads any of them. Either aggregate, or produce
  several figures.
</Callout>

## Mini tasks

<Exercise n={1} kind="practice" title="A three-panel day">
  Build a figure with voltage, real power and reactive power stacked over 24 hours,
  sharing one x-axis, with limit lines on the voltage panel.

  <Solution>
    ```matlab
    hours = 0:23;
    V_pu  = 1.02 - 0.001*[38 35 34 33 34 37 45 52 58 61 60 57 ...
                          55 54 56 59 63 68 66 60 54 48 43 40];
    P = [38 35 34 33 34 37 45 52 58 61 60 57 55 54 56 59 63 68 66 60 54 48 43 40];
    Q = P * 0.35;

    figure('Position', [100 100 700 700]);
    t = tiledlayout(3, 1, 'TileSpacing', 'tight', 'Padding', 'compact');

    ax1 = nexttile;
    plot(hours, V_pu, 'LineWidth', 1.4);
    yline(1.05, '--', 'Color', [0.6 0.6 0.6]);
    yline(0.95, '--', 'Color', [0.6 0.6 0.6]);
    ylabel('V (pu)'); ylim([0.93 1.07]); grid on;

    ax2 = nexttile;
    plot(hours, P, 'LineWidth', 1.4);
    ylabel('P (MW)'); grid on;

    ax3 = nexttile;
    plot(hours, Q, 'LineWidth', 1.4);
    ylabel('Q (MVAr)'); grid on;

    xticklabels([ax1 ax2], {});
    xlabel(t, 'Hour of day');
    title(t, 'Feeder 3 — 12 August 2026');
    linkaxes([ax1 ax2 ax3], 'x');
    xlim(ax1, [0 23]);
    ```

    Note `xlim` on one axes only — because the axes are linked, setting one sets all
    three. That's the property doing useful work rather than just being convenient.
  </Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} kind="read" title="Which layout?">
  Choose a layout for each:

  1. Voltage at 40 buses along one feeder
  2. Demand and temperature over a year
  3. The same voltage profile under 6 different PV penetration levels
  4. Convergence history of three different solvers on one system

  <Solution>
    1. **One axes, one line** — voltage against bus number. Forty *series* would be
       clutter; forty *points on one line* is a profile.
    2. **Two stacked tiles with linked x.** Different units entirely. A second y-axis
       would invite the reader to see a correlation you haven't demonstrated.
    3. **One axes, six lines with a legend** — same quantity, same units, and
       comparison is the whole point. A 2×3 grid would make comparison harder.
    4. **One axes, semilogy, three lines.** Same quantity, and the comparison of
       convergence *rates* is exactly what the figure is for.

    The pattern: same quantity and units → one axes. Different units → separate
    panels.
  </Solution>
</Exercise>

<KeyIdea>
  **Same quantity and units → one axes. Different units → stacked tiles with a linked
  x-axis.** Reach for two y-axes only when the quantities are genuinely coupled, and
  know that the choice of scaling is an argument you're making.
</KeyIdea>
