Two questions, one bench.

The first is old and settled: when you load a transmission line, what happens to the
voltage at the far end? The answer turns out to depend almost entirely on the *phase* of
the load current, not its size — and with a capacitive load the voltage goes **up**, which
is not what intuition suggests.

The second question is the one that ends blackouts. Push more and more power down the same
line and the receiving voltage sags further and further — until at some point there is no
solution at all. Not a slow decline: an actual cliff, past which the equations have no real
answer. That cliff is the nose of the PV curve, and finding it is what Part (b) is about.

## Why this experiment exists

<Callout type="engineering">
  On 28 September 2003, Italy lost supply to essentially the entire country. On 4 November
  2006, a single planned line switching in northern Germany split the European grid into
  three islands. Both events are, in part, stories about how much power a corridor of lines
  can carry before voltage collapses rather than sags.

  The mathematics you will derive in twenty minutes below — a quadratic in $V^2$ with a
  discriminant that goes negative — is the same mathematics a system operator's voltage
  stability assessment runs on a network of thousands of buses.

  The bench version has one line and one load. The structure is identical.
</Callout>

Three things make this the right first experiment.

It is **visible**. Increase the inductive load and watch a voltmeter fall. Switch to
capacitive and watch it rise past the source voltage. You do not need instrumentation to
believe it.

It is **predictive**. The approximate drop formula tells you the sign and roughly the size
before you switch anything on. An experiment where you know what should happen is an
experiment where a wrong reading means something.

And it **sets up everything else**. Experiment 3 measures the Ferranti effect, which is
this same voltage rise driven by the line's own capacitance. Experiment 3(b) adds shunt
compensation, which is this same effect used deliberately. Experiment 5 measures the ZIP
load model, which changes the answer to Part (b) — because a constant-power load makes the
nose sharper than a constant-impedance one.

## Theory

### The short-line model

For a line short enough that shunt capacitance can be ignored — which is what "simplified"
means in the experiment title — the line is a series impedance:

$$
Z = R + jX
$$

and the two ends are related by one equation:

$$
V_S = V_R + I\,Z
$$

Take the receiving-end voltage as the phase reference, $V_R = V_R \angle 0°$, and let the
current lag it by $\varphi$:

$$
I = I\angle{-\varphi} = I(\cos\varphi - j\sin\varphi)
$$

Substituting:

$$
V_S = V_R + I(\cos\varphi - j\sin\varphi)(R + jX)
$$

$$
V_S = \underbrace{V_R + I(R\cos\varphi + X\sin\varphi)}_{\text{in phase with } V_R} + j\underbrace{I(X\cos\varphi - R\sin\varphi)}_{\text{in quadrature}}
$$

The quadrature term rotates the phasor — it produces the load angle — but contributes very
little to the *magnitude*, because it adds in quadrature to a much larger in-phase
component. So to a good approximation:

$$
\boxed{\;\Delta V = |V_S| - |V_R| \approx I\,(R\cos\varphi + X\sin\varphi)\;}
$$

<KeyIdea>
  For a **leading** load the current angle flips sign, and so does the reactive term:

  $$\Delta V \approx I(R\cos\varphi - X\sin\varphi)$$

  Since $X \gg R$ on any transmission line, that second term dominates. A sufficiently
  capacitive load makes $\Delta V$ **negative** — the receiving end sits *above* the
  sending end. Nothing is generating voltage; the line's own reactance is being driven
  backwards by leading current.
</KeyIdea>

### Reading the formula before touching the bench

The formula predicts all three cases in the experiment sheet, without a single measurement:

  headings={['Load', 'φ', 'ΔV ≈ I(R cos φ ± X sin φ)', 'Prediction']}
  rows={[
    { option: 'Resistive', when: '0°', cost: 'I·R only — the X term vanishes', recommended: false },
    { option: 'Inductive (lagging)', when: '+φ', cost: 'I(R cos φ + X sin φ) — both terms add', recommended: false },
    { option: 'Capacitive (leading)', when: '−φ', cost: 'I(R cos φ − X sin φ) — the X term subtracts', recommended: false },
  ]}
/>

So: **small drop, large drop, rise**. In that order. And because $X \gg R$, the inductive
drop should be several times the resistive drop at the same current — that ratio is the
sharpest check on whether your readings are sensible.

Voltage regulation is this drop expressed as a fraction of the loaded voltage:

$$
\text{VR}\% = \frac{|V_{R,\text{no load}}| - |V_{R,\text{full load}}|}{|V_{R,\text{full load}}|}\times 100
$$

For a short line with no shunt path, no load means no current, so $V_{R,\text{no load}} = V_S$.
Capacitive load gives a **negative** regulation, which is a correct and slightly startling
result the first time you compute it.

### From two equations to the PV curve

Now Part (b). Model the source as a stiff voltage $E$ behind the line reactance $X$,
feeding a load drawing $P + jQ$ at voltage $V$. Neglect $R$ for now — we will come back to
why that matters on a real bench.

The current is:

$$
I = \frac{E\angle\delta - V\angle 0}{jX}
$$

and the complex power delivered to the load is $S = V I^*$, which expands to:

$$
P = \frac{EV\sin\delta}{X}, \qquad Q = \frac{EV\cos\delta - V^2}{X}
$$

These are the standard two-bus power flow equations. Now eliminate $\delta$ — rearrange
both, square, and add, using $\sin^2\delta + \cos^2\delta = 1$:

$$
(PX)^2 + (QX + V^2)^2 = (EV)^2
$$

Expanding gives a quadratic in $V^2$:

$$
\boxed{\;V^4 + V^2\left(2QX - E^2\right) + X^2\left(P^2 + Q^2\right) = 0\;}
$$

with solution:

$$
V^2 = \frac{\left(E^2 - 2QX\right) \pm \sqrt{\left(E^2 - 2QX\right)^2 - 4X^2\left(P^2+Q^2\right)}}{2}
$$

Everything interesting is in that discriminant.

<Callout type="engineering">
  **Two solutions, one discriminant, three consequences.**

  The $\pm$ gives **two voltages** for the same power. The upper root is the normal
  operating point. The lower root is a real solution to the equations at which the same
  power is delivered at low voltage and very high current — physically reachable, and
  unstable, because there the usual control actions push the system the wrong way.

  When the discriminant reaches **zero**, the two roots merge. That is the nose. Beyond it
  the discriminant is negative and **there is no real voltage at all** — not a low voltage,
  no solution. The load is asking for more power than the line can deliver at any voltage.

  This is why a load-flow program *diverges* near collapse rather than returning a bad
  answer. It is not a numerical failure. There is genuinely nothing to find.
</Callout>

At unity power factor ($Q = 0$) the algebra collapses to something you can quote:

$$
P_{\max} = \frac{E^2}{2X}, \qquad V_{\text{critical}} = \frac{E}{\sqrt{2}} \approx 0.707\,E
$$

The critical voltage is **independent of the line reactance** — a striking result. Halving
$X$ doubles the transferable power but leaves the collapse voltage at the same 0.707 pu.

<Callout type="warning" title="0.707 pu is not a warning threshold">
  It is where collapse has already occurred, for a lossless line at unity power factor.

  Real systems operate with lagging load, which pulls the nose down and to the left, and
  real operating limits sit at 0.95 pu. Nobody plans to approach 0.707 — the number is a
  bound, not a target, and quoting it as an operating limit in a viva is a mistake.
</Callout>

<Analogy>
  Think of the line as a pipe and $\delta$ as the pressure difference across it.

  Opening the valve further increases flow — up to a point. Past that point, the pressure
  drop needed to push more through has grown so large that the pressure at the far end has
  collapsed, and the flow you get *falls*. Pushing harder gets you less.

  In the electrical case: $P = EV\sin\delta / X$ has $V$ in it, and $V$ is itself falling
  as you load. Past the nose, $V$ falls faster than $\sin\delta$ rises.
</Analogy>

## Safety and pre-lab

<Hazard title="This bench is energised at 415 V three-phase">
  Three specific things about *this* experiment, beyond the general safety module.

  **The capacitor bank stores charge.** After switching off, the capacitors in the load
  bank and any line-model shunt capacitance hold voltage — potentially at close to peak
  value. Wait for the discharge resistors, then verify with a meter before touching
  anything. Do not trust a lamp or an indicator.

  **The voltage can go ABOVE nominal.** Under capacitive load the receiving end rises above
  the sending end. Do not assume the far end is the low-voltage end; check both.

  **Near the nose in Part (b) the current is high.** As voltage falls, the current for the
  same power rises. Watch the ammeter, not only the voltmeter, and stop at the trainer's
  rated current whether or not you have reached the nose.
</Hazard>

The topology is the same for both parts:

- The **variac** sets the sending-end voltage, so you control $V_S$ independently of load.
- The **line model** provides the series $R + jX$. On most trainers this is a set of
  switchable sections, so you can pick a line length.
- The **load bank** provides switchable resistive, inductive and capacitive steps.
- **Metering** goes at both ends: a voltmeter at each end, an ammeter in the line, and the
  power analyser at the receiving end for $P$, $Q$ and power factor.

<Callout type="warning" title="Set the sending-end voltage once, then leave the variac alone">
  Both parts of this experiment measure how the *receiving* voltage responds to load. If
  you adjust the variac to correct the voltage as you go, you have measured nothing — you
  have measured your own reflexes.

  Set $V_S$ to nominal, record it, and touch nothing but the load from then on. Re-check
  $V_S$ at every step and note it, because a weak supply will sag on its own and you need
  to know if it did.
</Callout>

<Steps>
  <Step n={1} title="Wire the circuit and have it checked" hazard="Do not energise before your demonstrator has checked the wiring.">
    Supply → variac → line model → load bank, with metering at both ends. Confirm the load
    bank is at minimum and all capacitor steps are off.
  </Step>

  <Step n={2} title="Energise and set the sending-end voltage" expect="the receiving-end voltage nearly equal to the sending end — the line is unloaded, so there is almost no drop">
    Raise the variac slowly to nominal. Record $V_S$ and the no-load $V_R$.

    The small difference between them is your no-load check: it should be close to zero.
    A large gap means something is drawing current that should not be.
  </Step>

  <Step n={3} title="Sweep the resistive load" expect="a small, steady fall in receiving voltage">
    Increase the resistive load in equal steps. At each step record $V_S$, $V_R$, $I$, $P$,
    $Q$ and power factor. Take a full set at each step before moving on.
  </Step>

  <Step n={4} title="Return to no load, then sweep the inductive load" expect="a noticeably larger fall than the resistive case at the same current">
    Reduce to minimum, confirm $V_R$ returns to its no-load value, then repeat with the
    inductive steps.

    Matching the *current* rather than the load setting is what makes the two sweeps
    comparable.
  </Step>

  <Step n={5} title="Return to no load, then sweep the capacitive load" hazard="Capacitors store charge. De-energise and verify discharge before rewiring." expect="the receiving voltage RISING, and passing above the sending end">
    Repeat with the capacitor steps. Watch $V_R$ rather than the load setting — this is the
    result that surprises people, and it is worth seeing happen.
  </Step>

  <Step n={6} title="Shut down safely">
    Load to minimum, variac to zero, isolate, and verify the capacitors have discharged
    before disconnecting anything.
  </Step>
</Steps>

#### Expected outcomes

<Expect
  quantity="Receiving-end voltage under resistive load"
  trend="falls slowly and almost linearly with current"
  magnitude="drop ≈ I·R, the smallest of the three cases"
>
  With $\varphi = 0$ the reactive term vanishes entirely and only $IR$ remains. Since $R$
  is the smaller of the two line parameters, this is the gentlest of the three curves.

  If your resistive drop is comparable to your inductive drop at the same current, either
  the line model is unusually resistive — check the nameplate — or the load bank is not as
  purely resistive as labelled. The power analyser will tell you: check that the power
  factor really is close to unity.
</Expect>

<Expect
  quantity="Receiving-end voltage under inductive load"
  trend="falls substantially faster than the resistive case at equal current"
  magnitude="drop ≈ I(R cos φ + X sin φ) — several times the resistive drop"
>
  Both terms now contribute, and the $X\sin\varphi$ term dominates because $X \gg R$. This
  is the case a distribution engineer worries about, and it is why voltage support is a
  reactive-power problem rather than a real-power one.

  The ratio between the inductive and resistive drops at the same current is roughly
  $(R\cos\varphi + X\sin\varphi)/R$, so it is a direct measure of your line's X/R ratio.
  Computing it is the cleanest check that your two sweeps are consistent with each other.
</Expect>

<Expect
  quantity="Receiving-end voltage under capacitive load"
  trend="RISES, and crosses above the sending-end voltage"
  magnitude="drop ≈ I(R cos φ − X sin φ), which goes negative once X sin φ exceeds R cos φ"
>
  Leading current flips the sign of the reactive term. Once the capacitive load is large
  enough that $X\sin\varphi > R\cos\varphi$, the drop is negative and the receiving end
  sits above the sending end.

  There is no energy source at the far end. The leading current drives the line reactance
  in the opposite sense, and the voltage across it adds to rather than subtracts from the
  source.

  This is the same physics as the Ferranti effect in Experiment 3 — there the capacitance
  is the line's own rather than a load — and the same physics that shunt capacitor banks
  exploit deliberately in Experiment 3(b). Three experiments, one mechanism.
</Expect>

#### Calculations and analysis

For each row, compute:

1. $\Delta V = V_S - V_R$, and $\text{VR}\% = (V_S - V_R)/V_R \times 100$
2. The power factor angle $\varphi = \cos^{-1}(\text{PF})$, signed by whether $Q$ is
   positive or negative
3. The predicted drop $I(R\cos\varphi \pm X\sin\varphi)$ from your nameplate $R$ and $X$
4. The percentage difference between predicted and measured

Then plot all three sweeps as $V_R$ against $I$ on one set of axes, with the sending-end
voltage as a horizontal reference line. Three curves that fan out from a common no-load

<Callout type="tip" title="Plot predicted and measured on the same axes">
  Two curves per load type — measured points, and the approximate-formula prediction as a
  line. Agreement validates both your wiring and your understanding; disagreement localises
  which one is wrong.

  Expect the approximation to be good at small angles and to drift at large ones, because
  the quadrature term you neglected grows. Quantifying that drift is a better result than
  reporting agreement.
</Callout>

#### Expected outcomes

<Expect
  quantity="The PV curve at unity power factor"
  trend="falls gently, then steepens, turning back on itself at the nose"
  magnitude="theoretical P_max = E²/2X for a lossless line; expect measurably less"
>
  The curve is the upper branch of the quadratic solution. Near no load it is almost flat —
  which is exactly why voltage is a poor early warning of proximity to collapse. Most of
  the voltage change happens in the last part of the range.

  You will very likely not reach the nose. The trainer's rated current arrives first, which
  is the honest and useful outcome: on a real short line, the **thermal limit binds before
  the voltage-stability limit**. That ordering reverses on long lines and heavily loaded
  corridors, which is when voltage stability becomes the operator's binding constraint.
</Expect>

<Expect
  quantity="The effect of lagging power factor"
  trend="the entire curve drops, and the nose moves to lower power"
  magnitude="from the discriminant: positive Q reduces (E² − 2QX), shrinking the region with a real solution"
>
  Look at the quadratic. Lagging $Q$ enters as $-2QX$ inside the squared term and as
  $+Q^2$ inside the subtracted one — so it attacks the discriminant twice over. The
  region where a real voltage exists shrinks from both directions.

  Leading $Q$ does the reverse, which is exactly why shunt capacitors and SVCs are the
  standard response to a voltage stability problem. Experiment 3(b) measures that directly.
</Expect>

<Expect
  quantity="Agreement with the lossless prediction"
  trend="measured maximum power below E²/2X, and the turning voltage above 0.707 E"
  magnitude="the gap grows with the trainer's R/X ratio"
>
  The derivation neglected $R$. A teaching trainer has a much lower X/R ratio than a real
  transmission line, so the resistive drop is a larger share of the total.

  Series resistance dissipates real power and adds an in-phase drop, both of which reduce
  the deliverable power and lift the turning voltage. So expect $P_{\max}$ below the
  lossless figure and $V_{\text{critical}}$ above $0.707E$.

  **Quantifying that gap is a better result than matching the ideal formula.** Compare the
  lossless prediction against a numerical solution that includes $R$ — the simulation
  section below does exactly this — and report both.
</Expect>

nose.

Overlay all three power factors, and overlay the theoretical curves computed from your
nameplate $R$ and $X$.

Then state, explicitly:

- The maximum power you reached, and **why you stopped** — current limit, or the turn
- The measured $V_R/V_S$ at that point
- How the lagging curve compares with the unity curve at the same power
- The gap between your measurements and the lossless $E^2/2X$, with $R$ offered as the
  explanation and the numerical solution as evidence

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not extrapolate to a nose you did not reach">
  It is tempting to fit a curve through your points and read off where it would have turned.

  Resist it. The extrapolation is dominated by the very region you have no data in, and the
  load model changes character there. Report the range you measured and say plainly that
  the nose lies beyond it. A stated limit is a result; an extrapolated nose is a guess
  wearing a graph.
</Callout>

function pv_curve()
% Part (b): PV curves at three power factors, lossless and with R.

E = 230;       % V, stiff source behind the line
X = 8.0;       % ohm per phase
R = 2.0;       % ohm per phase — set to 0 to see the textbook curve

pfs = [1.0, 0.8, 0.8];
lag = [true, true, false];          % third case is leading

figure; hold on; grid on

for k = 1:numel(pfs)
    phi = acos(pfs(k));
    if ~lag(k), phi = -phi; end

    P = linspace(0, 1.2 * E^2/(2*X), 400);
    Q = P .* tan(phi);

    % V^4 + V^2 (2QX - E^2) + X^2 (P^2 + Q^2) = 0
    b = 2*Q*X - E^2;
    c = X^2 * (P.^2 + Q.^2);
    disc = b.^2 - 4*c;

    ok = disc >= 0;                          % beyond this, NO real solution
    V_upper = sqrt((-b(ok) + sqrt(disc(ok))) / 2);
    V_lower = sqrt((-b(ok) - sqrt(disc(ok))) / 2);

    label = sprintf('pf = %.2f %s', pfs(k), ternary(lag(k), 'lag', 'lead'));
    h = plot(P(ok), V_upper, 'LineWidth', 1.8, 'DisplayName', label);
    plot(P(ok), V_lower, ':', 'Color', h.Color, 'HandleVisibility', 'off')
end

% Validation: at unity pf the lossless nose must sit at E^2/2X, 0.707E
plot(E^2/(2*X), E/sqrt(2), 'ko', 'MarkerFaceColor', 'k', ...
     'DisplayName', 'Theoretical nose (pf = 1)')

xlabel('Real power delivered (W)')
ylabel('Receiving-end voltage (V)')
title('PV curves — solid: stable branch, dotted: unstable branch')
legend('Location', 'southwest')
end

function out = ternary(cond, a, b)
if cond, out = a; else, out = b; end
end

```python
"""PV curves for a two-bus system — the Python equivalent.

Solves the same quadratic in V^2 and marks where the discriminant goes
negative, which is where no real voltage solution exists.
"""

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt


def pv_curve(E, X, pf, lagging=True, n=400, p_max_factor=1.2):
    """Upper and lower voltage branches against real power.

    Returns (P, V_upper, V_lower), each masked to where a solution exists.
    """
    phi = np.arccos(pf) * (1 if lagging else -1)

    P = np.linspace(0, p_max_factor * E**2 / (2 * X), n)
    Q = P * np.tan(phi)

    b = 2 * Q * X - E**2
    c = X**2 * (P**2 + Q**2)
    disc = b**2 - 4 * c

    ok = disc >= 0                       # past this point: voltage collapse
    return (
        P[ok],
        np.sqrt((-b[ok] + np.sqrt(disc[ok])) / 2),
        np.sqrt((-b[ok] - np.sqrt(disc[ok])) / 2),
    )


if __name__ == '__main__':
    E, X = 230.0, 8.0                    # <-- your nameplate values

    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6, 4.5))

    for pf, lagging, label in [(1.0, True, 'pf = 1.00'),
                               (0.8, True, 'pf = 0.80 lag'),
                               (0.8, False, 'pf = 0.80 lead')]:
        P, V_up, V_lo = pv_curve(E, X, pf, lagging)
        line, = ax.plot(P, V_up, lw=1.8, label=label)
        ax.plot(P, V_lo, ls=':', lw=1.2, color=line.get_color())

    # Validate against the closed form before believing the plot
    p_nose, v_nose = E**2 / (2 * X), E / np.sqrt(2)
    ax.plot(p_nose, v_nose, 'ko', ms=5, label='Theoretical nose (pf = 1)')

    P1, V1, _ = pv_curve(E, X, 1.0)
    assert np.isclose(P1.max(), p_nose, rtol=0.01), 'nose does not match E^2/2X'
    assert np.isclose(V1[-1], v_nose, rtol=0.01), 'critical voltage is not E/sqrt(2)'

    ax.set_xlabel('Real power delivered (W)')
    ax.set_ylabel('Receiving-end voltage (V)')
    ax.set_title('PV curves — solid: stable, dotted: unstable')
    ax.legend(loc='lower left')
    ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
    fig.tight_layout()
    plt.show()
```

<Callout type="tip" title="The two asserts are the point of the Python version">
  Checking the numerical curve against the closed-form $E^2/2X$ and $E/\sqrt{2}$ before
  plotting is the habit worth carrying: **two independent routes to the same number**.

  If the assert fails, the code is wrong. If it passes and the plot still looks wrong, your
  intuition about the plot is what needs revising. Either outcome is useful; a plot with no
  check behind it is neither.
</Callout>

## Where this shows up in practice

**Distribution planning.** A feeder's voltage profile is calculated with exactly the
approximate drop formula, summed section by section. When a new industrial load with poor
power factor connects at the end of a long rural feeder, this calculation is what decides
whether a capacitor bank, a voltage regulator or a reconductoring job is needed.

**Reactive power pricing.** Utilities penalise low power factor, and the formula shows why:
the $X\sin\varphi$ term means a lagging customer causes voltage drop for every other
customer on the feeder, without consuming much energy. The penalty prices an externality.

**Voltage stability assessment.** System operators compute PV curves — usually called
nose curves — for critical corridors, and set transfer limits from a margin to the nose,
typically 5% on power. The lower branch you plotted as a dotted line is why the criterion
is a *margin* rather than a voltage threshold: by the time voltage looks bad, the margin
has already gone.

**Solar hosting capacity.** Distributed PV injects real power at the far end of a feeder,
which reverses the sign of $I$ and pushes the voltage *up* — the same arithmetic as your
capacitive case. The upper voltage limit is now the binding constraint on how much PV a
feeder can host, which inverts a century of distribution design.

<Callout type="engineering">
  That last one is worth pausing on, because it connects this bench directly to current
  research.

  For a hundred years distribution networks were designed around one assumption: power
  flows outward, and voltage falls with distance. Every regulator setting, tap changer and
  protection scheme encodes it.

  Rooftop solar breaks the assumption. At midday on a lightly loaded feeder, power flows
  *backwards* and the far end becomes the highest-voltage point. The physics is unchanged —
  it is still $\Delta V \approx I(R\cos\varphi + X\sin\varphi)$, with $I$ reversed — but
  every operational rule built on the old assumption now points the wrong way.

  And note which term dominates *here*: distribution feeders have low X/R, so the
  $R\cos\varphi$ term matters as much as the reactive one. Real power injection moves the
  voltage. That is why PV inverters are now required to provide reactive support.
</Callout>

## Viva preparation

<Viva band="definition">
  <Q q="Define voltage regulation.">
    The change in receiving-end voltage from no load to full load, expressed as a fraction
    of the full-load voltage:

    VR% = (|V_R,no-load| − |V_R,full-load|) / |V_R,full-load| × 100

    For a short line with no shunt path, the no-load receiving voltage equals the sending
    voltage, since no current flows and there is no drop.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Can voltage regulation be negative? What does that mean physically?">
    Yes — under a leading (capacitive) load. The approximate drop is I(R cos φ − X sin φ),
    and once X sin φ exceeds R cos φ the drop is negative, so the receiving end sits above
    the sending end.

    No energy is being generated at the far end. Leading current drives the line reactance
    in the opposite sense, so the voltage developed across it adds to the source rather
    than subtracting from it. It is the same mechanism as the Ferranti effect.
  </Q>

  <Q q="What is the nose point of a PV curve?">
    The maximum real power the system can deliver, where the two solutions of the voltage
    quadratic merge and the discriminant reaches zero.

    Beyond it the discriminant is negative and there is no real voltage solution at all —
    the load demands more power than the line can supply at any voltage. This is why a
    load-flow diverges near collapse rather than returning a poor answer: there is nothing
    to converge to.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="procedure">
  <Q q="Why must the sending-end voltage be held constant throughout?">
    Because the experiment measures how the receiving voltage responds to load. If you
    adjust the variac as you go, the change you record is your correction, not the line's
    behaviour.

    You still record V_S at every step — a supply that sags under load would otherwise
    appear as a line effect and corrupt every computed regulation figure.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why take smaller steps near the end of the PV sweep?">
    The curve is nearly flat at light load and turns sharply near the nose. Equal steps put
    most of your data where nothing is happening and almost none where the behaviour
    changes.

    This is also the operational point: voltage is a poor early indicator of proximity to
    collapse, precisely because most of the change occurs in the last part of the range.
  </Q>

  <Q q="You stopped at rated current without reaching the nose. Is the experiment a failure?">
    No — it is a result. On a short line the thermal limit binds before the voltage
    stability limit, and demonstrating that ordering is a legitimate finding.

    Report the maximum power reached, the reason you stopped, and the theoretical nose from
    E²/2X for comparison. What you must not do is extrapolate a nose from data that stops
    well short of it.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="analysis">
  <Q q="Your measured maximum power is below E²/2X. Why?">
    The closed form neglects series resistance. A teaching trainer has a far lower X/R ratio
    than a real transmission line, so R contributes both a dissipative loss and an in-phase
    voltage drop, and both reduce the deliverable power.

    Series resistance also lifts the critical voltage above 0.707E. Solving the full
    quadratic with R included, rather than the lossless form, is what closes the gap — and
    quantifying that gap is a better result than reproducing the ideal figure.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why does lagging power factor lower the whole PV curve?">
    Look at the discriminant of V⁴ + V²(2QX − E²) + X²(P² + Q²) = 0.

    Lagging Q reduces (E² − 2QX), shrinking the squared term, and simultaneously increases
    X²(P² + Q²), enlarging the subtracted term. It attacks the discriminant from both
    directions, so the region with a real solution shrinks and the nose arrives at lower
    power.

    Leading Q does the reverse, which is exactly why shunt compensation is the standard
    remedy for a voltage stability problem.
  </Q>

  <Q q="What is the significance of the lower branch of the PV curve?">
    It is a mathematically valid operating point delivering the same power at low voltage
    and high current. Real systems do not operate there, because it is unstable under the
    usual control actions: on the lower branch, increasing load admittance *decreases*
    delivered power, so a load-restoring control such as a tap changer drives the voltage
    down further rather than recovering it.

    That mechanism — tap changers acting to restore voltage and thereby accelerating
    collapse — is a documented contributor to real voltage collapse events.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="application">
  <Q q="Where would you use this analysis in a real utility?">
    Feeder voltage profile calculations in distribution planning; setting transfer limits on
    transmission corridors from PV curve margins, typically 5% on power; sizing capacitor
    banks and SVCs; and increasingly, determining solar hosting capacity, where the same
    equations run with reversed current and the upper voltage limit binds instead of the
    lower.
  </Q>

  <Q q="How does distributed solar change the picture?">
    It reverses the sign of the current, so the voltage rises with distance rather than
    falling — the far end of the feeder becomes its highest-voltage point at midday.

    The physics is unchanged; what changes is which limit binds. And on distribution
    feeders, where X/R is low, the R cos φ term is comparable to the reactive one, so real
    power injection moves the voltage significantly. That is the reason modern grid codes
    require inverters to provide reactive support.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Callout type="warning" title="The most common error is arithmetic, not wiring">
  Using the *nominal* sending-end voltage in the regulation calculation instead of the value
  you measured at that step.

  If the supply sagged by 2 V under load and you used the nominal figure, every regulation
  number in your table is wrong by an amount that varies with load — which looks exactly
  like a real effect and is very hard to spot afterwards. Use the measured $V_S$ from the
  same row, every time.
</Callout>

## Frequently asked

<Viva band="definition" title="FAQ">
  <Q q="Why is the drop formula an approximation? When does it break?">
    It keeps only the component of the drop in phase with V_R and discards the quadrature
    part. Since the quadrature term adds in quadrature to a much larger in-phase component,
    its contribution to the magnitude is second-order.

    It breaks down at large load angles and at very high loading — precisely the conditions
    of Part (b) near the nose. That is why Part (b) needs the full quadratic rather than the
    approximation.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why plot voltage on the vertical axis when power is the independent variable?">
    Convention, and it earns its keep: with V vertical the curve reads as a nose, and the
    two solutions for a given power appear one above the other, which is the whole point.
    Plotted the other way it is a sideways parabola and the two branches are much harder to
    interpret.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Is the critical voltage really always 0.707 pu?">
    Only for a lossless line at unity power factor. Adding resistance raises it; lagging
    power factor changes it; a constant-power load characteristic changes it again.

    Treat 0.707 as a reference result that shows the critical voltage is independent of X in
    the ideal case — a genuinely useful insight — rather than as a number to expect on a
    real system.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<KeyIdea>
  The receiving-end voltage responds to the **phase** of the load current, not its size:
  $\Delta V \approx I(R\cos\varphi \pm X\sin\varphi)$, with the sign set by lagging or
  leading — which is why a capacitive load raises the voltage.

  Push real power far enough and the voltage quadratic's discriminant goes negative: no
  solution exists at any voltage. On a short line the thermal limit arrives first, and
  demonstrating *that ordering* is the honest result of Part (b).
</KeyIdea>
