**Aim — to study the overcurrent protection scheme for three-phase induction motors.**

This experiment does something the earlier reading might not lead you to expect. It is
**not** about protecting the motor from overload. It is about protecting it from a **short
circuit at its own terminals**, and the relay used is instantaneous with a deliberately
high setting.

That distinction shapes everything below.

<Callout type="warning" title="The CT ratio is 2.5 : 5 — a step-UP ratio">
  Note which way round this goes. **2.5 A in the primary produces 5 A in the secondary**, so
  the secondary current is **twice** the primary.

  That is unusual for a power-system CT, where secondaries are normally 1 A or 5 A from a
  much larger primary. On a teaching bench it makes sense: the motor currents are small, and
  stepping them up gives the relay and ammeters something comfortable to work with.

  It also means the setting calculation in step 6 goes in the direction opposite to your
  instinct. Get this backwards and your setting is out by a factor of four.
</Callout>

## Theory

Overcurrent protection is applied to three-phase induction motors to protect against
**phase faults at the motor terminals** — terminal short circuits, terminal flashovers and
similar. Instantaneous overcurrent relays with a high current setting are normally used,
because they give fast, reliable and inexpensive protection.

The scheme is the one in Figure 1 of the sheet: line current transformers in each phase,
feeding a three-phase protective relay of device type **50** (instantaneous overcurrent) or
**51** (time-delayed).

<Callout type="engineering">
  **Why "high setting" and "instantaneous" go together.**

  A motor's normal starting current is five to seven times full-load current, and it lasts
  for seconds. Any relay that must not trip during a start therefore has two options: wait
  long enough that the start finishes first, or be set high enough that the start never
  reaches it.

  A time-delayed relay takes the first route, and is what you use for **overload**
  protection — where the current is only slightly above rated and you can afford to wait.

  For a **terminal fault** you cannot afford to wait. The fault current is very large, the
  damage is immediate, and the whole point of the protection is speed. So you take the
  second route: set the relay above anything that occurs normally — including starting — and
  let it operate with no intentional delay.

  Hence 450% of full-load current, and hence *instantaneous*. Those two choices are the same
  decision seen from two sides.
</Callout>

<Callout type="warning" title="This scheme does not protect against overload">
  Set at 450% of full-load current, the relay is blind to a sustained 150% overload — which
  will destroy a winding over minutes and never come close to the setting.

  Overload protection is a **separate function**: a thermal overload relay (ANSI 49), or the
  thermal replica built into a modern motor protection relay, which models winding
  temperature rather than watching instantaneous current.

  A real motor starter has both. This experiment studies one of them, and stating clearly
  which one — and what it deliberately does not cover — is a good line for your conclusion.
</Callout>

### The two constraints on the setting

$$
I_{\text{start}} \;<\; I_{\text{set}} \;<\; I_{\text{fault, min}}
$$

Above starting current, so a normal start does not trip it. Below the smallest fault it
must detect, so a genuine terminal fault does.

At 450% the setting sits just above a typical machine's starting current. **Step 8 of the
procedure asks you to verify that experimentally** — which is the honest way to treat it,
because whether it holds depends on your particular motor.

### The setting calculation

The relay is set in **secondary** amperes. With a 2.5 : 5 CT:

$$
I_{\text{set,secondary}} = 4.5 \times I_{\text{FLC,primary}} \times \frac{5}{2.5}
= 9 \times I_{\text{FLC,primary}}
$$

<Callout type="tip" title="Write both numbers side by side">
  Primary and secondary, for every setting, in your record book. It costs one extra column
  and it is the single defence against the most common error in this experiment.

  Sanity check: with the CT stepping *up* by two, the secondary setting should come out
  **larger** than the primary current it represents. If yours came out smaller, you divided
  where you should have multiplied.
</Callout>

### LL and LLG faults

The Universal Fault Module applies two fault types, and they differ in one respect that
matters for protection.

<Compare
  headings={['Fault', 'What is connected', 'Sequence components present']}
  rows={[
    { option: 'LL (line to line)', when: 'Two phases shorted together', cost: 'Positive and negative. NO zero sequence', recommended: false },
    { option: 'LLG (line-line to ground)', when: 'Two phases shorted together AND to earth', cost: 'Positive, negative AND zero sequence' },
  ]}
/>

<Callout type="engineering">
  For a fault at the same point, the **line-to-line** fault current is:

  $$I_{LL} = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,I_{3\varphi} \approx 0.866\,I_{3\varphi}$$

  so an LL fault is *less* severe than a three-phase fault at the same location — which is
  why the three-phase fault is normally the one that sizes switchgear.

  The **LLG** fault adds a zero-sequence path, so the total fault current is generally
  higher than LL, and the earth-return component is what an earth-fault element (50N/51N)
  would see. A phase overcurrent relay watching the three line currents sees both fault
  types; only the LLG has anything for an earth element.

  Your observation table asks for LL and LLG under two grid conditions precisely so you can
  see whether that difference changes the relay's response.
</Callout>

## The zone of protection

Look at Figure 2 of the sheet: **Fault Point 1 and Fault Point 2 are downstream of CT4, CT5
and CT6**, between the CTs and the motor terminals.

<Callout type="engineering">
  This is not incidental — it is the entire logic of the scheme.

  A relay can only see what its CTs measure. Everything **downstream** of the CTs is inside
  the protected zone; everything upstream is outside it and invisible.

  Placing the fault points between the CTs and the motor puts the simulated terminal fault
  **inside** the zone, which is what makes the relay respond at all. A fault on the source
  side of the CTs would draw current the relay never sees.

  Real substations extend this idea until every part of the network sits inside at least one
  zone, with zones overlapping at the CTs so nothing falls between them. This bench is that
  principle with a single zone.
</Callout>

## Safety and pre-lab

<Hazard title="This experiment applies deliberate short circuits to a live circuit">
  That is unusual and worth pausing on. In every other experiment a fault is something to
  avoid; here you are creating one on purpose.

  **The fault duration switch matters.** Set to 0.05–5 s as the sheet specifies. The module
  clears the fault automatically after that interval — it is the reason a deliberate short
  circuit is survivable. Confirm the setting before pressing INITIATE FAULT.

  **Release the fault after each test.** Steps 9 and 10 both end with "release the fault",
  and leaving it applied while you set up the next condition is how equipment gets damaged.

  **The rotating shaft and timing belt.** The motor is belt-coupled to the dynamometer. Tie
  back hair, no loose sleeves, and keep hands clear of the belt run — a belt nip is a common
  laboratory injury and it does not need much speed to be serious.

  **CT secondaries.** Short them before disconnecting anything from the relay.
</Hazard>

<Callout type="warning" title="Step 5 says set CT4, CT5, CT6 to 'O (open)' — this does NOT mean open-circuiting the secondary">
  The O position refers to the CT module's **shorting switch**. Setting it to open **removes
  the short across the secondary**, which puts the relay burden into circuit so current
  flows through it.

  That is the opposite of the hazard described on the
  [current transformer page](/lab/instruments/current-transformer). The dangerous condition
  is a secondary with **no path at all**; here the path is the relay.

  The wording invites exactly the wrong reading, so it is worth being explicit: open shorting
  switch, closed measuring circuit. If you are ever unsure which a switch does, ask before
  energising.
</Callout>

  Relay output (B4/B5) → CR1 coil in the control station → CR1 contacts in Transmission

  re-energising into a fault.

  Knowing this path is what lets you distinguish "the relay did not detect the fault" from
  "the relay detected it but the trip circuit did not complete" — two very different
  findings that look identical from the LED alone.

<Callout type="warning" title="What does CR2 actually change? Ask your demonstrator.">
  CR2 is a contactor inside Transmission Grid A, and opening or closing it alters the circuit
  between the source and the fault point — which changes the fault current the relay sees.

  I have not seen your module's internal schematic, so I am **not** going to assert what it
  switches. The honest approach, and the more useful one: record the fault currents in both
  states, compare them, and infer the effect from your own measurements. Then ask your
  demonstrator to confirm.

  If the fault current is **higher** with CR2 closed, CR2 is reducing the impedance between
  source and fault — a stronger source. If **lower**, it is inserting impedance. Your
  numbers will tell you which, and that inference is a better answer than a repeated claim.
</Callout>

## Expected outcomes

<Expect
  quantity="Motor current during starting and at 1 N·m load"
  trend="a large starting transient, settling to a much smaller steady value"
  magnitude="starting several times full-load current; steady current well below the 450% setting"
>
  At standstill the slip is 1, the rotor sees full supply frequency, and the machine is
  electrically a short-circuited transformer limited only by leakage impedance. Hence the
  large starting current.

  At 1 N·m the machine is lightly loaded, so the steady current should sit close to no-load
  current — magnetising current dominating — and comfortably below a setting placed at 450%
  of *full-load* current.

  A meter that averages over a long window will under-read the starting transient
  substantially. If your starting figure looks implausibly close to the steady one, suspect
  the instrument before the machine.
</Expect>

<Expect
  quantity="Whether the protection is stable through starting"
  trend="expected not to trip — but this is the question the sheet asks you to answer"
  magnitude="depends on whether your motor's starting current stays below 450% of FLC"
>
  A setting of 450% is placed just above a typical machine's starting current, so the
  expected answer is that the protection **is** stable.

  If it does trip, that is a legitimate and informative result, not a failure. It means this
  particular motor's starting current exceeded 450% of its full-load current, and the
  correct engineering response is to raise the setting — while checking it still sits below
  the minimum fault current. Report the measured starting current alongside the setting and
  the conclusion follows directly from the two numbers.

  Either outcome answers the sheet's question. What matters is that the answer comes from
  your measurement rather than from an expectation.
</Expect>

<Expect
  quantity="Fault current on initiating LL and LLG faults"
  trend="far above both the load current and the relay setting, with the tripping LED operating"
  magnitude="LL fault current approximately 0.866 of a three-phase fault at the same point; LLG generally higher than LL"
>
  The fault points sit inside the protected zone — downstream of the CTs — so the relay sees
  the full fault current and should operate essentially immediately.

  Watch which **phases** carry the current. An LL fault involves two phases; the third
  should show a much smaller current. That asymmetry is the clearest confirmation that the
  fault type is what you think it is, and it is worth recording all three phase currents
  rather than just the largest.

  The LLG fault adds a zero-sequence path. Whether that raises the phase currents noticeably
  on your bench depends on the earthing arrangement, which is worth asking about.
</Expect>

<Expect
  quantity="Effect of CR2 state on fault current"
  trend="the fault current differs between CR2 open and CR2 closed"
  magnitude="the direction and size of the difference is what your measurement establishes"
>
  Comparing the two states is the point of running each fault twice.

  The engineering question underneath it: **does the relay still detect the fault in the
  weaker configuration?** A setting comfortably below the fault current in a strong source
  may sit uncomfortably close to it when the source is weakened, and a protection scheme
  that works only under the most favourable system condition is not a protection scheme.

  This is exactly why real coordination studies are run at **minimum** fault level as well as
  maximum. If your two measurements bracket a meaningful range, that point is the strongest
  thing you can put in your conclusion.
</Expect>

## Calculations and conclusions

The sheet asks you to **comment on the performance of overcurrent protection in the
induction motor**. A conclusion that earns marks addresses these in order:

1. **Was the scheme stable?** State the measured starting current, the steady current at
   1 N·m, and the setting — then say whether the relay held, and why those three numbers
   made that outcome inevitable.
2. **Did it detect every fault?** LL and LLG, in both CR2 states. Four tests, four answers.
3. **How much margin was there?** Fault current divided by setting, for the *worst* case
   you measured. A scheme that trips is not the same as a scheme with adequate margin.
4. **What does it not protect against?** Set at 450% of full load, it is blind to sustained
   overload, to single-phasing on a lightly loaded machine, and to locked rotor if the
   stall current sits below the setting. Naming these is what shows you understand the scheme
   rather than just the reading.
5. **What would you change?** With numbers. If your margin at minimum fault level was thin,
   say what setting you would use instead and why it still clears starting.

<Callout type="warning" title="Do not write 'the protection worked satisfactorily' and stop">
  It is the sentence every marker has read a hundred times, and it says nothing.

  "The relay operated on all four fault tests. The smallest margin was 3.2× the setting, on
  the LL fault with CR2 open, which is the weakest source condition tested — adequate, but it
  is the case that would bind if the source were weaker still." That is a conclusion.
</Callout>

```matlab
function oc_setting_check()
% Instantaneous overcurrent setting check for a motor terminal fault.
%
% REPLACE these with YOUR nameplate and measured values.
% Everything here is a prediction until you substitute measurements.

I_flc      = 0.5;    % A, motor full-load current from the nameplate
ct_primary = 2.5;    % CT ratio, primary
ct_second  = 5.0;    % CT ratio, secondary  -> steps UP by 2

setting_pct = 450;   % as specified by the experiment sheet

% --- The setting, in both references ---
I_set_primary   = setting_pct/100 * I_flc;
I_set_secondary = I_set_primary * ct_second / ct_primary;

fprintf('Setting: %.2f A primary  =  %.2f A secondary\n', ...
        I_set_primary, I_set_secondary);

% Sanity check: a step-up CT must give a LARGER secondary figure
assert(I_set_secondary > I_set_primary, ...
    'A 2.5:5 CT steps up. Secondary must exceed primary — check the ratio.');

% --- Constraint 1: must sit ABOVE starting current ---
I_start_measured = 2.1;                       % A, MEASURED in step 8
start_multiple   = I_start_measured / I_flc;
fprintf('Starting current: %.2f A = %.0f%% of FLC\n', ...
        I_start_measured, start_multiple*100);

if I_start_measured > I_set_primary
    fprintf(['  !! Starting current EXCEEDS the setting — expect a trip on start.\n' ...
             '     Raise the setting, then re-check constraint 2.\n']);
else
    fprintf('  Stable through starting, margin %.0f%%\n', ...
            (I_set_primary/I_start_measured - 1)*100);
end

% --- Constraint 2: must sit BELOW the minimum fault current ---
I_fault_min = 4.0;                            % A, MEASURED — weakest case
fprintf('Minimum measured fault current: %.2f A = %.1fx setting\n', ...
        I_fault_min, I_fault_min/I_set_primary);

assert(I_fault_min > I_set_primary, ...
    'Setting is above the minimum fault current — the relay would NOT detect it.');
end
```

<CodeFile name="fault_currents.py">
```python
"""Relative severity of LL and LLG faults, from symmetrical components.

Gives the ratios to expect between fault types at the same point, which is
what your observation table lets you check.
"""

import numpy as np


def fault_currents(Z1, Z2=None, Z0=None, E=1.0):
    """Fault current magnitude for each fault type, in per unit.

    Args:
        Z1: Positive-sequence impedance to the fault (complex).
        Z2: Negative-sequence; defaults to Z1.
        Z0: Zero-sequence; defaults to 3*Z1, a rough typical value.
        E:  Prefault voltage, per unit.

    Returns:
        dict of fault type -> |fault current| in per unit.
    """
    Z2 = Z1 if Z2 is None else Z2
    Z0 = 3 * Z1 if Z0 is None else Z0

    three_phase = abs(E / Z1)
    line_line = abs(np.sqrt(3) * E / (Z1 + Z2))

    # Double line to ground: the faulted phase currents come from the
    # sequence network with Z2 and Z0 in parallel behind Z1.
    Z_par = Z2 * Z0 / (Z2 + Z0)
    I1 = E / (Z1 + Z_par)
    I2 = -I1 * Z0 / (Z2 + Z0)
    I0 = -I1 * Z2 / (Z2 + Z0)

    a = np.exp(2j * np.pi / 3)
    Ib = I0 + a**2 * I1 + a * I2
    Ic = I0 + a * I1 + a**2 * I2

    return {
        'three_phase': three_phase,
        'line_line': line_line,
        'line_line_ground': max(abs(Ib), abs(Ic)),
        'ground_return': abs(3 * I0),
    }


if __name__ == '__main__':
    Z1 = 0.1j                       # replace with your system impedance
    f = fault_currents(Z1)

    for name, value in f.items():
        print(f'{name:>18}: {value:6.2f} pu')

    # The standard result: an LL fault is about 86.6% of a three-phase fault
    assert np.isclose(f['line_line'] / f['three_phase'], np.sqrt(3) / 2, rtol=1e-6), \
        'LL/3ph ratio should be sqrt(3)/2 for Z1 == Z2'

    print(f"\nLL / 3-phase = {f['line_line']/f['three_phase']:.3f}  (expect 0.866)")
    print(f"LLG / LL     = {f['line_line_ground']/f['line_line']:.3f}")
```
</CodeFile>

<Callout type="tip" title="What the assert is checking">
  That the LL fault current comes out at √3/2 — about 86.6% — of the three-phase fault, which
  holds whenever the positive and negative sequence impedances are equal.

  It is a closed-form result you can verify by hand, so it turns the script from something
  you hope is right into something you have checked. If it fails, the sequence algebra is
  wrong and the LLG figure beside it cannot be trusted either.
</Callout>

## Wider context

The scheme in this experiment is one function out of several that a real motor starter
carries. Worth knowing what the others are, and why this one alone is not enough.

<Callout type="engineering">
  **Single-phasing is the gap most worth understanding**, because it is invisible to the
  scheme you just tested.

  Lose one phase and the motor keeps running on two. The remaining phase currents rise — but
  on a lightly loaded machine they may still sit far below a setting placed at 450% of full
  load, so nothing operates.

  Meanwhile the rotor is being destroyed. Losing a phase creates a large **negative-sequence**
  component, and an induction motor's negative-sequence impedance is roughly its
  **locked-rotor** impedance — about a sixth of positive sequence — so a modest
  negative-sequence voltage drives a large current.

  That current's field rotates *backwards* relative to the rotor, which therefore sees it at
  nearly **twice supply frequency**, where skin effect raises the rotor bar resistance
  considerably. The heating per ampere is roughly five to six times that of normal current.

  So the machine cooks while the ammeters look unremarkable and the relay stays quiet. This
  is why ANSI 46 exists as a separate function, and it is a strong point for your conclusion
  about what this scheme does not cover.
</Callout>

## Viva preparation

<Viva band="definition">
  <Q q="What is the aim of this experiment?">
    To study the overcurrent protection scheme for three-phase induction motors —
    specifically, protection against phase faults at the motor terminals, such as terminal
    short circuits and flashovers, using an instantaneous overcurrent relay with a high
    current setting.
  </Q>

  <Q q="What is the difference between ANSI device 50 and 51?">
    50 is instantaneous overcurrent — it operates with no intentional time delay. 51 is
    time-delayed, typically with an inverse-time characteristic where operating time falls as
    current rises.

    This experiment uses an instantaneous relay because a terminal fault must be cleared
    immediately, and the high setting is what keeps it clear of starting current.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why is the relay set at 450% of full-load current?">
    It must sit above anything that occurs normally — including the starting current, which
    is several times full-load current and lasts for seconds — and below the minimum fault
    current it has to detect.

    450% places it just above a typical machine's starting current. Because the relay is
    instantaneous, it cannot wait for the start to finish, so being set above the start is
    the only option available.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="procedure">
  <Q q="How did you calculate the relay set-point?">
    The relay is set in secondary amperes, so the primary figure has to be referred through
    the CT ratio.

    With a 2.5 : 5 CT the secondary current is twice the primary, so the secondary set-point
    is 4.5 × full-load current × (5/2.5) = 9 × full-load current. I recorded both the primary
    and secondary values.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Step 5 says set the CTs to the open position. Does that not create the open-secondary hazard?">
    No — the O position refers to the CT module's shorting switch. Opening it removes the
    short across the secondary and puts the relay burden into circuit.

    The dangerous condition is a secondary with no path at all, where nothing opposes the
    primary MMF, the core saturates and a very high voltage appears across the terminals.
    Here the path is the relay.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why does the trip latch, and what is the RESET button for?">
    So the motor stays disconnected until a person has established why it tripped, rather
    than re-energising into an unresolved fault.

    The relay output energises CR1, which seals in through its own contact; CR1's contacts in
    Transmission Grid A break the supply to the motor. RESET drops the latch out.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="analysis">
  <Q q="Why are the fault points placed downstream of the CTs?">
    Because a relay can only see what its CTs measure. Everything downstream of the CTs is
    inside the protected zone; everything upstream is invisible to it.

    Placing the fault points between the CTs and the motor terminals puts the simulated
    terminal fault inside the zone, which is the condition the scheme is designed to detect.
  </Q>

  <Q q="How does an LL fault differ from an LLG fault?">
    An LL fault shorts two phases together and contains positive and negative sequence
    components but no zero sequence. An LLG fault also connects to earth, adding a
    zero-sequence path.

    For a fault at the same point, LL current is about √3/2 — 86.6% — of the three-phase
    fault current. LLG is generally higher than LL, and only LLG produces the earth-return
    current an earth-fault element would see.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why repeat the fault tests with CR2 in both states?">
    Because CR2 changes the circuit between the source and the fault, and therefore the fault
    current the relay sees.

    A setting comfortably below the fault current in one configuration may sit close to it in
    a weaker one. Real coordination studies are checked at minimum fault level as well as
    maximum, because a scheme that only works under the most favourable system condition is
    not a protection scheme.
  </Q>

  <Q q="What does this scheme NOT protect the motor against?">
    Sustained overload, which at 150% of full load never approaches a 450% setting but will
    destroy a winding over minutes — that needs a thermal overload relay, ANSI 49.

    Single-phasing, where on a lightly loaded machine the line currents may stay well below
    the setting while negative-sequence current heats the rotor five to six times harder per
    ampere than normal current — that needs ANSI 46.

    And locked rotor, if the stall current happens to sit below the setting.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<KeyIdea>
  This scheme protects against a **terminal short circuit**, not against overload — which is
  why the relay is instantaneous and set as high as 450% of full-load current. Those two
  choices are the same decision: a relay that cannot wait for a start to finish must instead
  sit above it. And the fault points are downstream of the CTs because a relay only protects
  what its CTs can see.
</KeyIdea>
