Here is the problem in one line: **a motor starting normally and a motor stalled and burning
out draw the same current.**

Six times full load, both. The relay must ride through the first and trip on the second, and
the only thing that distinguishes them is how long the current lasts.

Everything in this experiment follows from that.

## Why this experiment exists

<Callout type="engineering">
  Motors consume roughly two-thirds of all industrial electricity, and a failed motor
  usually stops a process rather than just costing a motor.

  So the protection has two failure modes and both are expensive:

  **Tripping on a start** stops production for a fault that never existed. Do it repeatedly
  and an operator will ask for the setting to be raised — and the setting will be raised, and
  the motor will then be unprotected. Nuisance tripping does not merely annoy; it *causes*
  the eventual under-protection.

  **Failing to trip on a stall** destroys the winding in tens of seconds. A stalled motor
  dissipates six times its rated current in a rotor that has almost no thermal mass and, at
  zero speed, no fan cooling either.

  The setting sits between those, and the margin is narrower than most people expect.
</Callout>

## Theory

### The IDMT characteristic

The relay's operating time falls as current rises, following IEC 60255:

$$
t = \text{TMS} \times \frac{k}{\left(\dfrac{I}{I_s}\right)^{\alpha} - 1}
$$

with $I_s$ the current setting and TMS the time multiplier. For the standard inverse curve,
$k = 0.14$ and $\alpha = 0.02$.

<Callout type="warning" title="The −1 makes the setting an asymptote, not a trip point">
  At $I = I_s$ the denominator is zero and the operating time is infinite.

  So a relay set at 100% of full-load current does **not** operate at full load — it needs a
  meaningful margin above the setting before the time becomes finite and useful. Which is
  why settings sit at 110–125% of full-load current rather than at 100%.
</Callout>

### The two constraints

The setting must satisfy both of these simultaneously:

$$
t_{\text{relay}}(6 I_{\text{FLC}}) > t_{\text{start}}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
t_{\text{relay}}(6 I_{\text{FLC}}) < t_{\text{locked rotor}}
$$

Ride through the start; trip before the machine cooks.

<Callout type="engineering">
  **The window can close, and that is a real design outcome rather than a failure.**

  A motor driving a high-inertia load — a large fan, a centrifuge, a flywheel — takes longer
  to accelerate. If the starting time grows until it approaches the locked-rotor withstand
  time, there is no TMS that satisfies both constraints, because the relay cannot tell the
  two conditions apart by current alone.

  The solutions all involve adding **information the relay does not currently have**:

  - A **speed switch** on the shaft. Rotating means starting; not rotating means stalled.
    This is the direct answer, and the cleanest.
  - A **thermal replica** relay, which integrates heating rather than watching instantaneous
    current, and so knows how much thermal capacity remains.
  - Reduced-voltage starting, which lengthens the start but lowers the current, moving the
    problem rather than solving it.

  Recognising *when* you have run out of window is part of the engineering. It is not a case
  of choosing a cleverer TMS.
</Callout>

### The instantaneous element

A separate **ANSI 50** element trips with no intentional delay, for genuine short circuits.
It must be set above anything that occurs normally — which means above the starting current,
with a further allowance.

<Callout type="engineering">
  **The allowance is for DC offset, and it is the subtlety in this experiment.**

  When a circuit with inductance is energised, the current cannot start instantaneously at
  its steady-state value. Depending on the point on the voltage wave at which the contacts
  close, a decaying **DC component** is superimposed on the AC.

  In the worst case — closing at a voltage zero — the first peak approaches **twice** the
  symmetrical peak. So a motor whose symmetrical starting current is 6× FLC can produce a
  first-cycle asymmetric peak approaching 12× FLC.

  An instantaneous element set at 8× would trip on that first peak, on a perfectly normal
  start, and would do so *intermittently* — because whether it happens depends on where in
  the cycle the contactor happened to close. An intermittent nuisance trip that cannot be
  reproduced is among the most frustrating faults in a plant.

  Hence the rule of thumb: set the instantaneous element at **1.5 to 2 times the starting
  current**, or use a relay with a short definite-time delay of one or two cycles that lets
  the DC component decay before it decides.
</Callout>

<Analogy>
  Think of pushing a heavy door that sticks.

  The steady force needed to keep it moving is modest. The first shove is much larger, and
  how much larger depends on exactly how you started — a sharp push gives a bigger spike
  than a gradual lean.

  DC offset is that first shove. It has nothing to do with anything being wrong; it is what
  energising an inductive circuit looks like, and the size depends on the instant you did it.
</Analogy>

## Safety and pre-lab

<Hazard title="A rotating shaft and a six-times overload">
  **The shaft.** Tie back hair, no loose sleeves, no dangling leads near the coupling.
  Confirm the guard is in place and everyone is clear before you start the machine.

  **The motor moves.** An induction motor produces a substantial torque reaction on
  starting. Confirm it is mechanically secured before energising.

  **Repeated starts.** Each start dumps a large amount of heat into a rotor with very little
  thermal mass. Two starts from cold and one from hot is the usual limit. This experiment
  will tempt you to start it repeatedly — space them out, and if the frame becomes hot to
  the touch, stop and let it cool.

  **CT secondaries.** The relay is fed from CTs. Short the secondary before disconnecting
  the relay from it.
</Hazard>

  Write both numbers side by side in your record — primary and secondary — for every
  setting.

## Procedure

<Steps title="Part 1 — Characterise the motor">
  <Step n={1} title="Record the nameplate and compute the derived values" expect="FLC, synchronous speed, and full-load slip written down before anything is energised">
    Every setting in this experiment is a multiple of the full-load current. Get it from the
    plate, not from a measurement.
  </Step>

  <Step n={2} title="Run the motor unloaded and record the no-load current" expect="roughly 30–50% of FLC — magnetising current dominates at no load">
    A useful sanity check on your metering before you rely on it.
  </Step>

  <Step n={3} title="Start the motor and capture the starting current" hazard="Stand clear of the shaft. Confirm everyone else is clear before you close the contactor." expect="5–7 × FLC, holding until the machine is nearly at speed, then falling sharply">
    A clamp meter with a peak-hold or inrush function is the practical instrument here. A
    recording analyser is better if you have one.

    Record both the **magnitude** and the **duration** — the duration is what the relay
    setting has to accommodate.
  </Step>
</Steps>

<Steps title="Part 2 — Set and test the relay">
  <Step n={4} title="Calculate the current setting" expect="110–125% of full-load current, expressed in secondary amperes">
    Above maximum load so it does not operate on normal running; below the minimum fault
    current so it can see a genuine fault.
  </Step>

  <Step n={5} title="Choose a TMS that satisfies both constraints" expect="an operating time at 6× FLC that exceeds your measured starting time but falls short of the locked-rotor withstand">
    Compute it from the IEC equation before you dial it in. Guessing and adjusting wastes
    the session.
  </Step>

  <Step n={6} title="Verify the pickup with the test set" expect="the relay just starting to operate at your setting, within its stated tolerance">
    Inject slowly increasing current and note where it picks up. This checks the setting is
    what you think it is.
  </Step>

  <Step n={7} title="Measure operating time at several multiples" expect="times following the IDMT curve — long at 2×, short at 10×">
    Inject at 2, 3, 5, 7 and 10 times the setting and record the operating time at each.
    Take each point at least twice.

    Note explicitly whether you are timing the **relay contact** or the **breaker**. They
    differ by 60–100 ms.
  </Step>

  <Step n={8} title="Repeat at a different TMS" expect="the same curve shape scaled by the TMS ratio">
    Two TMS values is what demonstrates that TMS is a clean multiplier rather than a shape
    change.
  </Step>

  <Step n={9} title="Test that the relay rides through a real start" expect="the motor starting normally with no trip">
    The point of the whole exercise. If it trips, your TMS is too low — and finding that
    here rather than in a plant is exactly why the experiment exists.
  </Step>

  <Step n={10} title="Shut down safely">
    Stop the motor, isolate, short the CT secondaries before disconnecting anything.
  </Step>
</Steps>

## Expected outcomes

<Expect
  quantity="Starting current"
  trend="5–7 times full-load current, sustained until the motor is near full speed"
  magnitude="duration from about 1 s unloaded to 10 s or more on a high-inertia load"
>
  At standstill the slip is 1, the rotor sees full supply frequency, and the machine is
  electrically a short-circuited transformer limited only by leakage impedance.

  The current does **not** taper smoothly — it stays high until roughly 80–90% of
  synchronous speed and then falls sharply. If your measurement shows a gradual decay from
  the first instant, check whether your instrument is averaging over a window long enough to
  smear the transient.

  A clamp meter without an inrush function will under-read this substantially, because its
  update rate is slower than the event.
</Expect>

<Expect
  quantity="Relay operating time versus current"
  trend="falls steeply with current, close to a straight line on log-log axes"
  magnitude="should match t = TMS × 0.14 / ((I/Is)^0.02 − 1) within the relay's stated tolerance"
>
  Plot your measured points against the IEC equation on **log-log axes**. An inverse curve
  is nearly a straight line there, so any systematic deviation is immediately visible in a
  way it is not on linear axes.

  Expect closer agreement at high multiples than at low ones. Near the setting the
  denominator is small and the operating time is very sensitive to the exact current — so a
  1% error in injected current becomes a large error in time. That sensitivity is a property
  of the curve, not of your technique, and saying so is a better discussion point than
  apologising for scatter.

  On an **electromechanical** relay expect more scatter than on a numerical one: friction,
  the disc's starting position and mechanical overshoot all contribute.
</Expect>

<Expect
  quantity="Effect of changing TMS"
  trend="the whole curve scales by the TMS ratio, with its shape unchanged"
  magnitude="doubling TMS should double every operating time"
>
  This is worth verifying explicitly because it justifies the coordination method. If TMS
  changed the curve *shape*, grading two relays would require re-deriving everything at
  every current; because it is a clean multiplier, you can shift one curve relative to
  another and the margin holds across the range.

  On an induction disc relay the reason is mechanical: TMS changes how far the disc must
  travel before the contacts meet, not how fast it travels.
</Expect>

<Expect
  quantity="The motor start, with the relay in service"
  trend="no trip"
  magnitude="the relay should see 6× FLC for the starting duration and not operate"
>
  The result the experiment is actually for.

  If it trips, compare your measured starting duration against the relay's operating time at
  the measured starting current. The gap tells you exactly how much TMS you needed — which
  is a far better outcome than a setting that happened to work first time.

  Report a trip honestly if it happens. It is a legitimate result, and the calculation that
  explains it is the substance of the discussion.
</Expect>

## Calculations and analysis

1. Express every measured current as a multiple of full-load current.
2. Compute the theoretical operating time at each injected current from the IEC equation.
3. Plot measured against theoretical on **log-log axes**, both TMS values on one figure.
4. Mark on the same axes: the starting current and duration as a single point, and the
   locked-rotor withstand time.
5. State whether your setting satisfies both constraints, with numbers.

<Callout type="tip" title="The plot that makes the whole experiment legible">
  One figure, log-log, containing:

  - the relay curve at your chosen TMS,
  - a marker at (starting current, starting duration) — which must sit **below** the curve,
  - a marker at (starting current, locked-rotor withstand) — which must sit **above** it,
  - your measured points.

  If the start marker is below the curve and the withstand marker is above it, your setting
  works, and the figure says so at a glance. This is exactly the figure a protection engineer
  produces, and it is worth building once properly.
</Callout>

<CodeFile name="idmt_setting.m">
```matlab
function idmt_setting()
% Motor overcurrent setting check.
%
% REPLACE the motor and relay values with YOUR measurements before use.
% Everything here is a prediction, not a measurement.

I_flc      = 7.5;    % A, from the nameplate
I_start    = 6.0;    % multiples of FLC, MEASURED
t_start    = 4.0;    % s, MEASURED
t_stall    = 15.0;   % s, locked-rotor withstand (cold)

Is  = 1.25 * I_flc;  % current setting: 125% of full load
TMS = 0.15;

k = 0.14; alpha = 0.02;          % IEC standard inverse
t = @(I) TMS * k ./ ((I ./ Is).^alpha - 1);

I_sweep = linspace(1.05*Is, 20*I_flc, 500);

figure; loglog(I_sweep/I_flc, t(I_sweep), 'LineWidth', 1.8); hold on; grid on

% The two constraints, as points
plot(I_start, t_start,  'go', 'MarkerFaceColor', 'g', 'DisplayName', 'Start (must be BELOW)')
plot(I_start, t_stall,  'rx', 'MarkerSize', 10, 'LineWidth', 2, ...
     'DisplayName', 'Locked rotor withstand (must be ABOVE)')

xlabel('Current (multiples of full load)')
ylabel('Time (s)')
title(sprintf('IDMT setting check — Is = %.1f A, TMS = %.2f', Is, TMS))
legend('Relay curve', 'Start', 'Withstand', 'Location', 'southwest')

% Verify both constraints explicitly rather than trusting the picture
t_at_start = t(I_start * I_flc);
fprintf('Relay operates in %.2f s at starting current\n', t_at_start);

assert(t_at_start > t_start, ...
    'FAILS: relay would trip during starting (%.2f s < %.2f s)', t_at_start, t_start);
assert(t_at_start < t_stall, ...
    'FAILS: relay too slow for a stall (%.2f s > %.2f s)', t_at_start, t_stall);
fprintf('Both constraints satisfied.\n');
end
```
</CodeFile>

<CodeFile name="motor_thermal.py">
```python
"""First-order thermal replica of a motor, and what a stall does to it.

Shows why the protection window exists: a start and a stall look identical
in current, and completely different in accumulated heat.
"""

import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt


def thermal_state(current_pu, dt, tau=600.0, theta0=0.0):
    """Integrate dθ/dt = I² − θ/τ over a current profile.

    Args:
        current_pu: Current in multiples of full-load, sampled at dt.
        dt: Time step, seconds.
        tau: Thermal time constant, seconds.
        theta0: Initial thermal state, per unit (0 = cold, 1 = at limit).

    Returns:
        Thermal state at each sample, in per unit of the trip threshold.
    """
    theta = np.empty(len(current_pu))
    th = theta0
    for i, i_pu in enumerate(current_pu):
        th += (i_pu**2 - th / tau) * dt
        theta[i] = th
    return theta


if __name__ == '__main__':
    dt = 0.01
    flc, start, t_start = 1.0, 6.0, 4.0

    # A normal start, then running at full load
    normal = np.r_[np.full(int(t_start / dt), start),
                   np.full(int(60 / dt), flc)]

    # The same start that never ends — a stall
    stalled = np.full(int(64 / dt), start)

    t = np.arange(len(normal)) * dt

    fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.5, 4))
    ax.plot(t, thermal_state(normal, dt), lw=1.8, label='Normal start, then run')
    ax.plot(t, thermal_state(stalled, dt), lw=1.8, label='Stalled at 6× FLC')
    ax.axhline(1.0, ls='--', c='r', lw=1.2, label='Trip threshold')

    ax.set_xlabel('Time (s)')
    ax.set_ylabel('Thermal state (pu)')
    ax.set_title('Why a thermal replica separates a start from a stall')
    ax.legend()
    ax.grid(alpha=0.3)
    fig.tight_layout()
    plt.show()

    # The two are identical for the first t_start seconds, by construction
    n = int(t_start / dt)
    assert np.allclose(thermal_state(normal, dt)[:n],
                       thermal_state(stalled, dt)[:n]), \
        'start and stall must be indistinguishable during the start'
```
</CodeFile>

<Callout type="tip" title="What the assert in the Python version is for">
  It states the premise of the whole experiment as a testable claim: for the first few
  seconds, a start and a stall are **identical** — same current, same accumulated heat, no
  way to tell them apart.

  The curves only diverge afterwards, which is exactly why the protection must be built on
  time rather than magnitude. Encoding that as an assertion rather than a sentence is worth
  doing, because it fails loudly if the model is ever changed in a way that breaks the
  premise.
</Callout>

## Where this shows up in practice

**Every industrial motor starter** has this setting in it. A refinery has thousands of
motors, each with a relay whose curve was chosen against that motor's starting time and
withstand curve.

**Motor protection relays** now integrate the thermal model directly, along with negative
sequence (ANSI 46) for single-phasing, undercurrent (37) for loss of load — a pump running
dry — and start-inhibit logic that refuses another start until the modelled temperature has
fallen.

**Arc flash studies** depend on these settings. The incident energy at a switchboard is
proportional to the clearing time, so a relay set slowly for coordination reasons directly
increases the PPE category someone must wear to work on that board. Protection settings are
a personnel safety issue, not only an equipment one.

<Callout type="engineering">
  That last point is worth carrying with you, because it is a connection students rarely
  make.

  Coordination pushes upstream settings **slower** — each grading margin adds delay. Arc
  flash energy is roughly proportional to clearing time. So better coordination produces
  higher incident energy at the upstream board, and the person working there needs heavier
  arc-rated clothing as a direct consequence of a setting chosen for selectivity.

  Modern practice manages this with a **maintenance mode** switch: temporarily reducing the
  instantaneous setting while someone is working on the board, accepting worse selectivity
  in exchange for faster clearing. Selectivity and safety genuinely trade against each
  other, and this is where.
</Callout>

  <Solution>
    ```matlab
    function coordination_check()
    % Two relays in series: motor feeder and incomer.
    k = 0.14; alpha = 0.02;
    t = @(I, Is, TMS) TMS * k ./ ((I ./ Is).^alpha - 1);

    Is_down = 9.4;   TMS_down = 0.15;   % motor relay
    Is_up   = 25.0;  TMS_up   = 0.30;   % incomer

    I = logspace(log10(30), log10(600), 400);

    figure; loglog(I, t(I, Is_down, TMS_down), 'LineWidth', 1.8); hold on; grid on
    loglog(I, t(I, Is_up, TMS_up), 'LineWidth', 1.8)

    % Grading margin across the whole range, not at one point
    margin = t(I, Is_up, TMS_up) - t(I, Is_down, TMS_down);
    fprintf('Minimum grading margin: %.3f s at %.0f A\n', min(margin), I(margin == min(margin)));

    assert(min(margin) > 0.25, 'Grading margin falls below 0.25 s');
    legend('Motor relay', 'Incomer', 'Location', 'southwest')
    end
    ```

    The essential move is checking the margin **across the whole current range** rather than
    at one convenient point. The two curves converge as current rises, so a pair that grades
    comfortably at 3× may lose selectivity entirely at 20×, which is precisely where a real
    fault sits.

    That is why `min(margin)` is the quantity to assert on. Verifying coordination at a
    single current is the standard way people convince themselves of something untrue.
  </Solution>
</Exercise>

<Exercise n={2} kind="practice" title="Observe single-phasing safely">
  If your bench and demonstrator permit it, remove one phase from a lightly loaded motor and
  measure what happens.

  <Solution>
    Only with explicit permission and supervision — and briefly, because the point is that it
    damages the machine.

    What to measure:

    ```python
    import numpy as np

    def sequence_components(Ia, Ib, Ic):
        """Symmetrical components from three phasor currents."""
        a = np.exp(2j * np.pi / 3)
        A = np.array([[1, 1,      1],
                      [1, a,      a**2],
                      [1, a**2,   a]]) / 3
        I0, I1, I2 = A @ np.array([Ia, Ib, Ic])
        return {'zero': I0, 'positive': I1, 'negative': I2,
                'unbalance_pct': abs(I2) / abs(I1) * 100}
    ```

    What you should find: the two remaining phase currents rise substantially — often by
    around √3 — and the negative-sequence component becomes large.

    The important observation is the comparison against the relay setting. On a lightly
    loaded motor the increased line current may still sit **within** the overcurrent
    setting, so the relay does not operate — while the rotor is being heated five to six
    times harder per ampere than by normal current, because the negative-sequence field
    rotates backwards and the rotor sees it at nearly twice supply frequency.

    Measuring the negative-sequence percentage and showing it exceeds a typical 46-element
    setting while the overcurrent element stays quiet is the cleanest possible demonstration
    of why that separate function exists.
  </Solution>

<Viva band="definition">
  <Q q="What does IDMT stand for, and what do Is and TMS do?">
    Inverse Definite Minimum Time. Operating time falls as current rises, approaching a
    definite minimum at very high currents.

    Is is the current setting — the threshold the relay compares against, set above maximum
    load and below minimum fault current. TMS is the time multiplier, which scales the whole
    curve without changing its shape. On an induction disc relay it changes how far the disc
    must travel before the contacts meet.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why is the relay set at 125% of full-load current rather than 100%?">
    Because of the −1 in the denominator of the IDMT equation: at exactly Is the operating
    time is infinite, so the setting is an asymptote rather than a trip point.

    A relay set at 100% would take an unusably long time to operate on a small overload. A
    margin above full load is needed before the operating time becomes finite and useful.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="procedure">
  <Q q="How did you choose your TMS?">
    From two constraints. The operating time at starting current must exceed the measured
    starting duration, so the relay rides through a normal start; and it must be less than
    the locked-rotor withstand time, so a stall is cleared before the winding is damaged.

    I computed the operating time from the IEC equation at my measured starting current and
    checked both inequalities before dialling anything in.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why plot the results on log-log axes?">
    Because an inverse-time curve is close to a straight line on log-log axes, so a
    systematic deviation from the characteristic is immediately visible. On linear axes the
    curve is so steep at low multiples that everything else is compressed into the corner.

    It is also the standard presentation for coordination studies, where several curves are
    compared at once.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="analysis">
  <Q q="A starting motor and a stalled motor draw the same current. How does the relay distinguish them?">
    By duration alone. The magnitudes are identical, so the relay rides through six times
    full load for longer than the starting time and trips before the locked-rotor withstand
    time expires.

    If the starting time approaches the withstand time — on a high-inertia load — no TMS
    satisfies both, and the relay needs information it does not have from current alone: a
    speed switch on the shaft, or a thermal replica that integrates heating.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Why must the instantaneous element be set well above the starting current?">
    Because of DC offset. Energising an inductive circuit produces a decaying DC component
    whose size depends on the point on the voltage wave at which the contacts close, and in
    the worst case the first peak approaches twice the symmetrical peak.

    So a symmetrical starting current of 6× FLC can produce a first-cycle peak near 12×. An
    element set at 8× would trip intermittently on normal starts, depending on switching
    instant — which is a particularly difficult fault to diagnose. Set it at 1.5 to 2 times
    the starting current, or use a short definite-time delay of a cycle or two.
  </Q>

  <Q q="Your measured operating times scatter more at low multiples than at high ones. Why?">
    Because near the setting the denominator (I/Is)^α − 1 is small, so the operating time is
    very sensitive to the exact current — a 1% error in injected current produces a large
    change in time.

    That sensitivity is a property of the characteristic, not of the measurement technique.
    On an electromechanical relay, friction and disc overshoot add further scatter.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<Viva band="application">
  <Q q="How do protection settings affect personnel safety?">
    Arc flash incident energy is roughly proportional to fault clearing time. Coordination
    requires upstream relays to be slower, so better selectivity directly increases the
    incident energy at the upstream board — and therefore the arc-rated PPE someone must
    wear to work on it.

    Modern practice uses a maintenance mode switch that temporarily lowers the instantaneous
    setting while someone works on the board, trading selectivity for faster clearing.
  </Q>

  <Q q="What protection functions does a modern motor relay provide beyond overcurrent?">
    Thermal replica (49), which integrates heating rather than watching instantaneous
    current; negative sequence (46) for single-phasing and unbalance; undercurrent (37) for
    loss of load, such as a pump running dry; instantaneous overcurrent (50) for short
    circuits; earth fault (50N/51N); and start-inhibit logic that refuses another start until
    the modelled temperature has fallen.
  </Q>
</Viva>

<KeyIdea>
  A starting motor and a stalled motor draw the **same current**, so the setting is chosen
  entirely by time: slower than the start, faster than the locked-rotor withstand. When
  those two close on each other, no TMS works and the relay needs information current cannot
  give it. And the instantaneous element sits at 1.5–2× starting current because DC offset
  can nearly double the first-cycle peak.
